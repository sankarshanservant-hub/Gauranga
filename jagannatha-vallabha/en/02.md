# Act Two. Bhava-pariksha (The Testing of Feeling)

*Madanika learns from Ashoka-manjari of Radha's longing; Shashimukhi hands Krishna Radha's love-letter; wishing to test the firmness of her love, Krishna feigns to reject her.*

*(Enter Madanika.)*

**Madanika** *(looking ahead)*. Why, it is Ashoka-manjari!

**Ashoka-manjari** *(Prakrit)*. Lady, I bow to you. Where are you going so lost in thought, as if you had taken a heavy task upon yourself?

**Madanika.** Child, this is a weighty matter indeed.

**Ashoka-manjari** *(Prakrit)*. How so?

**Madanika.** Child, don't you know? We went with our dear friend Radha to gather flowers.

**Ashoka-manjari** *(Prakrit)*. And what happened there?

**Madanika.** There, at the foot of an ashoka tree, Mukunda became the guest of her eyes.[^1]

**Ashoka-manjari** *(Prakrit)*. Surely the Flower-weaponed One has been up to some trick![^2]

**Madanika.** Of course.

**Ashoka-manjari** *(Prakrit)*. Then what have you decided to do about it?

**Madanika.** Simple girl, must you ask me even that?

**Ashoka-manjari** *(Prakrit)*. Mukunda must be sought out?

**Madanika.** Of course.

**Ashoka-manjari** *(Prakrit)*. But how did you come to know the heart of one trembling with shyness?

**Madanika.** Child,

> **15.** the armour of shyness stays firm in young girls' hearts only until the arrows of the Odd-arrowed One fall upon it.[^3]

**Ashoka-manjari** *(Prakrit)*. Even so — did she reveal it herself, or did you infer it?

**Madanika.** I inferred it myself.

**Ashoka-manjari** *(Prakrit)*. How?

**Madanika.**

> **19.** She looks at the moon with no pleasure; at the calls of the maddened cuckoos she stops her ears on some pretext; in her friends' talk her answers make no sense — from this I understood something of Smara's play in her.[^4]

*Song 5 — in raga Shri-gandhara.*

> **20.** Alas, alas! At the sandal breeze and the cuckoos' song her body shows the change the Bodiless One works; to hide it, how many, many childish games does she not suddenly begin!
> Seized by love's affliction, bearing burdens of ever new feelings — O Shiva, Shiva! — Radha languishes. *(Refrain.)*
> Again and again hiding the drops that fall from her motionless eyes, she suddenly goes aside to a friend in private and shows her the very essence of friendship.
> May this, spoken by the poet Ramananda Raya, charming the heart of Gajapati Rudra, live day by day in the assembly of connoisseurs devoted to the feet of Hari.[^5]

And where are you going?

**Ashoka-manjari** *(Prakrit)*. She told me too: "Friend, I long for a bed of fresh lotus petals — bring me such petals." So I am going for them.

**Madanika** *(aside)*. Ah, how cruelly the Flower-bowed One plays! I have heard:

> **23.** Radha, in whom love swelled ever more at the southern breeze, the cuckoo's call and the humming of bees, reflected long, and then, fair-faced, in private said this in faltering words to her friend Shashimukhi:

*Song 6 — in raga Todi-varadi.*

> **24.** "A bed of opened lotus petals, hidden from the eyes of all my friends — to it my mind is drawn; prepare it quickly, fulfil this desire of mine, O moon-faced one!
> A bracelet of fresh lotus-stalk shoots, a dwelling sprinkled with sandal juice!" *(Refrain.)*
> May this, spoken by the poet Ramananda Raya, delight the heart of Rudra, lord of elephants.[^6]

**Madanika.** Go; may your paths be auspicious. And I shall follow Mukunda.

**Ashoka-manjari** *(Prakrit)*. Then I bow to you. *(Exit.)*

**Madanika** *(walking about, fixing her gaze on the sky)*. Ho, parrots, do you know where this Mukunda is to be seen? What do you say? "At the foot of the bhandira tree, with Shashimukhi"? Very well — I myself sent Shashimukhi there. What do you say? "And where are you going?" There, to hide and hear what happens. There I go. *(Exit.)*[^7]

*End of the interlude (vishkambhaka).*[^8]

*(Enter Krishna with Shashimukhi.)*

**Krishna.** This way, this way.

*(Shashimukhi hands Him a love-letter.)*

**Krishna** *(reads)*:

> **29.** *(Prakrit)* "You pierce the heart deep — and Madana gets the heavy ill fame! You are seen in every direction, but Madana is seen nowhere."[^9]

**Krishna** *(aside)*. Ah, her love has gone beyond all bounds. Let Me test the firmness of her heart by feigning indifference.[^10] *(Aloud, concealing His feeling.)* Friend,

> **30.** who is this called Madana? Why has he come here? What wrong has she done that he pitilessly torments the fair-eyed one? Is he one of Kamsa's men? *(Proudly.)* Then tell Me where he is! Relying on My two arms alone I shall crush him and set this girl at ease. While I am here, should the women of Vraja know fear?[^11]

*(Enter the jester, tossing the curtain aside.)*

**Jester** *(Prakrit)*. Hey friend, he's no man of Kamsa — I'm the one called Madana! What will you do to me, a brahmin?[^12]

**Krishna.** Fie, fool, enough of jokes!

**Jester** *(Prakrit)*. Lady, put a pair of laddus in our dear friend's hand — then our dear friend will go there and drive Madana away.

**Madanika** *(listening, aside)*. Ah, this is a messenger entrusted with the whole affair![^13] For she, clever one, told Madhava in Vrindavana all sorts of things under the pretext of describing Radha's beauty. *(Looking closely, laughing.)*

> **33.** Drinking her words like streams of honey from an opening lotus, as if intoxicated, His crown swaying, Hari — though love was rising in Him, bent on hiding what was in His heart — slowly, slowly, with a lovely smile, said: "How is this?"

Well, love that has passed all bounds brings sweetness.

**Krishna** *(reading the letter again)*. Friend, I have not quite understood this.

> **34.** Surrounded by the cowherd boys, on the bank of the Yamuna in Vrindavana, I enjoy some game or other; why then does this fawn-eyed girl see Me alone, plainly, in every direction?

*Song 7 — in raga Sama-gurjari.*

> **35.** Ask this company of cowherd boys, friend, when I ever followed her! How does she see Me in every direction, how does she lose her senses?
> Friend, give up this play of words: if the cowherd boys learn of it, they will make great fun of Me. *(Refrain.)*
> And if she, the very mountain-stronghold of her family, must abandon the custom of her family — why then should the girl bear this fainting love for Me?
> May these words of Madhusudana, spoken by the poet Ramananda Raya for the joy of Gajapati Rudra, bring delight to all connoisseurs.[^14]

**Shashimukhi** *(aside, Prakrit)*. Ah, my dear friend's love is misplaced! What is to be done here?

**Jester** *(Prakrit)*. Hey, what's the use of talking with this wicked cowherdess's daughter? Friend, look, look:

> **37.** *(Prakrit)* "The swan-maiden, driven by the sun's rays, seeks the shade of a cluster of lotuses — and you see how it, swaying in the wind, drives her away!"[^15]

**Krishna** *(aside)*. Ah, how cleverly the rogue turned his words! *(Aloud.)* Fie, fool, why do you talk beside the point?

**Jester** *(Prakrit)*. Hey friend, I spoke right to the point.

**Madanika** *(aside)*. Ah, Radhika, you are fortunate in every way![^16]

**Shashimukhi** *(aloud, Prakrit)*. Noble one, to deceive one who is devoted to you is unworthy of such as you.

**Krishna.** Good woman, consider this too:

> **42.** her husband is dear to her; this girl is a guardian of her family's honour; why should the innocent one suddenly, without cause, overturn good conduct?[^17]

**Jester** *(Prakrit)*. Lady, our dear friend takes refuge in dharma, so off with you! *(Laying his hand on Krishna's heart.)* Lady, don't answer — she herself is scratching about inside my dear friend's heart. So I'll have to make everything plain. *(In His ear.)* Hey friend, you too have seen her a thousand times in dreams. Why then hide yourself now that she is before you?

**Krishna.** Fie, fool, how do you know My dream?

**Jester** *(Prakrit)*. What, do you leave me behind in dreams? No — there too I saw it all![^18]

**Krishna** *(aside)*. Though this chattering little brahmin spoke from his love of joking, he has spoken the truth. So be it; still, the nature of young girls must be tested. *(Aloud.)* Good woman, turn this girl back from unseemly rashness. *(To the jester.)* Friend, come, let us also go and bring in the calves. Good woman, you too gently persuade her to desist.

*Song 8 — in raga Mallara.*

> **46.** The lotus does not love the moon, the night does not seek the sun. Such is the conduct of women of good family: to go to another man is grave sin.
> Shashimukhi, restrain the lotus-faced one, in whom love has blossomed for an unfitting object. *(Refrain.)*
> If she does not regard the honour of her family — how should we not count it strange?[^19]
> May these words of Ramananda rise in the most compassionate heart of Rudra, lord of elephants.

*(Exeunt omnes.)*

*End of the Second Act, called "Bhava-pariksha".*

[^1]: The print reads *tvayā locanātithikṛtaḥ* — "became the guest of your eyes"; by the sense and the edition's Hindi translation, "of her (Radha's) eyes".

[^2]: The Flower-weaponed One (Kusumayudha) is Kama, the god of love; in the drama his names (Madana, Smara, Manmatha, Ananga etc.) stand for Radha's love, which Gaudiya Vaishnavas understand as prema, not worldly passion (CC Adi 4.163–171).

[^3]: The Odd-arrowed One (Vishamabana) is Kama with his five (an odd number of) arrows. *Śilīmukha* means both "arrow" and "bee".

[^4]: Signs of hidden love: the moon, the cuckoos and the spring breeze intensify the pain of separation (*viraha*), so Radha avoids them.

[^5]: "O Shiva, Shiva!" is an exclamation of pity ("alas!"). The closing line calls the audience "devoted to the feet of Hari": Ramananda writes for Vaishnava connoisseurs.

[^6]: A bed of lotus petals, bracelets of lotus stalks and sandal are cooling remedies for the "fever" of love-separation. The printed "*viṣa*" is to be read "*bisa*", lotus stalk. The word order of the song is unclear in places and is rendered by the sense.

[^7]: The device *ākāśa-bhāṣita* ("speech to the air"): a character converses with an unseen interlocutor (here the parrots) and repeats the answers herself.

[^8]: *Viṣkambhaka*: an interlude in which minor characters report what has happened or will happen.

[^9]: The letter is in Prakrit, in the *āryā* metre. Radha reproaches Krishna: He wounds the heart, but the blame falls on Madana (Kama); she sees Krishna everywhere, not Kama — a sign of the highest love, in which the beloved is seen everywhere. The edition's Sanskrit *chāyā* has the verb in the third person (*vidhyati*); the translation follows the Prakrit (*vijjhasi*, second person).

[^10]: The key to the whole act: Krishna only feigns indifference in order to test the firmness of Radha's love (*bhāva-parīkṣā*, "testing of feeling").

[^11]: Krishna deliberately takes "Madana" literally, as a man's name, and offers to deal with him as with the demons Kamsa used to send.

[^12]: The jester takes up the game: "Madana" is both Kama and, so he claims, his own name — and a brahmin may not be punished.

[^13]: *Nisṛṣṭārtha-dūtī*: in poetics, a "messenger with full authority", who herself decides what to say and how to arrange the meeting.

[^14]: "Mountain-stronghold of her family" — that is, one faithful to the custom of her family. Krishna continues His feigned test (see note 10).

[^15]: Prakrit, *āryā* metre. An allegory: Radha (the swan), driven by the heat of love, seeks refuge with Krishna (the lotus cluster), but He, "swaying" (feigning hesitation), pushes her away. The end of the line is rendered approximately from the edition's *chāyā*.

[^16]: Madanika sees that Krishna loves Radha too and is only hiding it.

[^17]: Here and in Song 8 Krishna appeals to worldly morality. But this is only a test, as He Himself says aside twice (before verse 30 and after verse 44) — just as in the *Bhāgavata* (10.29) Krishna first advises the gopis to go back to their husbands. According to Gaudiya Vaishnava teaching the gopis are Krishna's eternal associates, His own pleasure-potency (*hlādinī*). Their "marriage" to others is arranged by Yogamaya for the fullness of rasa, and the *parakīyā* love (love as for "another's" beloved) exists only in Vraja (CC Adi 4.47). Srila Bhaktisiddhanta Sarasvati stresses that this transcendental *parakīyā* cannot be measured by worldly morality and must not be imitated in this world.

[^18]: The jester, the hero's constant companion, jokes that he is with Krishna even in His dreams.

[^19]: The line is unclear (printed *kimiti vayaṁ kalayāma na citram*). The edition's Hindi translation takes it differently: "…why should we abandon our own bounds?"
