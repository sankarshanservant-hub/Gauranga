## 95–96

Those who cannot believe the words of Śrī Gurudeva and pervert their own qualification (*adhikāra*) do not obtain the right to the *saṅkīrtana* of Kṛṣṇa's name. Śrī Gaurasundara upheld the words of the *śruti*: “To those great souls who have supreme devotion to God, and the same devotion to the guru, the whole meaning of what has been said is revealed”[^95-1] — and He began the *saṅkīrtana* of Kṛṣṇa's name. Following His guru (*ānugatya*), He did not disobey his order and never stopped the unbroken *nāma-saṅkīrtana*. And Kṛṣṇa-nāma-Prabhu Himself, whose *kīrtana* was sounding so, exerted His own power of will (*icchā-śakti*) and moved Śrī Gaurasundara to dance and sing. Śrī Gaurasundara did not chant the holy name condescendingly, as if it were a material object. But those driven by the restlessness of the senses take Kṛṣṇa's name for their plaything: instead of serving the holy name, they try to become its masters and command it. Instead of *bhajana* they reap the fruits of *karma* — they merely aggravate their bile (*pitta*) and ruin their bodily health.

“I am a fool who cannot tell good from harm. I feared that if I went searching for the pure meaning of Vedānta, the false arguments of Māyāvāda spoken by Śrī Śaṅkarācārya would come and destroy My natural inclination to *bhajana*. So, knowing that I have no right to Vedānta as Śaṅkara explains it, I am freed from the *anarthas* of worldly existence simply by chanting the mantra of Kṛṣṇa. I take up the name of Kṛṣṇa, whom the liberated worship, and thereby attain Kṛṣṇa's lotus feet. In the quarrelsome age of Kali there is no *dharma* other than taking the name. I received all these instructions from Śrī Gurudeva, and by taking the name I became almost mad. Later I asked him again and learned that when the *jīva* attains the right to *prema*, an incomparable good comes to him. *Prema* is the highest goal of life (*parama-puruṣārtha*), desirable above all, and the petty hopes of those who crave the fruits of the four goals of life cannot be compared with it. A person in whom *prema* has awakened naturally disregards public shame and chants Kṛṣṇa's glories loudly — laughing, weeping, singing, dancing and so on. I learned that this is what is called ‘the life of a *bhāgavata*.’ I have done nothing artificially, hiding behind deceit. I chant Kṛṣṇa's glories because I firmly believe My Gurudeva's words. The holy name Himself has made Me a singer and a dancer, in defiance of the grave dignity of the *kaupīna*-clad Vedāntists. My own part in this — My own will or initiative — is very small: it is all the mercy of Śrī Nāma-Prabhu.”

## 101

The Māyāvādīs call “Vedānta” only the scripture that Śrī Śaṅkarapāda's *Śārīraka-bhāṣya* has in view. In other words, when the followers of Śaṅkara's doctrine say “Vedānta,” they mean the Upaniṣads and the *Brahma-sūtra* as read through their ācārya's commentary, which is built on the doctrine of *kevalādvaita*. The *Vedānta-sāra* of Sadānanda Yogīndra says: “Vedānta is the evidence of the Upaniṣads, together with the *Śārīraka-sūtras* and other works that assist it.” In truth, however, the word “Vedānta” does not mean the doctrine of *kevalādvaita*. All four Vaiṣṇava ācāryas are ācāryas of Vedānta, yet they are not Māyāvādīs and do not follow Śaṅkara's doctrine. The followers of Māyāvāda see no difference at all; starting from *kevalādvaita*, they worship themselves as God (*ahaṅgrahopāsanā*). They accept neither *śuddhādvaita*, nor *śuddha-dvaita*, nor *viśiṣṭādvaita*, nor *dvaitādvaita*, nor *acintya-bhedābheda*; they believe that *kevalādvaita* alone is the faultless doctrine of Vedānta. The Māyāvādīs are pleased with the impermanent service that is rendered to Kṛṣṇa with a material body and mind. That is, they regard devotion to Kṛṣṇa as merely a special kind of ritual work (*karma*); so for them it too is “non-devotion” (*abhakti*), and with this they are content.

## 106

> Subheading: The Lord's view and explanation of Vedānta —

*Sūtra*: “Knowers of *sūtras* know a *sūtra* to be brief, unambiguous, full of essence, facing every side, free of superfluous words, and faultless.” (*Skanda Purāṇa* and *Vāyu Purāṇa*) The *Vedānta-sūtra* is known by various names: (1) *Brahma-sūtra*, (2) *Śārīraka*, (3) *Vyāsa-sūtra*, (4) *Bādarāyaṇa-sūtra*, (5) *Uttara-mīmāṁsā* and (6) *Vedānta-darśana*. It is a book composed of *sūtras*, with four chapters (*adhyāyas*) and sixteen quarters (*pādas*). Each *pāda* contains several topics (*adhikaraṇas*). Each topic contains the five-membered argument (*pañcāvayava-nyāya*): the proposition (*pratijñā*), the reason (*hetu*), the example (*udāharaṇa*), the application (*upanaya*) and the conclusion (*nigamana*). In other words: “One verse [states] the subject and the doubt and sets out the *pūrvapakṣa*; the other verse establishes the *siddhānta*; the connections between them are clear.”

According to the different commentaries, it has from 162 to 223 topics (*adhikaraṇas*) and from 520 to 560 *sūtras*.

On the word “Vedānta” the lexicographer Hemacandra says: “Vedānta is the Upaniṣads together with the Brāhmaṇas — the remainder of the Vedas, their last portion, that is, the end of the Vedas.” A scripture that shows the ultimate purpose of the Vedas is also “Vedānta.” A scripture that uses the Upaniṣads as its evidence, and the *sūtras* and other works that assist it, are also “Vedānta.” The *Vedānta-sūtra* is called the *nyāya-prasthāna*, one of the three *prasthānas* (foundations of doctrine). The Upaniṣads are the *śruti-prasthāna*, and the *Gītā*, the *Mahābhārata*, the Purāṇas and so on are the *smṛti-prasthāna*.

The Vedas came into the material world from the breath of Śrī Nārāyaṇa. The scripture spoken by Śrī Nārāyaṇa that unfolds the Vedas is called the *Sātvata-pañcarātra*. The *Vedānta-sūtra* was compiled by Śrī Vyāsa, the *āveśa-avatāra* of Śrī Nārāyaṇa — or, in the opinion of some (Śaṅkara's commentary 3.3.32),[^106-1] by the sage Apāntaratamā. Śrī Gaurasundara says that the *Pañcarātra* and the Vedānta express one and the same teaching. Since Śrī Vyāsa composed the *sūtras*, they too must be known as the word of Śrī Nārāyaṇa Himself.

While composing the *sūtras*, Śrī Vyāsadeva examined the Vedānta doctrines set forth by seven other sages: Ātreya, Āśmarathya, Auḍulomi, Kārṣṇājini, Kāśakṛtsna, Jaimini and Bādari. Besides these, two more collections — the *sūtras* of Pārāśarī and of Karmandī-bhikṣu — also preceded the *sūtras* of Śrī Vyāsa.

The first two chapters of the *Vedānta-darśana* describe knowledge of *sambandha*; the third chapter, *abhidheya*, the practice of devotion (*sādhana-bhakti*); and the fourth, *prayojana*, the fruit: love for Bhagavān. The genuine commentary on Vedānta, written by Vyāsa, the author of the *sūtras* himself, is the *Śrīmad-Bhāgavatam*. Besides it, the commentaries of the four Vaiṣṇava ācāryas, who follow the *Śrīmad-Bhāgavatam* to a greater or lesser degree, and the many glosses written by their successors in their *sampradāyas*, declare that Vedānta aims at the worship of Bhagavān. This *Vedānta-sūtra* is honored even in the *sampradāya* that lacks devotion to Viṣṇu and holds to the doctrine of the featureless. The commentaries on this Vedānta written from the standpoint of *māyā*, and the glosses, treatises and other works that follow them, are devoid of service to Viṣṇu, and their reasoning departs from the real truth.

The word *mukhya-vṛtti* means *abhidhā-vṛtti*. *Abhidhā* is the power [of a word] by which one understands the meaning known from dictionaries, grammar and so on. The word *gauṇa-vṛtti* means *lakṣaṇā-vṛtti*. *Lakṣaṇā* is the power by which, for some purpose or through frequent usage, one understands another meaning connected with the actual sense.

*Bhāṣya* — as it is said: “Knowers of *bhāṣya* know that a *bhāṣya* takes up the words of the *sūtra*, explains them in sentences that follow the *sūtra*, and also explains its own words.”

The highest is the Truth possessed of features (*saviśeṣa-tattva*): it is what the Upaniṣads and the *sūtras* establish, and it is shown through the direct (*abhidhā*) meaning. The *nirviśeṣa-vādīs*, however, rely on the secondary (*lakṣaṇā*) meaning and show only a semblance of truth (*tattvābhāsa*); it is known not as “Tattvavāda” but as “Māyāvāda.” Śrī Viṣṇusvāmī's *śuddhādvaita* was obstructed by the doctrine of *kevalādvaita*. Only after this did *viśiṣṭādvaita* and the “Tattvavāda” of Śrī Madhvācārya, following the path of the *śruti*, refute the conclusions of those ignorant of the truth, which rested on logic alone. Śrī Mahāprabhu relied on *abhidhā-vṛtti* and honored the meaning of Vedānta. But the meaning of Vedānta that Śrī Śaṅkarācārya set down in his commentary, relying on *lakṣaṇā-vṛtti*, ruins everything. Thus in the *Padma Purāṇa*: “Hear, O Devī, I shall tell you in order of the *tāmasa* [scriptures], by merely hearing which even the learned fall. [In Māyāvāda] the words of the *śruti* are given a false meaning, which people condemn; in it, it is taught that action must be given up by its very nature. There, freedom from action (*naiṣkarmya*) is said to be the abandonment of all action. And in it I establish the oneness of the Supreme Soul and the *jīva*.”

See Antya 2.94–99.

> Subheading: To call the Truth formless, and the body of Viṣṇu and so on a transformation of *māyā* — this is “Māyāvāda” —

The *Vedānta-sāra* of Sadānanda Yogīndra says: “The real (*vastu*) is Brahman — being, consciousness and bliss, nondual. The whole mass of inert things, beginning with ignorance (*ajñāna*), is the unreal (*avastu*). Ignorance, they say, cannot be described as either existent or nonexistent; it consists of the three *guṇas*, is opposed to knowledge, is of a positive nature, and is ‘something.’ This ignorance is spoken of as one or as many, depending on whether one means the aggregate or the individual. This aggregate, because its *upādhi* is excellent, is one in which pure *sattva* predominates. Consciousness conditioned by it, possessing qualities such as omniscience, lordship over all and control over all, is *sad-asad-vyakta*, the inner ruler, the cause of the world, and it is called Īśvara. Because it illumines all ignorance, it is omniscient.”

The Śaṅkarite Vedāntist Sadānanda summarized the purport of Śaṅkara's doctrine in the book *Vedānta-sāra*. Today this book is a highly respected, authoritative foundation of Śaṅkara's *sampradāya*.

“Brahman is the nondual Reality, being, consciousness and bliss; the whole mass of inert things, beginning with ignorance, is unreal. ‘Ignorance’ means everything that is distinct from both the existent and the nonexistent, indescribable, made of the three *guṇas*, opposed to knowledge and of a positive nature. This ignorance is spoken of both as one and as many, depending on whether it is the aggregate or the individual. When this aggregate is endowed with an excellent *upādhi*, it receives the name ‘that in which pure *sattva* predominates.’ When this [aggregate ignorance], in which pure *sattva* predominates, is reflected in consciousness, [consciousness] receives the name ‘Īśvara’ — omniscient, lord of all, ruler of all, manifest as both the existent and the nonexistent (*sad-asad-vyakta*), the inner ruler of the *jīvas*, the cause of the world. Īśvara is ‘omniscient’ because He illumines all ignorance. In their view, being Īśvara is merely a transformation of material *sattva* born of ignorance. The *jīva*, in turn, is one in whom impure *sattva* predominates, and he is endowed with an individual *upādhi*.”

## 115

> Subheading: To regard Viṣṇu, the Master of *māyā*, as a product of *māyā* — this is godlessness (*pāṣaṇḍatā*) —

The Truth possessed of features is Viṣṇu. Viṣṇu's *prakṛti* is the root of the material, inert world. In the reasoning of *vivarta-vāda*, the *prakṛti*, or *māyā-śakti*, of the featureless Brahman has no real existence. The scriptures have described Viṣṇu's *māyā* at length. Between His body and the possessor of that body there is nonduality (*advaya-jñāna*). In material things, the body and its possessor are different. Material things are objects of enjoyment, but Viṣṇu is eternally the enjoyer. To regard the “enjoyer” as the “enjoyed” is an offense. And to present the One whom the *jīva* eternally serves as a servant on a level with the *jīva* is to blaspheme Him.

## 120

> Subheading: To consider the Lord, like the *jīva*, to be full of ignorance — this too is Māyāvāda —

## 121

> Subheading: The *Brahma-sūtra* accepts only the doctrine of the transformation of potency (*śakti-pariṇāma-vāda*) —

Setting out to describe the nature of the Lord, the *jīva* and *prakṛti* as indescribable and to explain it in terms of ignorance, Śaṅkara concealed the transcendent supremacy of the Lord.

Śrī Rāmānujapāda writes in the *Vedānta-sāra*: “Objection: the statement ‘In the beginning all this was the Ātmā alone’ establishes oneness before creation. How, then, can Nārāyaṇa, qualified by subtle spirit and matter, be the cause? Answer: [it is said:] ‘That from which these beings are born, by which, once born, they live, and into which, departing, they enter.’ What is taught here is that beings, having given up their gross forms and taken on subtle ones, abide in Brahman — not that their own nature ceases. For [in the *śruti*] we hear: ‘The *akṣara* dissolves into darkness (*tamas*); darkness becomes one with the supreme Deity’ — that is, *prakṛti*, called by the word *tamas*, becomes one with the Paramātmā. To become one is to exist without being perceived separately; and that is the meaning of the word *laya* (dissolution): as birds dissolve into a tree, and deer into a forest.”

You may ask: “Before the creation of the world only the Ātmā existed (*Bṛhad-āraṇyaka* 1.4.1); how then can Nārāyaṇa, possessed of subtle spiritual and material potencies, be the root cause of the world?” The answer can be given thus. The statement of the *Taittirīya*, “He from whom these beings were born, by whom they are sustained, and into whom they enter” (*Taittirīya*, *Bhṛgu-vallī* 1), shows that beings give up their gross material forms, are released from them, take on subtle forms and [so] abide in Brahman, but their *svarūpa* is not destroyed. For when the imperishable soul dissolves into *prakṛti*, called by the word *tamas*, *prakṛti* becomes nondifferent (one) with Brahman. At that time *prakṛti* is not perceived separately from Brahman, and so the existence of *prakṛti* abides in Brahman itself. This is what the word *laya* means. An example: just as birds in a tree or animals in a forest dissolve into the tree or the forest, that is, are hidden within it.

Śrī Vedavyāsa, the author of the *Brahma-sūtra*, says: “The *ānandamaya* [is Brahman], because [this] is repeated” (*Brahma-sūtra* 1.1.12). In connection with this *sūtra*, while explaining the *sūtra* “And [scripture] teaches its union with that in it” (*Brahma-sūtra* 1.1.19), Śrī Śaṅkara wrote what may be paraphrased thus: “The statement about the *ānandamaya* lacks the word ‘Brahman,’ so the *ānandamaya* cannot be called the principal Brahman. If the *ānandamaya* were called ‘Brahman,’ then, since it has parts, one would have to speak of a Brahman possessed of features. Yet at the end of the statement about the *ānandamaya* the featureless Brahman is spoken of. If one says that in the word *ānandamaya* the suffix *mayaṭ* means abundance — ‘abounding in bliss’ (the sense in which the *bhāgavatas*, who teach spiritual variety, have used it) — then it follows that suffering also exists in it. For the word ‘abundant’ is used according to predominance, not according to scarcity. The *śruti* does not repeat (*abhyāsa*, repeated mention) the ‘*ānandamaya*’ but repeats ‘*ānanda*’ alone — precisely because the *ānandamaya* is not ‘pure Brahman.’ Had it been established that the *ānandamaya* is Brahman, then perhaps the repetition of ‘*ānanda*’ alone might have been taken as a repetition of the *ānandamaya*. But since the *ānandamaya* is connected with parts, it is certain that it is not Brahman. For all these reasons, and because in *śrutis* such as ‘Brahman is *ānanda*’ the word *ānanda* is used of the Supreme Brahman, it is clear that in other *śrutis* too what is repeated is ‘*ānanda* alone, Brahman,’ not ‘*ānandamaya*.’ True, in the *śruti* ‘*ānandamayam ātmānam*’ we see the *ānandamaya* itself repeated; but it stands in the series with the *annamaya* and the rest, and so the *ānandamaya* cannot denote pure Brahman. Near the statement about the *ānandamaya* there are the words ‘He desired: may I become many’; but the *ānandamaya* has no close connection with pure Brahman, and so it does not denote pure Brahman. The statement that follows, ‘He is *rasa*,’ and the rest depends on that [pure Brahman] and so does not point to the *ānandamaya*. Since there are no words indicating parts, such as ‘joy is His head,’ it is certain that the principal Brahman is ‘*ānanda*,’ not ‘*ānandamaya*.’ You may say: does not that *śruti* intend the Brahman possessed of features? The answer: you cannot say so — that is refuted by the *śrutis* which say He is ‘beyond speech and mind.’ Therefore the suffix *mayaṭ* in the word *ānandamaya* indicates transformation (*vikāra*), not abundance.”

Thus Śrīpāda Śaṅkara, in explaining these *sūtras*, wanted to remove the suffix *mayaṭ*, that is, to show that it is useless or superfluous — and what effort he made to repeat one and the same point again and again in *sūtras* 12–19! On this Śrīmad Jīva Prabhu says in his *Sarva-saṁvādinī*: “If one secretly intends [to show] that the author of the *sūtras* did not know the meaning of Vedānta, and, with an artfulness that betrays one's own cleverness in correcting his mistake, explains that *sūtra* on the *ānandamaya* thus — ‘In [the passage] “*ānandamaya*” and so on, Brahman, principal in its own right, is taught [by the words] “Brahman is the tail, the foundation”’ — and in the *sūtra* on *vikāra* (1.1.13) explains the word *vikāra* as ‘part’ and the word *prācurya* as ‘similarity,’ then it would follow that the author of the *sūtras* did not know the meaning of words, since by those words he would not be expressing those meanings. That the words — the suffix *mayaṭ*, *vikāra* and *prācurya*, stated one after another — have some other sense would not occur even to a child.”

Reading Śaṅkara's commentary, one gets the impression that his hidden aim is, as it were, [to show] that Śrī Vedavyāsa, the author of the *sūtras*, could not understand the meaning of Vedānta. So Śrī Śaṅkara, on the pretext of correcting the mistake of the ācārya Śrī Vedavyāsa, the author of the *sūtras*, used his own cleverness and artfully explained the *sūtra* on the *ānandamaya* thus: “Among the statements of the *śruti* beginning with ‘*ānandamaya*,’ the principal Brahman is ‘taught’ in the statement ‘Brahman is the tail, the foundation’; and in *sūtra* 1.1.13 I shall explain the word *vikāra* as ‘part’ and the word *prācurya* as ‘similarity.’” Explained this way, it follows that the author of the *sūtras* (Vyāsa) had no knowledge of words, since the words he used do not yield those meanings of Vedānta. What other meaning could the words *vikāra*, *prācurya* and the rest, derived from the suffix *mayaṭ* and stated one after another, possibly have? Even a child can see this! In other words, it is easy to understand that to give the suffix *mayaṭ* any meaning other than “transformation” and “abundance” is a plain error.

See Madhya 6.170–175 and Madhya 25.40–41.
