## 149

There is no limit to the good fortune of Nanda the cowherd and the residents of Vraja, for the eternal, complete Brahman, whose very nature is supreme bliss, has appeared as their friend.

## 150–153

“*Apāṇi-pādo javano grahītā*” (“without hands and feet, He moves swiftly and takes”; *Śvetāśvatara Upaniṣad* 3.19) — this is the *śruti*.[^150-1] First it says that Brahman has no material hands and feet; then, with the words “He moves swiftly and takes all things,” it states that He has transcendental hands and feet, and so shows Brahman to be possessed of features (*saviśeṣa*). The Māyāvādīs, however, abandon the primary meaning of the *śruti* and, by means of the secondary sense (*lakṣaṇā-vṛtti*), wrongly establish featurelessness (*nirviśeṣatva*), which denies Brahman's features. The Māyāvādīs declare Brahman “eternally formless,” but according to the scriptures that Brahman eternally abides as Bhagavān, whose form is full of bliss and of the six opulences. The Māyāvādīs have concluded that Brahman is “without potencies,” yet many scriptural statements rooted in the Vedic word “His supreme potency is heard to be manifold” (*Śvetāśvatara Upaniṣad* 6.8) acknowledge three natural potencies of that Brahman.

## 155–156

The *kṣetrajña-śakti* is the *jīva-śakti*. Although this *jīva-śakti* is all-knowing, it is covered by *avidyā*, a function of *māyā*, and constantly suffers all the miseries of worldly existence. Moreover, this potency named “*kṣetrajña*,” O king, covered and cramped by *avidyā*, exists in all beings in varying degrees. The meaning is this: Bhagavān's *cit-śakti* is the highest, the *jīva-śakti* is intermediate, and the *māyā-śakti*, called *avidyā* and karma, is the lowest. Covered by *māyā* — that is, removed from the function of the *cit-śakti* — the *jīva-śakti* suffers the miseries of worldly existence. In that state of remoteness it enters the wheel of karma that opens before it and attains higher and lower conditions.

## 158–163

According to the Vedas and Vedānta, one must know the nature (*svarūpa*) and the mutual relation of three truths: Īśvara, the *jīva* and *māyā*. First of all it is (absolutely) necessary to know the nature of Īśvara. His nature is eternity, knowledge and bliss (*sac-cid-ānanda*). Bhagavān's one *cit-śakti* manifests in three forms, according to three aspects — *sat*, *cit* and *ānanda*. In the aspect of bliss it is *hlādinī*, in the aspect of existence *sandhinī*, and in the aspect of consciousness *saṁvit*. That *saṁvit* is knowledge related to Kṛṣṇa. Īśvara's *svarūpa-śakti* manifests in three forms: the *antaraṅgā* (internal), which is the *cit-śakti* itself; the *taṭasthā* (marginal), which is the *jīva-śakti*; and the *bahiraṅgā* (external), which is the *māyā-śakti*. In each of these three manifestations one should understand three states, according to the functions of *hlādinī*, *sandhinī* and *saṁvit*. The *cit-śakti* gives the *jīva* the essence of her *hlādinī* and *saṁvit* combined. When the *jīva-śakti* accepts it, the *māyā-śakti* acts guilelessly as the *cit-śakti*: her (non-spiritual power of covering and projecting) withdraws, and she makes the *jīva* fit for loving devotion to Kṛṣṇa (*kṛṣṇa-prema-bhakti*). The sixfold opulence of the Supreme Lord is the play of His majesty; to call Him “formless” and “without potencies” is to speak in an utterly un-Vedic way. Īśvara by nature is the master of *māyā*, whereas the *jīva*, being by nature an atomic particle of consciousness, is subject to *māyā*. The *Muṇḍaka Upaniṣad* (3.1.1–2) says: “Two birds, inseparable friends, cling to the same tree. One of them eats the sweet *pippala* fruit; the other, not eating, only watches. On that same tree the person (*puruṣa*), sunk and bewildered, grieves over his helplessness. But when he sees the other, the Lord whom all serve, and His glory, he is freed from grief.” That is: “When the *jīva* forgets Īśvara, he becomes liable to punishment; for that offense *māyā*, Īśvara's jailer, imprisons the *jīva* and punishes him.” What is established here is that Īśvara's nature includes mastery over *māyā*, not subjection to her.

Although the *jīva*'s nature contains an existence free from *māyā*, it also has one property — the capacity to come under *māyā*'s control; this is called marginality (*taṭasthatva*). Since there is such an eternal difference both in nature and in essential identity, you cannot say that the *jīva* and Īśvara are one in any condition whatever. On the other hand, since the *Gītā* calls the *jīva* a “potency,” you are obliged to accept also that the *jīva* is one with Īśvara, according to the Vedānta saying “The potency and the possessor of potency are not different.” This inconceivable oneness and difference (*acintya-bhedābheda*) is the secret of the truth of Īśvara and the *jīva*.

## 164–165

Earth, water, fire, air, ether, mind, intelligence and *ahaṅkāra* — these eight are particular functions of My own lower (*aparā*) potency; the *jīva-tattva* is distinct from them.

## 166–167

According to the Vedic scriptures, Īśvara's form of eternity, knowledge and bliss is eternal. Formlessness is a particular transformation of material *sattva-guṇa*, its opposite — that is, a notion that denies the form found in material *sattva*. The spiritual form that lies beyond *prakṛti* has a spiritual shape as well. The formlessness of *māyic sattva* cannot touch it. Whoever does not accept such a Śrī Vigraha is counted among the godless (*pāṣaṇḍīs*).

## 168

Because the Buddhist Śākyasiṁha did not accept the injunctions of the Vedas, the Vedic Āryans condemn him as a *nāstika*, a denier of the Vedas. But the atheism that the Māyāvādī has preached while sheltering behind the Vedas is even more blameworthy than Buddhism, for a hidden enemy who comes disguised as a friend is far more dangerous than an open one.

## 169

The *sūtras* of Vyāsa contain the doctrine of pure devotion. In the commentary that the Māyāvādī wrote on those *sūtras*, the spiritual form of the Supreme Brahman is denied, and so is the *jīva*'s existence separate from Brahman. That commentary is therefore utterly opposed to the truth of pure devotion, and by hearing it the *jīva* is ruined. For the pride born of a vain hope — the desire to become one with Brahman — destroys pure devotion, and in reality Īśvara is not acknowledged at all.

## 172

“If *pariṇāma-vāda* is accepted, Īśvara will be subject to change (*vikārī*), and then Vyāsa will have to be called ‘mistaken’” — saying this, he found fault with the primary meaning of the *sūtra*, gave it a secondary meaning and established *vivarta-vāda*.

## 175

To explain the spiritual existence of the *jīva*, the statement *tat tvam asi* (“you are that”) is found in one part of the Vedas; it is not a *mahā-vākya*.

## 181

Bhagavān said to Śrī Mahādeva: “With your own imagined scriptures (*āgamas*) turn people away from Me. Conceal Me in such a way that the *jīvas* who have turned away from Me do not lose interest in the work of increasing the number of *jīvas*.”

## 182

Mahādeva said: “In the age of Kali I shall assume the form of a brāhmaṇa and by means of false scriptures establish hidden Buddhism in the form of Māyāvāda.”

## 186

Even sages whose delight is in the self (*ātmā*) and who are free from the knots of desire render causeless devotion to Śrī Kṛṣṇa, the doer of mighty deeds, for Hari, who steals the hearts of the whole world, has such a quality.

Eleven meanings of the eleven words of the verse, and seven more meanings obtained by joining the word *ātmārāma* to each of the seven principal words of the verse — *munayaḥ*, *nirgranthāḥ*, *urukrama*, *ahaitukī*, *bhakti*, *guṇa* and *hari* — eighteen meanings in all.
