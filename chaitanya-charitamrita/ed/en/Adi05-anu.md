## 14–18

Śrī Jīva writes in the *Śrī Kṛṣṇa-sandarbha* (section 106): “What, then, is that abode where He sports? To this it is said. The *Skanda Purāṇa* says: ‘As the cities dear to Bhagavān exist on earth, so they exist in Vaikuṇṭha, honored for the sake of those pastimes.’ According to these words, the places that exist in Vaikuṇṭha should be understood to be these very places. And they lie above all the Vaikuṇṭhas. * * * The *Svāyambhuva-āgama* also says that this place stands in its own right, above all: in the dialogue of Īśvara and Devī, in the section on meditation on the fourteen-syllable [mantra], in the eighty-fifth chapter (*paṭala*): ‘One should remember the all-pervading Vaikuṇṭha, strewn with many wish-fulfilling creepers. Below it is *prakṛti*, the equilibrium of the *guṇas*, the cause of all.’ * * * Therefore, by the rule ‘as they exist on earth’ as well, it is established that Śrī Kṛṣṇaloka, consisting of Dvārakā, Mathurā and Gokula, exists in its own right and, as the place of the pastimes of Bhagavān Himself, lies above all. Hence Vṛndāvana is that very Gokula which shines above all and is renowned as Goloka. In the *Brahma-saṁhitā*: ‘The great abode called Gokula is a lotus of a thousand petals. * * * Around it is a wondrous quadrangle called Śvetadvīpa.’ * * * The abode of this Śrī Kṛṣṇa, fit for living together with Nanda, Yaśodā and others, is the great inner palace; of its nature it is said that its origin, that is, its eternal manifestation, is from a part of Ananta, Śrī Baladeva. Read in a double sense,[^14-1] the words also convey this: there is the origin, that is, the dwelling, of Śrī Baladeva Himself, whose part is Ananta. * * * Next the coverings of Gokula are described, beginning ‘Outside it is a quadrangle.’ Outside that Gokula, on every side, a quadrangular, that is, four-cornered, place is called Śvetadvīpa, since that part has no particular name ‘Gokula.’ But the inner circle of the quadrangle should be known as Vṛndāvana, and the outer circle only as Śvetadvīpa; its other name is ‘Goloka.’ * * * Vaikuṇṭha is called Brahmaloka. * * * In the *Nārada-pañcarātra*, in the *Vijaya-ākhyāna*: ‘There, above all, in Goloka, Śrī Govinda Himself always sports, full of supreme bliss, the Lord of the gopīs and of Gokula.’ Thus it is established that Śrī Kṛṣṇaloka lies above all. It is also determined that this world, divided into parts according to the differences of those pastimes and associates, consists of three places — Dvārakā, Mathurā and Gokula. Elsewhere [in scripture], however, the places of the same names renowned on earth are spoken of as these very places: for of them, as of the other Vaikuṇṭhas, it is said that they lie beyond the material world, are eternal, have an unearthly form and are the eternal abode of Bhagavān.”

In what kind of abode does this Bhagavān move about? On this it is said: “As cities dear to Bhagavān exist in this material world, so three cities abide in Vaikuṇṭha for the sake of His pastimes.” According to these words of the *Skanda Purāṇa*, it should be known that the places existing in Vaikuṇṭha are the very same places that are in the material world. Above the material creation lie all the Vaikuṇṭhas. The *Svāyambhuva-tantra*, too, says that the place of Vaikuṇṭha stands in its own right, above all. In that book, in the section on meditation on the fourteen-syllable [mantra], in the 85th *paṭala*, in the dialogue of Devī and Maheśvara, it is said: “One should remember the all-pervading, undivided Vaikuṇṭha, strewn with many wish-fulfilling creepers. Below it lies *prakṛti* — the equilibrium of the *guṇas*, the cause of all material causes.” Therefore from the rule “as the abodes of Hari exist on earth, so also there” we understand that Kṛṣṇaloka, consisting of Dvārakā, Mathurā and Gokula, exists in its own right. It is the field of the pastimes of Bhagavān Himself, and so it is established that these abodes lie above all. Hence Vṛndāvana-Gokula is renowned as “Goloka,” shining above all. In the *Brahma-saṁhitā*: “The great abode shaped like a lotus of a thousand petals is known as ‘Gokula.’ The wondrous place around it, a quadrangle — that is, bounded by four straight lines — is called ‘Śvetadvīpa.’” In that abode of Kṛṣṇa is His great inner palace, fit for living with Nanda, Yaśodā and others. Its nature is described thus: that abode eternally arises from Ananta, the part of Baladeva Prabhu. The *tantra* scriptures convey the same: the abode of Bhagavān is where Anantadeva, the part of Baladeva, appears and dwells. The coverings of Gokula are described thus. Outside Gokula, on every side, lies a quadrangular place (a four-cornered field) known as “Śvetadvīpa.” The part that is Śvetadvīpa does not bear the name “Gokula”; but the inner circle of the quadrangle is known by the name “Vṛndāvana,” and only the outer circle should be known as “Śvetadvīpa”; its other name is “Goloka.” The word “Brahmaloka” means “Vaikuṇṭha.” In the *Nārada-pañcarātra*, in the account of the *Vijaya-ākhyāna*, it is said that in that abode, above all, in Goloka, Govindadeva Himself — Gopīnātha, the Lord of Gokula — always sports in supreme bliss. From this it is established that Kṛṣṇaloka lies above all worlds. It is also determined that this very Kṛṣṇaloka, by the differences of pastimes and associates and by the differences of parts, is the three places with many chambers, named “Dvārakā,” “Mathurā” and “Gokula.” Elsewhere [in scripture] we hear that the places of the same names on earth, in the material world, are of the same nature: for of them, as of the other Vaikuṇṭhas, it is said that they lie beyond the material world, are eternal, have an unearthly form and are the eternal abode of Bhagavān. Therefore they too should be known as nondifferent [from the abodes of Kṛṣṇaloka].

## 28

Śrī, Bhū and Nīlā. Following a Bengali reading, some call Nīlā “Līlā-śakti.” These three potencies abide in Vaikuṇṭha beside Nārāyaṇa. Once Bhūta-yogī, Sara-yogī and Bhrānta-yogī (the Āḻvārs) took shelter at night in a brāhmaṇa's house in the village of Gehalī. Then Nārāyaṇa entered that house and revealed His form to them.

*Prapannāmṛta*, Chapter 77, verses 61–62: “They saw Viṣṇu, the original Bhagavān. He was mounted on Tārkṣya (Garuḍa) and looked like a cloud with lightning; He bore Lakṣmī on His chest, His eyes were like lotuses, and in two hands shone the conch and the disc. His arms reached to His knees, His body was lovely, and Bhūmi and Nīlā shone at His two sides. He wore yellow garments, His body was adorned with ornaments and anointed with sandal paste, and He had four arms.”

In the *Sītā Upaniṣad*: “Mahālakṣmī of the Lord of the gods is both different and nondifferent from Him; She consists of the conscious and the unconscious. As potency this goddess is of three kinds: *icchā-śakti* (the potency of will), *kriyā-śakti* (the potency of action) and *sākṣāt-śakti* (the direct potency). *Icchā-śakti* is of three kinds: Śrī, Bhūmi and Līlā.”

In his commentary on the *Gītā* (Chapter 4, verse 6) Śrī Madhvācārya cites scripture: “She who is the mother of the *mahat* and the rest, conceived of as Śrī, Bhū and Nīlā, and the bewildering one named Durgā — through them Viṣṇu, though unborn, is renowned as if born among those whose minds are bewildered by the power of His own *cit*[-potency].” * * * “Mahāmāyā, Vaiṣṇavī, divided as Śrī, Bhū and Durgā, is infinitely below His potency. Yet because she has taken shelter of the Lord, even Ananta, Brahmā, Rudra and others have not a fraction of her power. Without the grace of Viṣṇu even they cannot overcome her.” (*Vyāsa-yoga*, Chapter 7, section 14.)

Madhva's commentary on the *Gītā* (Chapter 14, verse 3): “‘The great Brahman’ is *prakṛti*. She is divided as Śrī, Bhū and Durgā. Umā, Sarasvatī and others are other *jīvas*, possessing a part of her.” So too the *Kārṣāyaṇa-śruti*: “Śrī, Bhū and Durgā are the great *māyā*; she brings forth the worlds and binds the universe. Umā, Vāk and others are other *jīvas*, her parts; in all the Vedas they are sung as one with her in nature.”

Śrī Jīva Prabhu in the *Bhagavat-sandarbha* (section 80): “As the *Padma Purāṇa* says: ‘The eternal, auspicious form of the Lord abides in the supreme abode; He is eternally delighted by the sovereign Śrī and by Bhūmi and is surrounded by them.’ In the *Mahā-saṁhitā* this is distinguished by name and nature as well: ‘His threefold potency — Śrī, Bhū and Durgā — which is divided, is the *jīva-māyā* of the Great Soul. *Ātma-māyā* is His will, and *guṇa-māyā* is material.’” (Ibid., section 22): “Śrī here is the potency that maintains the world, Bhū the potency of its creation, Durgā the potency of its dissolution. His potency that has become distinct in these forms and is directed toward the *jīvas* is called *jīva-māyā*. In the *Padma Purāṇa*, in the dialogue of Śrī Kṛṣṇa and Satyabhāmā, after the words ‘I Myself, dividing threefold, abide through the three kinds of *guṇas*,’ it is said: ‘Then all the gods, hearing this and urged by those words, full of devotion, bowed down to Gaurī, Lakṣmī and Dharā.’”

## 36

> Subheading: (*Bhakti-rasāmṛta-sindhu*, *Pūrva-vibhāga*, *Sādhana-bhakti-laharī*, verse 278)

**yat** (if in some scripture) **arīṇāṁ** (of the enemies of Bhagavān) **priyāṇāṁ ca** (and of the devotees of Bhagavān) **ekam prāpyam** **uditam** (is declared), **tat** **kiraṇārkopamā-juṣoḥ brahma-kṛṣṇayoḥ aikyāt** (because of the oneness — that is, it should be understood that the featureless Brahman, which is like the rays, and Kṛṣṇa, who is like the sun, are in truth nondifferent).

*Śrīmad-Bhāgavatam* 10.2.32: “Others, O lotus-eyed Lord, think themselves liberated, but their intelligence is impure because they lack devotion to You. Having climbed with difficulty to the highest position, they fall down from there, for they have neglected Your feet.”

Śrī Rūpa Prabhu writes in the *Laghu-bhāgavatāmṛta*, describing the glory of Śrī Kṛṣṇa (25–37): “There, in the fourth part [of the *Viṣṇu Purāṇa*] (*Viṣṇu Purāṇa* 4.15.1–10), is Maitreya's question: ‘Slain by Viṣṇu as Hiraṇyakaśipu and as Rāvaṇa, he obtained enjoyments unattainable even by the immortals. Yet then he did not attain *sāyujya* with Him. How, then, did he, as Śiśupāla, attain *sāyujya* with the eternal Hari?’ Parāśara's answer: ‘To slay the lord of the Daityas, He who creates, maintains and destroys all the worlds took an unprecedented body and manifested the form of Nṛsiṁha. But Hiraṇyakaśipu did not think, “This is Viṣṇu.” He thought, “This is a being born of an unsurpassed store of merit.” His one-pointed mind, driven by an excess of *rajas*, was absorbed in that thought. Therefore, having died at His hand, he obtained, as the Ten-headed One (Rāvaṇa), an unsurpassed wealth of enjoyment surpassing everything in the three worlds. But his mind did not dissolve into that beginningless and endless Bhagavān, the Supreme Brahman, for he had not made Him his support. As the Ten-headed One, too, subject to Anaṅga (Kāma), he gave his heart to Jānakī and saw only the form of Him who had taken the form of Daśaratha's son. He did not think, “This is Acyuta,” and because of his attachment, at the hour of death his heart saw in Him only a man. Then the sole fruit of being felled by Acyuta was birth as Śiśupāla in the line of the kings of Cedi, renowned throughout the earth, and unimpeded wealth. At that time all the names of Bhagavān found their causes [in Kṛṣṇa]. Therefore his mind, bound by an enmity toward Him that had run through many births, endlessly uttered all these names of Acyuta, prompted by those causes — in abuse, threats and the like. And because of his deep-rooted enmity, His form never left his mind — not while he walked, ate, bathed, sat or lay down, nor in any other state. So, uttering His names in his abuse and holding Him alone in his heart, when the hour of his destruction came, freed of faults such as enmity, he saw Bhagavān — shining with the garland of rays of the disc He had hurled, the embodiment of imperishable effulgence, the Supreme Brahman. At that very moment he was slain by Bhagavān's disc; remembrance of Him burned up his entire store of sins; Bhagavān brought him to his end — and he dissolved into Him alone. All this I have told you. For this Bhagavān, even when glorified and remembered with persistent enmity, bestows a fruit that all the gods and *asuras* find hard to attain. What then of those who possess true devotion?’

“Parāśara did not say here that these two were [the Lord's] associates. It is said only that the two of them had three births. Therefore they are not held to be [the Lord's] associates in every *kalpa*; otherwise their fall in every *kalpa* would make no sense.

“When Hari manifested the wondrous form of Nṛsiṁha, Hiraṇyakaśipu had no firm idea that this was Viṣṇu. He concluded, ‘This is someone endowed with merit.’ His mind was driven by an excess of *rajas*, and, absorbed in that idea, he then obtained as Rāvaṇa the best of all and most rare wealth of enjoyment, whose only cause was his death [at His hand]. Since he was not certain that this was Viṣṇu, there was no unbroken absorption through intense enmity. And without it enmity leads only to hell, as with Vena. Yet he obtained wealth as the fruit of death at His hand. Remembering this good, the sage spoke with the word *eva* (‘only’). Since there was no absorption and his faults were not destroyed, he did not see [the Lord] as pure; and so, although the form of the Supreme Brahman was manifest, he did not dissolve into Him. As Rāvaṇa too, having given himself over to great *kāma*, he likewise saw a man in Śrī Rāma even at the hour of death. Therefore, as king of Cedi, he again obtained the highest wealth. Then in Kṛṣṇa there arose causes for all the names of the Lord of Ramā. From this he concluded that He was Viṣṇu — for He had twice been the cause of his death. Out of intense enmity and great absorption he endlessly uttered all those names, constantly abusing, threatening and so on. And seeing such a form and knowing for certain, ‘This is Viṣṇu,’ he remembered it everywhere and always, just like the names. His heap of sins born of enmity was burned; when the disc was hurled, its radiance removed his demonic nature, and at the end, his vision purified, he saw the radiant Supreme Brahman in a form like a man's. When the blow of the disc destroyed the body of the Daitya, he dissolved into that very Supreme Brahman. Having said this, [the sage] recalled that Bakī (Pūtanā) and others attained liberation through [Kṛṣṇa's] childhood pastimes, while Kālanemi and others, even by the Lord's will, did not attain liberation in other [avatāras] — and he said again: ‘For this Bhagavān …’ *Hi* (‘for’) means ‘as is well known’: this Kṛṣṇa is Bhagavān Himself. Therefore He quickly draws the hearts both of those who love Him and of those who hate Him. Hence the greatness spoken of in the words ‘when glorified …’ is no wonder here.”

Paraphrase of the meaning: “In the fourth part of the *Viṣṇu Purāṇa* Maitreya asks Parāśara: ‘That Daitya who, in the bodies of Hiraṇyakaśipu and Rāvaṇa, obtained enjoyments unattainable even by the immortals, yet did not obtain liberation — how did he, in the body of Śiśupāla, attain *sāyujya* with Śrī Kṛṣṇa?’ Parāśara's answer: ‘When Śrī Nṛsiṁhadeva appeared, Hiraṇyakaśipu did not think of Him, “This is Viṣṇu,” but took Him for some being born of a heap of merit. Because of the predominance of *rajo-guṇa*, at the hour of death he could not think of His form. But because he died at His hand, in the body of Rāvaṇa he obtained boundless wealth of enjoyment that gave him power over the three worlds. He did not regard Bhagavān as his support (*ālambana*), that is, as the *viṣaya-vigraha*, the one to be served — so his mind did not dissolve into Bhagavān. In the body of Rāvaṇa, subject to *kāma*, he set his heart on Jānakī and only saw the form of Bhagavān Śrī Rāmacandra. But at the hour of death he did not understand that Śrī Rāma was Viṣṇu: in his heart there was only the idea that He was a man. Then, because he fell at Śrī Rāma's hand, he was born in the body of Śiśupāla in the renowned line of the kings of Cedi and obtained great wealth. Śrī Kṛṣṇa was called Vāsudeva, and [Śiśupāla] saw Viṣṇu in Him. For many births he had been hostile to Him; that enmity had grown firmly into his heart, and so even in abuse and threats he uttered the names of Kṛṣṇa. Because of his deep-rooted enmity the beautiful form of Bhagavān never left the mind of Śiśupāla, absorbed in Kṛṣṇa, in any state — not while he walked, ate, bathed, sat, lay down and so on. He uttered that name in anger and the like and held that form in his heart; so at the last hour his offenses — enmity and the rest — went away, and in the radiance of the rays of the Sudarśana disc that had come to destroy him, he saw the true form of Bhagavān, the Supreme Brahman. Remembrance of Bhagavān (though hostile) burned up his heap of sins; slain by Bhagavān's disc, Śiśupāla came near to Bhagavān and dissolved into Him. O Maitreya, this is the answer to your question. If the haters of Kṛṣṇa, by hostile cultivation (*pratikūla-anuśīlana*), even while persisting in enmity, attain a good destination, what doubt can there be that pure devotees, by favorable cultivation, will attain the highest goal of all — the lotus feet of Kṛṣṇa, that is, love for Kṛṣṇa?’ Parāśara did not say that these two Daityas had formerly been Bhagavān's associates Jaya and Vijaya; he said only that they took birth three times. Therefore Parāśara did not mean that these two associates of Bhagavān are born as *asuras* in every *kalpa*. Otherwise the associates of Bhagavān would fall in every *kalpa*, and that is entirely incongruous. (That is to say: in Bhagavān Viṣṇu, just as the will to create, the will to fight is also eternally present. A *mahārāja* fond of sport always plays with rivals set against him. When there are no rivals, he makes his own courtiers or servants into opponents and sports with them; and these servants, playing in a hostile mood, give their master delight. So too Bhagavān Viṣṇu: He makes hostile the *jīvas* who have been turned away from Him since time without beginning, or some of His own associates; they take on a hostile mood, and so both sides satisfy their longing for the play of battle. Therefore the fall of Bhagavān's associates in every *kalpa* is incongruous.) When Bhagavān appeared in the unearthly form of Nṛsiṁha, Hiraṇyakaśipu did not see Viṣṇu in Him: He seemed to him merely a being born of a heap of merit. The predominance of *rajo-guṇa* disordered his intelligence: he thought of Nṛsiṁha, ‘This is some mighty creature,’ and so at the last hour he could not think of His form. Therefore, merely because he died at Nṛsiṁha's hand, he obtained in the body of Rāvaṇa a rare wealth of enjoyment. Since there was no firm conviction that this was Viṣṇu, and no intense enmity, his absorption in Bhagavān did not grow. Enmity without such growing absorption in Bhagavān, as with King Vena, leads only to hell. Without the most intense absorption the offenses born of abuse and the like are not destroyed. There was no absorption, the offenses did not vanish, and so Hiraṇyakaśipu did not see the pure form of Bhagavān and could not dissolve into Him, though the Supreme Brahman Nṛsiṁhadeva stood manifest before him. In the body of Rāvaṇa, too, his mind was wholly subject to *kāma*, and so, like Hiraṇyakaśipu, he saw a man in Śrī Rāma. Therefore that Daitya, in the form of Śiśupāla, again obtained, as before, a fine wealth of enjoyment. Śrī Kṛṣṇa was called Vāsudeva, and by that name [Śiśupāla] then concluded that He was the cause of his death in his two previous births. Out of intense enmity and great absorption he constantly uttered all those names, even in abuse and threats; seeing in Him the four-armed form and so on, and convinced that this was Viṣṇu, he thought of that form unceasingly, just as he uttered the names. Therefore his heap of sins born of enmity was burned; the radiance of the disc hurled by Śrī Kṛṣṇa destroyed his demonic nature; gaining purified, divine vision, he saw the form of the Supreme Brahman, like that of a man. Then the blow of the Sudarśana disc destroyed his demonic body, and he dissolved into the Supreme Brahman. Having said that Śiśupāla, through the most intense absorption in Śrī Kṛṣṇa by way of enmity, attained merging (*sāyujya-laya*) in Him, and having considered that Pūtanā and others slain in His childhood pastimes attained liberation, while Kālanemi and others slain in other avatāras, by the design of the Lord, did not, [the sage] uttered this prose passage. *Hi* is used in the sense ‘as is well known.’ If one glorifies and remembers the source of the avatāras rather than other avatāras — even with enmity, that is, in a hostile mood — even such *asuras* attain a good destination.” See also the commentary of Śrīpāda Baladeva Vidyābhūṣaṇa.

Śrī Sanātana Prabhu in the *Bṛhad-bhāgavatāmṛta* (second part, called *Goloka-māhātmya*, 30–31): “Ah, how can liberation be praised, when even the Daityas obtain it — those whom the scriptures themselves condemn as killers of cows, brāhmaṇas and others? *Sādhus* and *asuras* are opposites in every way; so it is fitting that their means and their goal be opposite too.”

In the tenth volume of the *Śrī Sajjana-toṣaṇī*, the translation by Śrīmad Bhaktivinoda Ṭhākura: “The scriptures condemn the Daityas — Kaṁsa and others — as killers of cows, brāhmaṇas and others. How then can the *sāyujya* liberation those Daityas obtained be praised? The *sādhus* are the devotees of Bhagavān, and the *asuras* are those who hate Him. Just as *sādhus* and *asuras* are always opposite in nature, so their means and goals must be opposite too. The means of the *asuras* is enmity toward the *sādhus* and the killing of cows and brāhmaṇas, and their goal is liberation. The means of the devotees is devotion, and their goal is *prema*. Therefore those who strive for such liberation, like wicked people, take up a wicked means — the mere endeavor of *jñāna*.”

## 41–48

Saṅkarṣaṇa is another name for “Mahā-Saṅkarṣaṇa” (He is described below, in 42–48).

In the second *pāda* of the second chapter of the *Brahma-sūtra*, in the *Utpatty-asambhava-adhikaraṇa*, Śrī Śaṅkarācārya in his commentary put forward a mistaken argument about the *catur-vyūha*. Settling the matter, the author refutes that doctrine in 41–47. Śrīpāda [Śaṅkara] erred in taking Viṣṇu, the nondual Knowledge (*advaya-jñāna*), for one of the objects of the visible world; in the *Pañcarātra* Śrī Nārāyaṇa Himself made this clear to him. Yet to bewilder the conditioned *jīvas* of demoniac nature he had to resort to deception (*vipralipsā*). Because of this the followers of Advaita — Appayya Dīkṣita and others — have reached the utmost limit of error. With the capacities of conditioned *jīvas*, knowledge of the *catur-vyūha* is not possible. The ācārya spoke such ill words to increase their folly. The *catur-vyūha* is made of *śuddha-sattva*, delights in the play of the *cit-śakti* and possesses the six kinds of majesty. To call Them poor and powerless, and to think of Them so, is the trait of deluded *jīvas*. Such *jīvas* are fit only to be bewildered by *māyā*. Whoever cannot understand [the difference between] Vaikuṇṭha and the realm of *māyā* falls into just this kind of error. In his commentary on *sūtras* 42–45 of the second *pāda* of the second chapter of the *Brahma-sūtra*, Śrīpāda Śaṅkara made a vain attempt to refute “the doctrine of the *catur-vyūha*.” Below are quoted from Śrīpāda Śaṅkarācārya's commentary his words on the “*catur-vyūha*,” rooted in his distorted understanding.

*Utpatty-asambhavāt* (42) (Śaṅkara's commentary): * * * “On this the Bhāgavatas hold: Bhagavān Vāsudeva alone, stainless, whose nature is knowledge, is the highest reality. Dividing Himself fourfold, He abides in the form of the *vyūha* of Vāsudeva, the *vyūha* of Saṅkarṣaṇa, the *vyūha* of Pradyumna and the *vyūha* of Aniruddha. Vāsudeva is the name for the Supreme Self, Saṅkarṣaṇa for the *jīva*, Pradyumna for the mind, Aniruddha for the ego (*ahaṅkāra*). Of these, Vāsudeva is the highest *prakṛti*, and the others — Saṅkarṣaṇa and the rest — are effects. To this we say: what is asserted — that the supreme Nārāyaṇa, the Supreme Self, the Self of all, by Himself dividing Himself in many ways, abides in the form of *vyūhas* — is not refuted. But what is said further — that from Vāsudeva Saṅkarṣaṇa is born, from Saṅkarṣaṇa Pradyumna, and from Pradyumna Aniruddha — to this we say: the birth of the *jīva* called Saṅkarṣaṇa from the Supreme Self called Vāsudeva is impossible, for then defects such as impermanence would follow. If the *jīva* were born, defects such as impermanence would attach to it, and then liberation — attaining Bhagavān — would be impossible for it, for an effect, on reaching its cause, would dissolve into it. And the Ācārya will reject the birth of the *jīva* [by the *sūtra*] ‘The self [is not born], because of *śruti*, and because of eternity [known] from them.’ Therefore their construction is incoherent.”

The meaning of the commentary is this: “The Bhāgavatas hold that Bhagavān Vāsudeva is one, stainless, His body is knowledge, and He is the highest reality. He Himself has divided Himself into four and so abides. These four *vyūhas* are: 1) the *vyūha* of Vāsudeva, 2) the *vyūha* of Saṅkarṣaṇa, 3) the *vyūha* of Pradyumna, 4) the *vyūha* of Aniruddha; these four *vyūhas* are His body. Another name of Vāsudeva is ‘Paramātmā’; of Saṅkarṣaṇa, ‘*jīva*’; of Pradyumna, ‘mind’; and of Aniruddha, ‘*ahaṅkāra*.’ Of the four *vyūhas*, the *vyūha* of Vāsudeva is the highest *prakṛti*, that is, the root cause. Saṅkarṣaṇa and the others have arisen from the *vyūha* of Vāsudeva, and so Saṅkarṣaṇa, Pradyumna and Aniruddha are the effects of that highest *prakṛti*. For a long time the *jīva* goes to the temple of Bhagavān, gathers [the articles of worship], performs worship (*ijyā*), studies [scripture] and practices *yoga*; thus it becomes free from sin, obtains a pure body and attains the highest *prakṛti*, Bhagavān. The great-souled Bhāgavatas say that Nārāyaṇa is beyond *prakṛti*, renowned as the Paramātmā, and is the Self of all — this does not contradict *śruti*. We also accept that He by Himself abides in the form of many *vyūhas*. Therefore this part of the Bhāgavata doctrine is not refuted by this *sūtra*. The Ācārya composed this *sūtra* only to reject the part where the Bhāgavatas say that from Vāsudeva Saṅkarṣaṇa was born, from Saṅkarṣaṇa Pradyumna, and from Pradyumna Aniruddha. The birth of the *jīva* called Saṅkarṣaṇa from the Paramātmā called Vāsudeva is utterly impossible: the *jīva* would then be subject to defects such as impermanence. If the *jīva* is born, defects such as impermanence are unavoidable for it. And if the *jīva* is perishable by nature, liberation — attaining Bhagavān — is impossible for it altogether. When the cause perishes, the effect must perish too. Ācārya Vedavyāsa rejected the birth of the *jīva* by the *sūtra* of the third *pāda* of the second chapter, ‘The self [is not born], because of *śruti*, and because of eternity [known] from them,’ and by rejecting its birth he will prove its eternity. Therefore this construction is incoherent.”

*Na ca kartuḥ karaṇam* (43) (Śaṅkara's commentary): “For this reason too their construction is incoherent: in the world we do not see an instrument, such as an axe, arising from an agent such as Devadatta. Yet the Bhāgavatas describe that from the agent, the *jīva* called Saṅkarṣaṇa, there arises the instrument, the mind called Pradyumna, and from that, born of the agent, arises the ego called Aniruddha. Without an example we cannot accept this. Nor do we find any such *śruti*.”

The meaning of the commentary is this: “There is a reason why such a construction is incoherent. In the world we do not see an instrument — a sickle and the like — arising from an agent such as Devadatta. Yet the Bhāgavatas describe that from the agent, the *jīva* named Saṅkarṣaṇa, there arose the instrument, the mind named Pradyumna, and from that Pradyumna, born of the agent, arises Aniruddha, the *ahaṅkāra*. If the Bhāgavatas cannot explain this by an example, how can it be accepted? Nor do we hear any words of *śruti* that disclose this truth.”

*Vijñānādi-bhāve vā tad-apratiṣedhaḥ* (44) (Śaṅkara's commentary): “It may also be said thus: Saṅkarṣaṇa and the others are not meant as the *jīva* and the rest. What, then? All of them are accepted as Lords, endowed with the attributes of the Lord — knowledge, sovereignty, power, strength, valor and splendor; all of them are Vāsudevas, faultless, without [external] support, untainted. Therefore the defect described — the impossibility of origin — does not apply to them. To this it is said: even so, *tad-apratiṣedhaḥ* — the impossibility of origin is still not removed. The point is that this defect, the impossibility of origin, arises in another way. How? If the intention is that these four — Vāsudeva and the others — are Lords distinct from one another, of equal attributes, and have no single self, then positing many Lords is pointless, for the Lord's work is accomplished by one Lord alone. And their own doctrine collapses, since they accept that Bhagavān Vāsudeva alone is the highest reality. If, however, the intention is that these four *vyūhas* of equal attributes belong to one Bhagavān, the impossibility of origin remains as before. For Saṅkarṣaṇa cannot arise from Vāsudeva, nor Pradyumna from Saṅkarṣaṇa, nor Aniruddha from Pradyumna, since there is no pre-eminence (*atiśaya*) among them. Between effect and cause there must be pre-eminence, as between clay and pot. Without pre-eminence one cannot establish which is the effect and which the cause. And the followers of the *Pañcarātra* doctrine accept no difference — whether in any one of them or in all — arising from greater or lesser knowledge, sovereignty and the like: they hold all the *vyūhas* to be Vāsudeva without distinction. Nor would the *vyūhas* of Bhagavān stop at the number four, for the whole world, from Brahmā down to a blade of grass, is understood to be a *vyūha* of Bhagavān.”

The meaning of the commentary is this: “The Bhāgavatas may also intend the following: Saṅkarṣaṇa and the others named are not of the nature of the *jīva*; all of them are Lords, all possess the potency of knowledge and the potency of sovereignty, are endowed with power, valor and splendor, all are Vāsudeva, all are faultless, without [external] support and untainted. So the defect of impossibility of origin does not apply to them. To this we say: even with such an intention the defect of impossibility of origin is not removed — it remains, only in another form. Vāsudeva, Saṅkarṣaṇa, Pradyumna and Aniruddha are different from one another and not of one self, and at the same time all are alike in attributes and all are Lords. If this is meant, one must accept many Lords. But there is no need to accept many Lords: the purpose is served by accepting one Lord. Moreover, since it is asserted that Bhagavān Vāsudeva is one, that is, without a second, and is the highest reality, the defect of contradicting one's own doctrine (*siddhānta-hāni*) also arises. And if this *catur-vyūha* belongs to Bhagavān Himself and They are all alike in attributes, the defect of impossibility of origin still cannot be avoided. For without some pre-eminence (inequality — more or less) Saṅkarṣaṇa cannot be born of Vāsudeva, Pradyumna of Saṅkarṣaṇa, or Aniruddha of Pradyumna. One must accept that there is some distinction between effect and cause, as when a pot comes from clay. Without pre-eminence one cannot point out which is the effect and which the cause. And consider further: the adherents of the *Pañcarātra* doctrine do not accept a difference between Vāsudeva and the others in greater or lesser knowledge and so on; on the contrary, they honor the four *vyūhas* as Vāsudeva without distinction. Are the *vyūhas* of Bhagavān exhausted by the number four? Certainly not. Both *śruti* and *smṛti* prove that the whole world, from Brahmā down to a blade of grass, is a *vyūha* of Bhagavān.”

*Vipratiṣedhāc ca* (45) (Śaṅkara's commentary): “And in this scripture many kinds of contradiction are found, such as the positing of quality and possessor of qualities. For we see [statements such as]: ‘Knowledge, sovereignty, power, strength, valor and splendor are qualities; and they are the Self, the Bhagavāns, the Vāsudevas.’”

The meaning of the commentary is this: “In the *Pañcarātra* and other scriptures of the Bhāgavatas many contradictory constructions are seen — for instance, on the relation of quality and its possessor. It is in no way possible to be at once the quality and the possessor of qualities. Yet the Bhāgavatas say that the potency of knowledge, the potency of sovereignty, power, valor and splendor — these qualities — and Pradyumna and the others, though different, are the Self and Bhagavān Vāsudeva.”

In reply to this doctrine of Śrīpāda Śaṅkarācārya, Śrī Rūpa Prabhu writes in the *Laghu-bhāgavatāmṛta* (describing the *catur-vyūha*, verses 80–83): “Of the four *vyūhas* renowned by the name ‘Mahāvasthā,’ He [Vāsudeva] is the first; He is to be worshipped in the *citta*, for He is its presiding deity (*adhidaivata*). He is also spoken of as the seat of *viśuddha-sattva*. His own part is Bhagavān Śrī Saṅkarṣaṇa. This Saṅkarṣaṇa is regarded as the second *vyūha*; He may also be called the *jīva*, for He is the place from which all *jīvas* become manifest. His luster is sweeter than myriads of full autumn moons. He is worshipped in the *ahaṅkāra*; He has placed His own part in Śeṣa. Becoming the indwelling ruler (*antaryāmī*) of Rudra, the enemy of Smara (Kāma), and of *adharma*, the serpents, Antaka and the enemies of the gods, He brings about the destruction of the world. The third *vyūha* is Pradyumna, renowned as His *vilāsa*. The wise worship this Pradyumna in the *buddhi-tattva*. In Ilāvṛta the goddess Śrī serves Him, singing His praises. His luster is like pure *jāmbūnada* gold, and in places like a dark rain-cloud. He is the source of the creation of the universe; He has placed His own part in Kāma. Becoming the indwelling ruler of Vidhi (Brahmā), the Prajāpatis, the attached and Smara, He properly accomplishes creation. The fourth *vyūha* is Aniruddha, praised as His *vilāsa*. The thoughtful worship this Aniruddha in the *manas-tattva*. Like a dark-blue cloud, He is intent on protecting the universe. Becoming the indwelling ruler of Dharma, the Manus, the gods and kings, He maintains the world. In the *Mokṣa-dharma*, however, it is said that Pradyumna is the presiding deity of the mind and Aniruddha of the *ahaṅkāra*. Yet the order given [above] is accepted in all the *Pañcarātras*. The *Padma Purāṇa*, however, says that the four *vyūhas* — Vāsudeva and the others — abide in order in the four directions of Paramavyoma, beginning with the east.”

Of the four renowned *vyūhas* of Nārāyaṇa, the Lord of Mahā-Vaikuṇṭha in Paravyoma, called “Mahāvasthā,” this Vāsudeva is “the first *vyūha*.” He is to be worshipped in the *citta*, for He is its presiding deity and abides in *viśuddha-sattva* (*Bhāgavatam* 4.3.23). Śrī Saṅkarṣaṇa is His own part, that is, His *vilāsa*. Saṅkarṣaṇa is called “the second *vyūha*,” and since He is the source from which all *jīvas* become manifest, He is also called “*jīva*.” The radiance of His body is sweeter than the white rays of countless full autumn moons. He is worshipped in the *ahaṅkāra-tattva*. He has invested His supporting potency (*ādhāra-śakti*) in Anantadeva, and as the *antaryāmī* of Rudra, the enemy of Smara, and of *adharma*, the serpents, Antaka and the *asuras*, He carries out the destruction of the world. The *vilāsa-mūrti* of that Saṅkarṣaṇa is Pradyumna, the third *vyūha*. The wise worship this Pradyumna in the *buddhi-tattva*. The goddess Lakṣmī serves Him in Ilāvṛta-varṣa, singing of His qualities. The radiance of His body is in some places like molten *jāmbūnada* gold, in others like a fresh dark rain-cloud. He is the source of the creation of the universe; He has invested His creative potency in Kandarpa. As the *antaryāmī* of the creator (Vidhātā), of all the Prajāpatis, of beings attached to sense objects — gods, humans and others — and of Kandarpa, He accomplishes creation. His *vilāsa-mūrti* is the fourth *vyūha*, Aniruddha. The thoughtful worship this Aniruddha in the *manas-tattva*. The radiance of His body is like a dark-blue cloud. He is intent on protecting the universe. As the *antaryāmī* of Dharma, the Manus, the gods and kings, He maintains the world. The *Mokṣa-dharma* names Pradyumna the presiding deity of the mind and Aniruddha the presiding deity of the *ahaṅkāra*. The order stated before (that is, that Pradyumna is the presiding deity of *buddhi* and Aniruddha of the mind) is accepted by all kinds of *Pañcarātra*. [The *Padma Purāṇa*, however, describes the four *vyūhas* — Vāsudeva and the others — as abiding in order in the four directions of Paravyoma, beginning with the east.]

On the *vilāsa* of Bhagavān and His inconceivable potency the *Laghu-bhāgavatāmṛta* (44–66) says: “But surely we hear in scripture the words of the *Mahā-varāha Purāṇa*: ‘All the bodies of that Supreme Self are eternal and everlasting; they are neither given up nor taken on, and never anywhere born of *prakṛti*. They are masses of supreme bliss, knowledge through and through; all are full of every excellence and free from every fault.’ And in the *Śrī Nārada-pañcarātra*: ‘As a gem, joined with blue, yellow and other colors, takes on different appearances, so Acyuta [appears differently] according to the difference of meditation.’ How, then, do you explain gradation (*tāratamya*) among Them? To this it is said: oneness and separateness, and likewise being a part and being the whole, are quite fitting in Him, the One, by His inconceivable, unlimited potency. Separate manifestation within oneness — as [in the *Bhāgavatam* 10.69.2]: ‘How wonderful! The one [Kṛṣṇa], in one body, at the same time married sixteen thousand women in separate palaces.’ Oneness of form within separateness — as in the *Padma Purāṇa*: ‘That God, the Supreme Person, beyond the *guṇas*, having become many, again becomes one and lies down — the faultless Hari, the original creator.’ That one and the same is both part and whole and has opposing potencies — as [in the *Bhāgavatam* 10.40.7]: ‘Those absorbed in You worship You, who have many forms and one form.’ And in the *Kūrma Purāṇa*: ‘He is neither large nor small, yet in every way large and small; He is said to be colorless in every way, yet He is dark, with eyes reddish at the corners. Because He possesses majesty, Bhagavān is said to have opposing attributes. Yet faults are in no way to be ascribed to the Supreme; these attributes, though opposed, are to be reconciled in Him in every respect.’ In the sixth canto — on His inconceivable potency that unites opposites — in prose (*Bhāgavatam* 6.9.34–37): ‘This play of Yours seems hard to understand: without needing support, without a body, without waiting for our help, You Yourself, remaining unchanged — beyond the *guṇas* — create, destroy and maintain this world endowed with the *guṇas*. So do You, like a Devadatta here, fallen into the stream of the *guṇas*, out of dependence accept the fruits of Your good and bad deeds? Or, delighting in Yourself, tranquil, seeing all equally, do You remain indifferent? Truly, we do not know. For in You — Bhagavān, the Lord of countless excellences, whose greatness is unfathomable — the two do not contradict each other. You are beyond the reach of the disputes of disputants whose hearts and intentions are shaped by recent fancies, doubts, deliberations, false proofs and scriptures of bad logic, and who cling stubbornly to their views. In You, the One, in whom all that is made of *māyā* has ceased — when Your own *māyā* is set in between — what thing could be hard to accomplish? You have no two natures; yet You follow the opinions of people of even and uneven minds, as a piece of rope [seems] a snake and the like to those who think so.’

“Here are the *kārikās*: ‘Without bodily effort, without the support of earth and the like, without helpers — the work of You, the changeless, is very hard to understand. By “the stream of the *guṇas*” the battles of the gods and *asuras* and so on are meant. “Fallen” means attached to it; that would be dependence — for Your dependence on the gods who have taken shelter of You is born of mercy. Hence: “Your own deeds,” that is, the good and the other, done by Your own [gods], in the form of happiness, suffering and so on — do You accept these as Your fruit? Or, delighting in Yourself, do You remain wholly indifferent to them? We do not know. But in You the two are not contradictory at all. The reason is given by the two words beginning with *bhagavati*, and likewise by the five words beginning with *īśvare*. By the word “Bhagavān” omniscience is clearly indicated, by another word the possession of good qualities, and by the word *kevala* the nature of Brahman. Though by the nature of Brahman there would be indifference everywhere, the first two qualities give partiality toward the devotees. “But how can one nature be twofold at one and the same time?” To this he says, “recent …” and so on — for You are beyond the disputes of such disputants, beyond their range. Therefore, when that inconceivable potency of Your own is set in between, what thing, even a contradictory one, could be hard to accomplish here? For such indeed is its inconceivability. And that it is inconceivable is understood from its being the support of many opposing actions. The author of the *Brahma-sūtra* says: “Because of *śruti*, because the basis is the word.” And the *Skanda Purāṇa*: “What is inconceivable should not be subjected to reasoning.” This is seen even in gems and the like. Without such potency the Supreme Lord's supreme lordship would not be established, for His greatness is spoken of as unfathomable. Ignorance or sorcery (*indra-jāla*) can be seen anywhere; therefore the Lord's supreme lordship is not established by them. That these are not in Him is clearly stated by the words “in whom has ceased ….” Otherwise the six words beginning with *bhagavati* would have been used to no purpose. Therefore, by scripture and by reason, the two do not contradict each other. Yet according to the opinions of people of high and low minds, who do not know the truth, You appear now this way, now that, like the rope. “But surely knowledge alone is Brahman, while Bhagavān has many attributes; so here too two natures are seen.” To this he says, “… no two natures …” — for there is never duality in His nature: these are certainly two attributes of the One. Therefore, if contradiction is seen in the manifestations (*vilāsas*) of His potency, that is His inconceivable majesty: it adorns Him and is no fault. The same statement of contradiction is found in the third canto too (*Bhāgavatam* 3.4.16): “You are free from desire, yet You act; You are unborn, yet You take birth; You are Time itself, yet in fear of an enemy You take refuge in a fortress and flee; You delight in Yourself, yet You live as a householder among many women. Here the intelligence of the wise is bewildered.” If all this were not real, the intelligence of the wise would not be bewildered. Thus the cause in the pastimes is the inconceivable potency alone: as His will is, so it manifests.’”

Here the following objection may be raised. In the *Mahā-varāha Purāṇa* we hear: “All the bodies of that Supreme Self, Hari, are eternal, and all of them appear in the world again and again. These bodies are neither given up nor taken on, and so they are never products of *prakṛti*. All these bodies are condensed supreme bliss, by nature of the single taste of spirit; they are endowed with every spiritual quality and free from every fault.” And the *Nārada-pañcarātra* says: “As a *vaidūrya* gem takes on blue, yellow and other colors in different places, so Bhagavān Acyuta, according to the difference of worship, manifests His form in different ways.” Why, then, do you explain a gradation among these avatāras? In answer to this doubt it may be said: by the power of His inconceivable, unlimited potency, in one vessel — in one and the same Puruṣottama — oneness and separateness, being a part and being the whole, are in no way impossible or incongruous. Separate manifestation within oneness — as in the Tenth Canto (the words of Nārada): “How wonderful! One and the same Śrī Kṛṣṇa at one and the same time married sixteen thousand women in separate palaces.” Oneness of form within separateness — as in the *Padma Purāṇa*: “That faultless original creator, beyond the *guṇas*, Puruṣottama, the God Hari, having taken many forms, again lies down in one form.” That one and the same is both part and whole and has opposing potencies — as in the Tenth Canto: “You, of many forms, are one; the devotees whose minds are absorbed in You worship You.” And the *Kūrma Purāṇa* says: “Bhagavān is in every respect not large, yet large; not small, yet small; colorless, yet dark, with eyes reddish at the corners. By the power of His majesty He is called the possessor of opposing forms. Yet faults are in no way to be ascribed to the Supreme Lord; on the contrary, all these mutually opposed qualities are to be reconciled in Him in every respect.” And the prose of the Sixth Canto speaks of His inconceivable potency that unites opposites: “O Bhagavān, Your transcendental play, or sport, seems hard to understand; that is, the ordinary relation of cause and effect is not seen in You. For You need no support, make no bodily effort, are Yourself beyond the *guṇas* and do not wait for our help — and by Your own nature alone You create, maintain and destroy this world endowed with the *guṇas*, without undergoing any change at all. O Lord, do You, like an ordinary man named Devadatta, fallen into the stream of the *guṇas* in this world, out of dependence regard as Your own the fruits of action — happiness, suffering and the like? Or, without losing Your *cit-śakti*, delighting in Yourself and at peace, do You remain indifferent to all this, that is, abide only as a witness? This we do not know. You are full of the six opulences, Your excellences cannot be counted to the end, You are the Lord, the ruler of all, and Your greatness cannot become the object of anyone's intellect. There are disputants whose intelligence is covered and scattered by fancies that claim to reveal the nature of things, by doubts, deliberations, false proofs and scriptures full of bad logic; their disputes cannot touch You. In You, the possessor of inconceivable potency, both of these qualities are not opposed to each other. You are beyond all material knowledge and consist of pure knowledge alone; when Your potency of will is set in between, what work could be hard to accomplish in You? The impersonal and the personal, or that which has spiritual qualities and that which is without qualities, are not two different natures of Yours: they are only two perceptions of Your one nature, according to the difference of conceptions. But just as, for those whose minds are occupied with a snake and the like, one piece of rope appears in different forms — as a snake and so on — so You follow the intentions of those whose minds are even and uneven, that is, unsteady — that is, You manifest different doctrines among them.”

Here is the *kārikā* on this passage: “Without bodily effort, without the support of earth and the like, without helpers — the stick, the wheel and so on — Your work, free from change, is very hard to understand. By ‘the stream of the *guṇas*’ the battles of the gods and *asuras* and the like are meant. ‘Fallen’ means attached; this is what is called *pāratantrya*, that is, dependence. For Your dependence on the gods who have taken shelter of You is born of mercy (that is, Your independence does not suffer by it). Therefore — do You regard as Your own ‘Your own deeds,’ that is, ‘the deeds of Your own people’ — the good and bad fruits in the form of happiness, suffering and the like earned by Your gods? Or, delighting in Yourself, do You remain wholly indifferent to them? This we do not know. But in You (the possessor of opposing qualities) both are possible. The reason is the two epithets beginning with *bhagavati* and the five epithets beginning with *īśvare*. Of these, by the word *bhagavat* omniscience is clearly understood, by the epithet *aparigaṇita* and the others the possession of good qualities, and by the word *kevala* the nature of Brahman. Though by the nature of Brahman one might expect indifference everywhere, the two qualities beginning with *bhagavati* make partiality toward the devotees possible. If you say, ‘How can one nature be twofold at the same time?’ — in answer to this doubt it is said, ‘*arvācīna* …’ and so on; that is, You are beyond the disputes of those disputants who cannot grasp the nature of the Truth — You are out of their reach. Therefore, when the inconceivable potency of Your own is set in between, what work, even a contradictory one, could be hard to accomplish in You? Your nature is inconceivable to disputants who lack devotion; Your potency is likewise inconceivable. Only by seeing that it is the support of many opposing actions can one infer that this potency of Yours is inconceivable. The author of the *Brahma-sūtra* has said: ‘The inconceivable object of service is within the reach of *śruti* alone, that is, of the evidence of the word.’ And the *Skanda Purāṇa* says: ‘One should not build inferences about the inconceivable.’ Such inconceivable power can be noticed even in material gems, potent herbs and the like. Without such inconceivable potency the Supreme Lord's supreme lordship could not be established. It is by the power of this inconceivable potency that the Lord's greatness is glorified as unfathomable. Ignorance and the art of sorcery can be seen anywhere; therefore ignorance, sorcery and the like do not prove the Supreme Lord's supreme lordship. For the epithet *uparata* and the others show that neither is present in the Lord. If ignorance and sorcery were accepted in the Lord, the use of the six epithets beginning with *bhagavati* would become pointless. Therefore, by the scripture and reasoning that reveal the inconceivable potency, these two qualities — that He protects the universe and that He is indifferent to it — cannot contradict each other. Just as for those whose minds, through ignorance, are occupied with a snake and the like, a piece of rope appears as a snake and so on, so You, for those whose thoughts are occupied with various conceptions and who therefore lack true knowledge, appear in this or that way according to their opinions. If you say, ‘Knowledge alone is called Brahman, and that which bears many attributes is called “Bhagavān”; so here two different natures are seen,’ — to remove this doubt it is said, ‘for there are not two natures.’ By this no duality at all is asserted of His nature; only two attributes of one and the same nature are determined. Therefore, if contradiction is seen in the manifestations of His potency, that is called inconceivable majesty: it adorns Him and is no fault at all. Such contradiction is mentioned in the Third Canto too: ‘Action in Him who is free from material endeavor; birth in the Unborn; He who is Time itself takes refuge in a fortress out of fear of the enemy and flees from Mathurā; He who delights in Himself enjoys with sixteen thousand women — in all this even the intelligence of the knowers of truth is bewildered.’ If all these deeds were not real, the intelligence of the knowers of truth would never be bewildered. Thus the cause of the pastimes is Bhagavān's inconceivable potency: as His will is, such are the pastimes it manifests.”

The *Pañcarātra* scripture is a book that unfolds the Vedas; it is wholly approved by the Vedas and devoted to the section on worship (*upāsanā-kāṇḍa*). It is not a *rājasic* or *tāmasic* *tantra*: the wise know it as the “*Sātvata-saṁhitā*.” Śrī Nārāyaṇa Himself spoke it; this is specially stated in the *Mahābhārata*, in the *Mokṣa-dharma-parva* of the *Śānti-parva*, Chapters 3–9, verse 68. It was propagated by divine sages (*divya-sūris*) — Śrī Nārada and others — free from the four defects beginning with *bhrama*. The book *Śrī Bhāgavatam* is also known as the “*Sātvata-saṁhitā*.” Śaṅkara puts forward as a *pūrvapakṣa*, as if it were the *Pañcarātra* doctrine, what is wholly opposed and contrary to that doctrine, and then tries to refute it. This is merely an utter violation of logic and truth, and below it is shown briefly by way of refutation.

(1) In his commentary on *sūtra* 42 Śrīpāda Śaṅkara called Saṅkarṣaṇa “the *jīva*.” In fact the Bhāgavatas have never called Saṅkarṣaṇa “the *jīva*.” He Himself is Adhokṣaja, Acyuta, *viṣṇu-tattva*, the presiding Lord of the *jīva*, the complete whole (*aṁśī*), all-pervading consciousness (*vibhu-caitanya*), the cause of all material and transcendental creation — not the *jīva*, a part, a minute consciousness (*aṇu-caitanya*). The *jīvātmā* has neither birth nor death — this is accepted by the Bhāgavatas and by any follower of *śruti* who studies and hears the scriptures.

(2) In reply to the commentary on *sūtra* 43: the manifestation of all the other *viṣṇu-tattvas* from *mūla-Saṅkarṣaṇa* is described in the *Brahma-saṁhitā*: “As the flame of a lamp, passing to another [wick], itself shines as a lamp, with the same qualities though its cause is separate, so He who in just this way shines forth as Viṣṇu — Govinda, the original Puruṣa — Him I worship.” That is: “As the ray of a lamp on another base acts as a separate lamp — having the same qualities as the first lamp — so the original Puruṣa Govinda manifests Himself by becoming Viṣṇu. Him I worship.”

(3) In the commentary on *sūtra* 44 Śrīpāda puts forward the *pūrvapakṣa*: “They are different from one another and not of one self.” The followers of the *Pañcarātra* never accept this as their doctrine. In his commentary on *sūtra* 42, as quoted above, Śrīpāda Śaṅkara himself accepted: “that He by Himself, dividing Himself in many ways, abides in the form of *vyūhas* — this is not refuted,” that is, “that He by Himself abides in the form of many *vyūhas* we too accept as agreeing with *śruti*.” This contradicts the *pūrvapakṣa* objection in this *sūtra*. In other words, his commentaries on *sūtras* 44 and 42 contradict each other: what he accepted before, he now tries to refute as a *pūrvapakṣa*. By accepting the *catur-vyūha* of Nārāyaṇa the Bhāgavatas do not thereby accept “a doctrine of many Lords.” They know the Truth as Bhagavān, nondual Knowledge, and never preach a plurality of Gods contrary to the Vedas. They firmly believe in the inconceivable great potency of Śrī Nārāyaṇa. See the paraphrase of the *Laghu-bhāgavatāmṛta* given above. Among these four *tattvas* — Vāsudeva, Saṅkarṣaṇa, Pradyumna and Aniruddha — there is no relation of cause and effect: “there is nothing else beyond the existent and the nonexistent”; “this difference between body and the possessor of the body never exists in the Lord” (*Kūrma Purāṇa*). All of Them are the *tattva* that rules over *māyā*, the presiding Lords of *śuddha-sattva*, *turīya*; in Their manifestation there can be no power of *māyā*, no change, no transformation, no fragmentation. They are the one nondual Knowledge, Adhokṣaja, the complete Reality. The evidence of *śruti*: “Oṁ. That is full, this is full; from the full the full comes forth. When the full is taken from the full, the full remains.” (*Bṛhad-āraṇyaka Upaniṣad* 5.1.) The whole world, from Brahmā down to a blade of grass, is the gross, external manifestation of Bhagavān Viṣṇu. Those who teach the merging of spirit and matter (*cid-acit-samanvaya*) vainly try to identify it with, or make it equal to, the Śrī *catur-vyūha*, the Lord of the three potencies. This is mere atheism rooted in extreme enmity toward Bhagavān. The world from Brahmā down to a blade of grass, or the universal form of Viṣṇu, is His external splendor, the *eka-pāda-vibhūti*; it is connected with *māyā*, or *prakṛti*, and is therefore material. To think the *catur-vyūha*, the Lord of spirit and matter, equal to it, or to strive to make it so, is the trait of the *māyāvādīs*.

(4) In reply to the commentary on *sūtra* 45 — a paraphrase of the words quoted in the *Laghu-bhāgavatāmṛta* in describing the transcendence of Bhagavān's qualities (97–99): “If you say, ‘Every quality is a product of *prakṛti* and therefore like a mirage; what wonder, then, that they cannot be counted?’ — you have no right to say so. Bhagavān's qualities can never be material: all His qualities constitute His very nature, and so all of them are certainly, by nature, bliss. As in the *Brahma-tarka*: ‘Bhagavān Hari possesses qualities that constitute His own nature; therefore the qualities of Viṣṇu and of liberated *jīvas* are never separate from their nature.’ In the *Śrī Viṣṇu Purāṇa*: ‘May Hari be gracious — the supreme, utterly pure, original Puruṣa, the Supreme Lord untouched by the material qualities such as *sattva*.’ And in that same *Viṣṇu Purāṇa*: ‘Knowledge, power, strength, sovereignty, valor and splendor in their fullness — without the *heya*, that is, material, qualities — are what the word *bhagavat* denotes.’ And in the *Padma Purāṇa*: ‘That the Supreme Lord is glorified in scripture as *nirguṇa* (without qualities) means only that He has no *heya*, that is, material, qualities.’ And in the first chapter of the first canto: ‘O Dharma, the qualities I have listed, and many other great qualities, abide eternally in Śrī Kṛṣṇa; the qualities sought by those who desire greatness never leave Śrī Kṛṣṇa.’[^41-1] Thus this Śrī Kṛṣṇa possesses countless transcendental qualities and boundless potency, and His form is a mass of complete bliss.” See *Bhāgavatam* 3.26.21, 25, 27, 28.

A paraphrase of how Śrī Rāmānujapāda refuted Śaṅkara's arguments in his *Śrī-bhāṣya*:

Śrī Śaṅkara rejected certain parts even of the *Pañcarātra* scripture, spoken by Bhagavān and leading to the highest good: he suspected that, like the scriptures of Kapila and others, they contradict *śruti* and are therefore not authoritative. The *Pañcarātra* scripture says: “From Vāsudeva, the supreme cause, whose nature is Brahman, was born the *jīva* named ‘Saṅkarṣaṇa’; from Saṅkarṣaṇa, the mind named ‘Pradyumna’; and from the mind, the *ahaṅkāra* named ‘Aniruddha.’” [The objection is:] one cannot speak here of the birth of the *jīva*, for that contradicts *śruti*. With the words “The spiritual *jīvātmā* is never born and never dies” (*Kaṭha Upaniṣad* 2.18), all the *śrutis* speak of the *jīva* as beginningless, that is, as unborn. Therefore what is meant here is the appearance of the presiding deities of the *jīva*, the mind and the *ahaṅkāra* (*sūtra* 42).

It is said that from Saṅkarṣaṇa the mind named Pradyumna is born. Here too it is impossible for the instrument, the mind, to be born from the agent, the *jīva*, for *śruti* says that “the vital breath, the mind and all the senses are born from the Paramātmā.” If, then, the mind is said to be born from the *jīva*, this contradicts such words of *śruti* as “they are born from the Paramātmā.” Therefore, since this statement establishes a meaning contrary to *śruti*, its authority is rejected (*sūtra* 43).

[The answer is:] Saṅkarṣaṇa, Pradyumna and Aniruddha have the nature of the Supreme Brahman, and therefore the authority of the scripture that teaches this can never be rejected. That is, these *vyūhas* — Saṅkarṣaṇa and the others — are by no means meant to be like ordinary *jīvas*, liable to fall under the sway of *māyā*: all of Them are Lords, all possess the six opulences — knowledge, sovereignty, power, strength, valor, splendor and the rest. Therefore the *Pañcarātra* doctrine is not without authority. Only those unversed in the *Pañcarātra*, or the Bhāgavata doctrine, could say such unscriptural things as “it speaks of the birth of the *jīva*, and that is a contradiction.” The Bhāgavata doctrine is this. He who loves the devotees who have taken shelter of Him, and who is called the Supreme Brahman named Vāsudeva, by His own will abides in four ways, so that those who seek His shelter may approach Him. Thus the *Pauṣkara-saṁhitā* says: “The scripture in which *brāhmaṇas*, as is their duty, worship the *cāturātmya* (the *catur-vyūha*), calling It by names handed down in succession — that is the ‘*āgama*.’” And the *Sātvata-saṁhitā* says that the worship of this *cāturātmya* is worship of the Supreme Brahman named Vāsudeva. The Supreme Brahman named Vāsudeva, whose body is the fullness of the six opulences, is divided [into forms] as *sūkṣma*, *vyūha* and *vibhava*. The devotees, each according to his qualification (*adhikāra*), worship Him by acts informed by knowledge, and so fully attain Him. By worshipping the *vibhavas* — Nṛsiṁha, Raghunātha, or the avatāras Matsya, Kūrma and others — one attains the *vyūhas*, Saṅkarṣaṇa and the others; and by worshipping the *vyūhas* one attains the Supreme Brahman named Vāsudeva. For the *Pauṣkara-saṁhitā* says: “By this scripture, through acts informed by knowledge, one attains the unmanifest Supreme Brahman named Vāsudeva.” Hence it is established that Saṅkarṣaṇa and the others too are the Supreme Brahman, for They too have forms by Their own will. The *śrutis* declare: “He, without being born as material things are born, descends, that is, appears, in many forms.” Out of love for those who have taken shelter of Him He accepts form by His own will, and so the authority of the *Pañcarātra* scripture, which teaches this, is not rejected. In this scripture Saṅkarṣaṇa, Pradyumna and Aniruddha are the presiding deities of, respectively, the *jīva*, the mind and the *ahaṅkāra*; therefore there is no contradiction in Their being called by the words “*jīva*” and so on. In the same way Brahman is called by the words “*ākāśa*,” “*prāṇa*” and so on (*sūtra* 44).

In this scripture the birth of the *jīva* is denied. For the *Parama-saṁhitā* says: “The three *guṇas* — unconscious, serving another's purpose, always subject to change — are the field of those who perform *karma*: such is the form of *prakṛti*. The Puruṣa's connection with it is by way of pervasion, and that this connection is beginningless is also true.” Thus in all the *saṁhitās* the “*jīva*” is eternal, and therefore the *Pañcarātra* doctrine denies its birth. Whatever is born must perish: accepting the birth of the *jīva*, one would have to accept its destruction as well. But since the *jīva* is eternal, its birth is excluded of itself, by its eternity. Above, the *Parama-saṁhitā* said: “the form of *prakṛti* is always subject to change”; so birth, destruction and the like should be understood as included in this “constant change.” Thus Śaṅkarācārya's charge that Saṅkarṣaṇa and the others are born as *jīvas* has been refuted (*sūtra* 45). See Śrīdhara [Svāmī]'s commentary on *Bhāgavatam* 3.1.34.

## 58

> Subheading: The two forms of *māyā*, “*pradhāna*” and “*prakṛti*” —

**“*Pradhāna*” and “*prakṛti*”**: see Madhya 20.271. Śrī Jīva Prabhu writes in the *Paramātma-sandarbha* (section 49):

“This *māyā* has two portions. One is the efficient portion (*nimitta*): it consists of the *guṇas* and is called *māyā*. The other is the material portion (*upādāna*): it is substance and is called *pradhāna*. The difference between them is described in four verses (*Śrīmad-Bhāgavatam* 11.24.1–4). … (section 53) Elsewhere (*Śrīmad-Bhāgavatam* 10.63.26) the differences between these two, the material and the efficient, are also stated according to the difference in their functions: ‘Time, destiny, *karma*, the *jīva*, his nature, the subtle elements, the field, the vital air, the ego, the transformations, their aggregate, and the flow like seed and sprout — this is Your *māyā*. I take shelter of You, the limit of its negation.’ Here time, destiny, *karma* and nature are efficient portions; the rest are material portions. The *jīva*, who possesses them, is of a dual nature: thus a particle of the efficient potency is present within the material class as well. … (section 55) The potency well known by the name *māyā*, as the efficient portion, is seen to be threefold: knowledge, desire and action. … Now the characteristic of *pradhāna*, the material portion (*Śrīmad-Bhāgavatam* 3.26.10): ‘That which is made of the three *guṇas*, unmanifest, eternal, consisting of cause and effect, without distinctions and yet containing distinctions, they call *pradhāna* and *prakṛti*.’ That which is made of the three *guṇas* — the combination of the three *guṇas*, *sattva* and the others — is what they call the unmanifest, *pradhāna* and *prakṛti*. Why is it called unmanifest? It is ‘without distinctions’: the three *guṇas* are in balance in it, so its distinctions are not manifest; hence one understands why it is also called *avyākṛta*, the undeveloped. Why is it called *pradhāna*? It ‘contains distinctions’: the *mahat* and the other distinctions are effects of its own portions, and it is their shelter and therefore superior (*śreṣṭha*) to them. … The efficient portion is *māyā*; the material portion is *pradhāna*.”

Bhagavān's external potency, *māyā*, has two portions: the efficient portion, “*māyā* made of the *guṇas*,” and the material portion, “*pradhāna* made of substance.” The mutual difference between these two names is described in four verses of the twenty-fourth chapter of the eleventh canto of the *Bhāgavatam*. Elsewhere, in the twenty-sixth verse of the sixty-third chapter of the tenth canto, the two portions — material and efficient — are divided according to the difference in their functions: “O Bhagavān! Agitating ‘time,’ efficient ‘*karma*,’ ‘destiny,’ which brings forth the fruits, and its impression, ‘nature’ (*svabhāva*) — this is the conditioned *jīva*, marked by these four efficient portions of *māyā*. The subtle elements are ‘substance’; *prakṛti* is the ‘field’; the *sūtra* is the ‘vital air’; *ahaṅkāra* is the ‘self’; and the eleven senses and earth, water, fire, air and ether are the sixteen transformations; their total aggregate is the ‘body.’ From the body comes *karma* as a seed, and from *karma* the body as a sprout: this endless flow is ‘*māyā*.’ O Prabhu, You are the Truth at which all this is negated; You I worship.” “Although the *jīva* is a particle of the efficient potency, he possesses portions of both natures and so follows the material class as well.” The potency known by the word “*māyā*,” the efficient portion, has three divisions: “knowledge,” “desire” and “action.” The characteristic of “*pradhāna*,” the material portion: “That in which the three *guṇas* — *sattva*, *rajas* and *tamas* — are combined is called ‘the unmanifest,’ ‘*pradhāna*’ and ‘*prakṛti*.’ The reason for the name ‘unmanifest’ is this: it is without distinctions — the three *guṇas* are in balance in it — so its distinctive qualities are not manifest; therefore *pradhāna* also received the name *avyākṛta*. The reason for the name ‘*pradhāna*’: the *mahat-tattva* and the other distinctions are *māyā*'s own effects, and she, like a distinct object, is their shelter and is therefore superior to them.” … In the efficient portion, “*māyā*”; in the material portion, “*pradhāna*.”

## 61

> Subheading: Bhagavān is the original cause of the world —

## 59–61

See Madhya 20.259–261. The external potency, *māyā*, is known in the material portion of the world by the names “*pradhāna*” and “*prakṛti*,” and in the efficient portion of the world by the name “*māyā*.” Inert *prakṛti* is not the cause of the world. For Kṛṣṇa, as Kāraṇārṇavaśāyī Mahā-Viṣṇu, grants *prakṛti* the material, substantial potency and so infuses her with power. The example is red-hot iron. Iron has no “power” to burn or give heat, but touched by fire, red-hot iron can burn and heat other things. In the same way inert *prakṛti*, which is like iron, has no independent capacity to be the substance or the material cause. Only when the glance-potency of Kāraṇodakaśāyī, who is like fire, is infused into her does iron-like *prakṛti*, a mere likeness of the material cause, acquire the power to burn and give heat. To regard *prakṛti*, known as the material, as the material cause of the world is therefore sheer delusion. Śrī Kapiladeva also says: “As the firebrand, the sparks and the smoke are born of the fire itself and are thought of as one with it, yet the fire is distinct from the firebrand…” (*Śrīmad-Bhāgavatam* 3.28.40) Smoke, the burning log and the sparks contain the substance of fire, and so they are spoken of as one with fire; nevertheless fire is something distinct from the firebrand. “The elements,” like the smoke, “the *jīva*,” like the sparks, and “*pradhāna*,” like the firebrand — all of them receive their powers from Bhagavān, who is like the fire, the substance of everything, and so each displays its own separate identity. Even so, the substance of them all is Bhagavān. “*Pradhāna*” is held to be the material of the world, but it appears as such only through the substance Bhagavān has placed in it. “*Pradhāna*” cannot be an independent material cause separate from Bhagavān. To forget Kṛṣṇa, the root shelter of all material causes, and to ascribe that role to the *prakṛti* of Sāṅkhya is as fruitless as expecting milk from the teat-like lumps of flesh on a goat's neck.

> Subheading: By a semblance of His body He touches *māyā*, and so Bhagavān Himself is the material cause —

## 59–66

In the Vedic view, the world is created only by the joining of potency that comes from the Reality itself, and in that form it appears before the conditioned *jīva*. In the non-Vedic view, the visible world is born of *prakṛti*. The potency of the Reality works in three ways: as *cit*, as *acit*, and as a potency that partakes of both. Some, following a path foreign to the Śrutis, think that the world arose from inert *prakṛti*; the Vedas do not accept that view. The Reality, Bhagavān, is nondifferent from His spiritual potency. When the *cit-śakti* is infused into the *acit* potency, a temporary, perishable semblance of spiritual being appears. Bhagavān's *jīva-śakti*, called *taṭasthā*, combines *cit* and *acit*. It is eternally subordinate to the spiritual potency, yet from time without beginning it is liable to wander in the visible world, the transformation of the *acit* potency. Cut off from the Reality, the *jīva* misuses pure spirit, and this gradually becomes his experience of bondage. In truth, once the *jīva* knows his own *svarūpa*, he can understand that readiness to serve is the ground of his eternal highest good. When he turns away from service, this potency called *taṭasthā* imagines itself the possessor of potency and sets out to enjoy. To become masters of *acit*, his senses begin to act against the purely spiritual potency. Only Kṛṣṇa's own potency gives power to His *acit* potency, which is of a different nature (*vijātīya*). An example: fire and its power to burn, infused into iron that has no fire, display the iron as fire. In truth the *acit* potency gains its activity only from Kṛṣṇa's *cit-śakti*. The *taṭasthā jīva*, driven by the *acit* potency, thinks that the visible material world is born of *prakṛti*. But the liberated *jīva*, situated in pure spirit, can understand that the *cit-śakti* of the Possessor of potency imparts a share of strength to the *acit* potency and so makes it active. *Prakṛti*, the root cause of the *acit* potency, brings with it every kind of undesirability, limitation and inferiority. A *jīva* who follows reasoning in the conceit of his bondage sees the goat's milk-giving udder and yet vainly seeks milk from the teat-like growths on her neck. Equally foolish is it to call *prakṛti*, whose root is *acit*, the cause of the material world. Bhagavān's *acit* potency, “*māyā*,” appears before the *jīva* who is averse to Hari as the “efficient” and the “material” cause and turns him away from accepting the true Reality. When knowledge of his *svarūpa* dawns, the *jīva* notices the twofold action of the *acit* potency: “covering” (*āvaraṇī*) and “projecting” (*vikṣepātmikā*). A thing such as a pot has a twofold cause: the potter is the efficient cause, while the clay, the wheel, the rod and the like are the material cause and the instruments. In the same way, when one considers the Reality, the controller of the visible world and its elements is shown to be the Possessor of potency (*śaktimat-tattva*). If, however, one considers the potencies separately, *māyā*, made of the three *guṇas*, governs the elements — the material portion — through the *guṇas*. The *taṭasthā-śakti*, the *jīva*, turning away from Hari in this visible world, takes on the role of enjoyer. That the Reality is perceived in the visible world as *acit* is merely the fruit of aversion to Kṛṣṇa. The perception of *acit* displays enjoyment, that is, devotion to the senses. But in perceiving Bhagavān with readiness to serve, the *jīva* sees his own relationship with Him. Kṛṣṇa is the cause of the eternal spiritual world. He is also the cause of the world of *acit*, whose truth is covered, and He is the root cause and ordainer of the *jīva* called *taṭasthā*. The perception of *acit* is the work of Bhagavān's external potency, and the perception of *cit* is the work of His internal potency. From the word born of spiritual perception we can learn that every attribute of independent agency and of being the source of all is established in the Reality, Bhagavān. That Reality is the Great (*Bṛhat*), and His minute fragments are what the word “*jīva*” denotes. The Reality, Bhagavān, though divided, does not display the quality of fragmentation; and fragmented perception is never identical with undivided perception. In terms of pervaded and pervader, Brahman and the *jīva* are of the same kind. Yet the Lord is the master of *māyā*, while the dependent is subject to *māyā*. But when one who is subject to *māyā* becomes subject to the Lord of *māyā*, his subjection to *māyā* can no longer remain.

## 81

> Subheading: The avatāras are only portions —

The *Laghu-bhāgavatāmṛta*, describing the characteristics of the avatāras (section 1), says: “When the forms mentioned before appear for the work of the universe, as if for the first time, either themselves or through another medium, they are then called avatāras. That medium is either a *tad-ekātma-rūpa* or His devotee — like Śeṣaśāyī and others, or like Vasudeva and others.” That is, when the original form mentioned before, Śrī Kṛṣṇa, appears for the work of the universe either Himself or through a medium, He is called an “avatāra.” The medium is of two kinds: a *tad-ekātma-rūpa* and a devotee. Śeṣaśāyī is a *tad-ekātma-rūpa*, while Vasudeva and others are devotees. Śrī Baladeva's commentary: “‘Themselves’ means without a medium; ‘through another medium’ — if they appear in the world in either way, they are then called avatāras. Descent from what lies beyond the world into the world is indeed avatāra. Through a medium: as Garbhodakaśāyī from Śeṣaśāyī Kāraṇārṇavaśāyī, Kṛṣṇa from Vasudeva, Rāma from Daśaratha. ‘The work’: agitating *prakṛti* and producing the *mahat* and the rest; increasing the happiness of the gods and others by crushing the wicked; distributing the bliss of *prema* to *sādhakas* who long for Him by appearing before them Himself; and spreading pure devotion — such is the meaning of ‘for the work.’”

In the realm of *māyā* everything is fragmented by differences of place, time and person, and actions there are fragmented. In those actions, in their efficient or material portion, one sees the causality of Bhagavān's *svarūpa*. The Godhood in the form of Mahā-Viṣṇu, the cause of those actions, is a portion of Kṛṣṇa. It is this portion that is called an “avatāra.” Ordinarily, by gross vision and on the maxim of the lame and the blind, inert *prakṛti* is called the “material” cause and the enjoyer, the *puruṣa-jīva* made of the three *guṇas*, the “efficient” cause. But *prakṛti* is neither the “material” nor the “efficient” cause of the world; this the devotees (*bhāgavatas*) have subtly realized. By the power of His glance *prakṛti* is known as the “material” of the world, and *māyā* is famed as its “efficient agent”; both these potencies are given by Bhagavān Himself. Those manifestations of Bhagavān in which He displays the pastime of empowering *māyā* — to create the universe or for its welfare — those manifest forms are called “portions” or “avatāras.” In truth the avatāras, who are likened to lamps, are Viṣṇu. But because they exercise authority over *māyā*, in the language of *māyā* they are merely called “portions” or “avatāras.” See Madhya 20.263–264.

## 86

> Subheading: Though related to *māyā* in His glance and other acts, He is in truth beyond *māyā* —

In the *Laghu-bhāgavatāmṛta*, where Viṣṇu's freedom from the *guṇas* is described, Śrī Rūpa's *kārikā* is given: “Relation with the *guṇas* as their controller is called *yoga*. Therefore He who is the Supreme's own portion is not bound up with them.” That is, Viṣṇu's relation with the *guṇas* as their controller is called *yoga*. That Puruṣa, therefore, is never bound by the *guṇas*. Still less is any of the Viṣṇus — His own portions, in truth nondifferent from the Supreme Person — ever in any way joined with the *guṇas*. Śrī Baladeva's commentary: “But how can the Supreme Person have a relation with the *guṇas*? That contradicts such statements as ‘*māyā*, abashed, withdraws from His sight’ (*Śrīmad-Bhāgavatam* 2.7.47). To this the word *yoga* replies. The *guṇas* are the controlled, and the Puruṣa who appears in three ways is the controller: such is the relation, and here it is called *yoga*, not bondage. And He is not joined with them, for in the words of Drumila, lord of *yogīs* (*Śrīmad-Bhāgavatam* 11.4.5), no relation with the *guṇas* is mentioned.”

“If you say: is not a relation of Mahā-Viṣṇu with the *guṇas* established? For then it would contradict the words ‘*māyā*, abashed, stands turned away from Bhagavān.’ The answer is this: the word ‘*guṇa*’ means ‘the controlled.’ The Puruṣa who appears in three forms — Viṣṇu, Brahmā and Śiva — is related to *prakṛti* as her controller. In the world this is called *yoga*; it is never denoted by the word ‘bondage’ by those three *guṇas*. That Viṣṇu is never joined with the *guṇas*, for in the words of Drumila, one of the nine Yogendras, no relation of the three *guṇas* with Viṣṇu is mentioned at all.”

With both kinds of cause — material and efficient — He is related as the one who glances; yet *māyā* can in no way overpower Him. The world with all its transformations is a transformation of Bhagavān's potency of desire (*icchā-śakti*), but in Him no material transformation is possible. See Ādi 2.52, 54.

> Subheading: (*Śrīmad-Bhāgavatam*, Canto 1, Chapter 11, verse 38)

## 88

> Subheading: The Lord (*Īśvara*) possesses inconceivable potency —

## 89

> Subheading: The world's relation with the Lord — difference and nondifference —

Without Bhagavān's existence the visible world can have no foundation at all. The world rests in Bhagavān; but one must not therefore take the external perception — the vision of *acit*, full of enjoyment — to be Bhagavān, nor regard the world of enjoyment as Godhood. Enjoyment, that is, aversion to Bhagavān — in other words, *māyā* — does not rest in Bhagavān: in the Reality, Bhagavān, aversion to Bhagavān can have no place. Bhagavān Adhokṣaja descends into the world, into the phenomenal realm (*prapañca*), and yet He does not become, and cannot become, a fragmented and perishable thing of this world. In both His manifest and unmanifest pastimes He is eternally beyond *māyā* and eternally her master — that is, His nature as the *nirguṇa* Vaikuṇṭha is eternally present. Through various pastimes He descends into the world and remains the original presiding deity of everything that exists in the world.

Nor can the world exist as a second reality, with a being separate from Him. Viṣṇu Himself never comes into contact with the material world or with *māyā*. Neither His own *svarūpa* nor His opulence of the same nature (*tad-rūpa-vaibhava*) belongs in the least to the measurable world of enjoyment or to *prakṛti*, which is turned away from Him. This is the independent sovereignty and the Godhood of Bhagavān, who acts by His own will and is full of inconceivable majesty.

See the *Gauḍīya-bhāṣya*, with its various commentaries, on the verse “*yathā mahānti*” (34), one of the *catuḥ-ślokī* in the ninth chapter of the second canto of *Śrīmad-Bhāgavatam*, and also *Śrīmad-Bhāgavatam* 11.15.36.

The *Laghu-bhāgavatāmṛta*, describing the three Puruṣas (sections 137–139), says: “The four-armed *vilāsa* of this Garbhodaśāyī enters the lotus of the worlds and, called Viṣṇu, lies in the Ocean of Milk. As the indwelling Lord, this Viṣṇu abides in the hearts of all living beings, from the gods down to the unmoving, as if in many forms. The form of Viṣṇu called in the *Sātvata-tantra* “the third Puruṣa, who abides in all beings” is the *vilāsa-mūrti* of Garbhodaśāyī Viṣṇu.

The *Laghu-bhāgavatāmṛta*, describing the Puruṣa (section 12), gives Śrī Baladeva's commentary: “Viṣṇu is not joined even with *sattva*; by His will alone He merely controls it. That is why it is said: ‘The highest good comes from Him.’ So the *Vāmana Purāṇa* says: ‘Brahmā, Viṣṇu and Īśa are the three forms of the great-souled Viṣṇu. In Brahmā He is in the form of Brahmā, in Śiva in the form of Śiva, but the god Janārdana, in the form of Viṣṇu, remains apart.’” Viṣṇu is the presiding deity of the *guṇa* of *sattva*, yet He is never bound by *sattva-guṇa*: by His will alone He merely controls it. That is why it is said, “From Him alone comes the welfare of the *jīva*.” So the *Vāmana Purāṇa* says that one and the same Viṣṇu exists in the forms of Brahmā, Viṣṇu and Śiva: in Brahmā He is in the form of Brahmā, in Śiva in the form of Śiva, and Janārdana in the form of Viṣṇu remains separate from them both.

And where Viṣṇu is described (sections 29–30): “Viṣṇu extends (*tanoti*) *sattva*, and so in the scriptures He is known as *sattva-tanu*. His avatāras, likewise, are *sattva-tanu*. Or else *sattva* is His ‘body’ (*tanu*) in the sense of an external seat. That He is *nirguṇa*, beyond the *guṇas*, is therefore fully established in all the scriptures. So it is said (*Śrīmad-Bhāgavatam* 10.88.5): ‘Hari is indeed *nirguṇa*, the Puruṣa Himself, beyond *prakṛti*.’”

He extends *sattva-guṇa*, and so in the scriptures Viṣṇu has received the name *sattva-tanu*. The avatāras of Kṣīrābdhiśāyī Viṣṇu are likewise called *sattva-tanu*; or He is called *sattva-tanu* because that body, *sattva*, is merely His external seat. That is why all the scriptures call Viṣṇu *nirguṇa*. So too in the tenth canto: “Hari is *nirguṇa*, the Supreme Lord Himself, beyond *prakṛti*, the giver of knowledge to Brahmā and the other gods, the witness of all; whoever worships Him attains freedom from the *guṇas*.” This is why the verse of the *Bhāgavatam* says: “From this *sattva-tanu* comes every kind of good.”

## 120

> Subheading: Viṣṇu in the form of Śeṣa, the devotee of Kṛṣṇa —

Śrī Jīva Prabhu writes in the *Śrī Kṛṣṇa-sandarbha* (section 86): “‘Ananta, the *kalā* of Vāsudeva, thousand-mouthed, self-effulgent (*svarāṭ*), the god, will appear first, wishing to please Hari.’[^120-1] The *kalā* of Vāsudeva, the son of Śrī Vasudeva, is His first portion, Śrī Saṅkarṣaṇa. *Svarāṭ*: He who shines by His own [glory] alone. Therefore He is Ananta, unlimited by time or place; He, called Śeṣa, is also the thousand-mouthed one. By one portion of Himself He is the one called Śeṣa. In the *Skanda Purāṇa*, in the *Ayodhyā-māhātmya*: ‘Then, as all looked on, Śakra spoke sweetly to Lakṣmaṇa, true to his word, who had become Śeṣa: “Attain Your own eternal supreme abode of Viṣṇu. Your form has come too — Śeṣa with his shining hoods.” Having said this to Lakṣmaṇa, the king of the gods, together with the gods, sent Śeṣa, who is able to bear the burden of the earth, to Pātāla.’ Therefore both in the words ‘My abode called Śeṣa’ (*Śrīmad-Bhāgavatam* 10.2.8) and here, in the words ‘remains, called Śeṣa’ (*Śrīmad-Bhāgavatam* 10.3.25), He is called an unfailing portion. Or else [Śeṣa is He] from whom the name and fame of Śeṣa come.”

The *kalā* of Bhagavān (the portion of His portion) is Śrī Anantadeva, thousand-mouthed and *svarāṭ*. Wishing to do what pleases Śrī Hari, He is always present before Him. The first portion of Vāsudeva, the son of Vasudeva, is Saṅkarṣaṇa. He is self-manifest and is therefore *svarāṭ*; and so He is Ananta, that is, free from the limits of time and place. He is present also in the form of the thousand-mouthed “Śeṣa.” “By one portion” means in the form of the avatāra called Śeṣa. In the *Skanda Purāṇa*, in the *Ayodhyā-māhātmya*: “As all looked on, Indra, the king of the gods, began to speak to Lakṣmaṇa, true to his vow, who bore the form of Śeṣa: ‘Go to Your own eternal abode of Viṣṇu — Your form, Śeṣa adorned with hoods, has come as well.’ Having said this, the king of the gods sent Lakṣmaṇa in the form of Śeṣa, able to bear the burden of the earth, to Pātāla and went to the abode of the gods.” (That is, when Lakṣmaṇa, the *vyūha* Saṅkarṣaṇa, descended with Śrī Rāma, Śeṣa, the bearer of the earth who lives in Pātāla, came and merged with him. Later, when the time of disappearance came, “Śeṣa” separated from Lakṣmaṇa and went to his own abode, Pātāla, and Lakṣmaṇa went to Vaikuṇṭha, the abode of Viṣṇu.) Therefore in the words “My abode called Śeṣa” too, “Śeṣa” means that by which the end (*śeṣa*) is reached — that which remains at the very end. Just as the remainder is nondifferent from the original thing, so Śeṣa is spoken of as a nondifferent portion of Vāsudeva. Or else “Śeṣa” is He from whom comes His fame by the name of Śeṣa.

The *Laghu-bhāgavatāmṛta*, describing the truth of Rudra (section 19), gives Śrī Baladeva's commentary: “‘Like Śeṣa’: Śeṣa who is the bed of Śārṅgī (Viṣṇu), His supporting potency, belongs to the category of the Lord; but the Śeṣa who bears the earth is a *jīva* whom He has entered.” That is, Śeṣa in the form of the bed of Viṣṇu, the wielder of the bow Śārṅga — the supporting potency — belongs to the category of the Lord, while the earth-bearing “Śeṣa” belongs to the category of empowered (*śaktyāviṣṭa*) *jīvas*. And again, where the truth of Śrī Rāma is described (section 28): “Saṅkarṣaṇa, the second *vyūha*, is Rāma Himself. Uniting with Śeṣa, the bearer of the earth, He became manifest. Śeṣa is twofold: one bears the earth, the other is the bed of Śārṅgī. Of them, the one who bears the earth is considered Saṅkarṣaṇa, because Saṅkarṣaṇa has entered him. But the one who is the bed thinks of Himself as His friend and servant.” That is, Saṅkarṣaṇa of the second *catur-vyūha*, uniting with the earth-bearing “Śeṣa,” descended in the form of Rāma. “Śeṣa” is twofold: the bearer of the earth and Bhagavān's bed. The earth-bearing “Śeṣa” is an *āveśa-avatāra* of Saṅkarṣaṇa; that is why he too is called “Saṅkarṣaṇa.” The one who is the bed thinks of Himself as the servant and friend of the wielder of Śārṅga.

And so the *Brahmāṇḍa Purāṇa* says: “He who is four-armed in Vaikuṇṭha, who is the Lord of Śvetadvīpa, who is Nārāyaṇa, the friend of Nara — He is Puruṣottama, the son of Nanda. For the original Saṅkarṣaṇa abides in Śrī Kṛṣṇa, and from Him Badarīnātha and other forms are manifested (that is, they appear by His mere wish). The sages saw only the pastimes of one or another of these forms. They followed the accounts of Viṣṇu with those different pastimes, and so they called Śrī Kṛṣṇa by the names of those forms of Viṣṇu. Therefore, even if the original source of the avatāras is called an “avatāra,” in truth there is no fault in it. See Ādi 2.110–115.

> Subheading: Though Advaita Prabhu is an avatāra of Mahā-Viṣṇu, He considers Himself a servant of Gaura —

Mahāprabhu accepted Śrī Īśvara Purī as His *dīkṣā-guru*, and so Advaita Prabhu was a godbrother of Mahāprabhu's guru and worthy of respect.

## 201–202

For one who longs to serve Śrī Rādhā-Govinda, the one desirable goal of life is to gain the shelter and grace of Śrī Svarūpa, Rūpa, Sanātana, and Raghunātha Gosvāmī Prabhus. ## 203

Śrī Sanātana Gosvāmī Prabhu is the ācārya of the conclusions of *bhakti* (*bhakti-siddhānta-ācārya*). At the end of the fourth chapter of the Antya-līlā of this book, the author writes: “Sanātana wrote the book *Bhāgavatāmṛta*, from which we learn the truth about the devotee, devotion, and Kṛṣṇa. He wrote the *Daśama-ṭippanī*, a book that is the essence of conclusions, from which we learn Kṛṣṇa's pastimes, *rasa*, and *prema*. He wrote the book *Hari-bhakti-vilāsa* on Vaiṣṇava conduct, in which we find the full extent of a Vaiṣṇava's duties.”[^203-1] Śrī Raghunātha dāsa Gosvāmīpāda writes of Śrī Sanātana in his prayer *Vilāpa-kusumāñjali*: “I was blind and had no wish to drink, yet with great effort he made me drink the nectar of devotion joined with renunciation. He is an ocean of mercy, and he suffers at the suffering of others. I take shelter of that master, Sanātana.”[^203-2] Śrī Kavirāja Gosvāmī (Antya 4.236) first names Śrī Rūpa, Śrī Sanātana, and Śrī Jīva, and then writes: “These three gurus and Raghunātha dāsa — I bow to the feet of them all, for I am their servant.” Śrī Raghunātha dāsa, too, calls Śrī Sanātana Prabhu the ācārya of the conclusions of *bhakti*.

Śrī Rūpa Gosvāmī Prabhu is the ācārya of *bhakti-rasa* (*bhakti-rasa-ācārya*). (Antya 4.224): “Rūpa Gosāñi wrote the *Rasāmṛta-sindhu*, the essence, in which we find the full expanse of the *rasa* of devotion to Kṛṣṇa; and another book named *Ujjvala-nīlamaṇi*, in which we find the full extent of the *rasa* of Rādhā and Kṛṣṇa's pastimes.”[^203-3]

## 223

Ādi 4.147: “Kṛṣṇa's sweetness has a natural power: it stirs all men and women, and Kṛṣṇa Himself first among them.”

In Śrī Rūpa Prabhu's *Laghu-bhāgavatāmṛta*, where the supremacy of Kṛṣṇa's sweetness is described (sections 351–352), a story from the *Padma Purāṇa* is retold: “Lakṣmī-devī saw the sweetness of Śrī Kṛṣṇa, longed for it, and began to perform austerities. Śrī Kṛṣṇa asked her, ‘Why are you performing austerities?’ Lakṣmī replied, ‘I wish to enjoy pastimes with You in Vṛndāvana in the form of a *gopī*.’ Śrī Kṛṣṇa said, ‘That is very hard to attain.’ Lakṣmī spoke again: ‘O Lord, then I wish to stay on Your chest like a golden line.’ And Śrī Kṛṣṇa said, ‘So be it.’ Since then Lakṣmī has remained on Kṛṣṇa's chest as a golden line.” In the *Śrīmad-Bhāgavatam* (10.16.36) the wives of the serpent say: “Lakṣmī is the most beautiful of all, and yet she longed for the dust of Your feet. For its sake she gave up all desires, took vows, and performed austerities for a long time.”

## 225

> Subheading: To see mundane stone, wood, or metal in the transcendental Deity is a grave offense —

## 225–226

The *Bhakti-sandarbha* (section 286) says: “The highest worshippers see the Deity as none other than the Supreme Lord Himself. For as soon as the idea of difference arises, *bhakti* is broken; therefore only this is proper.”

The highest worshippers see the Deity (*śrī-mūrti*) as the Supreme Lord Himself. If one considers Bhagavān's Deity different from Bhagavān, *bhakti* is broken. Therefore one must regard the Deity as Bhagavān Himself. Once fallen from *bhakti*, the *jīva* becomes a nondevotee and falls into offenses. A verse of the *Padma Purāṇa* says: “One who sees stone in the worshipable Viṣṇu … is a dweller in hell.” By its meaning, a *jīva* who thinks that the form of Śrī Viṣṇu is made of material substance, or is only a symbol (*pratīka*), earns the name *nārakī*, a dweller in hell. The impersonalists are deprived of seeing the Deity with eyes of love, and their vision is mundane. Therefore Vaiṣṇavas call them “offensive Māyāvādīs.” And the *Śrīmad-Bhāgavatam*, in the verse *yasyātma-buddhiḥ* (“one who takes [the body] as the self…”),[^225-1] says that a person with such notions as *bhauma ijya-dhīḥ* (“regarding earthly things as worshipable”) does not gain eligibility for service, because of his ignorance.

## 228

> Subheading: To be a Vaiṣṇava is to worship Rādhā-Govinda under the shelter of Nityānanda and Gaura —

## 228–229

All the Vaiṣṇavas who live in Śrī Vṛndāvana bring supreme good. They are devoted to Kṛṣṇa's name and have taken shelter of the *bhakti* called *kīrtana*. The treasure of their lives is Śrī Gaura-Nityānanda. Apart from the eternal service of Rādhā-Kṛṣṇa, they know no other, invented *bhakti*. But nowadays some abandon the worship of the pure devotees of old and devise new paths! Some say, “Whether Śrī Gaurāṅga is Rādhā-Kṛṣṇa or not, we like His name Gaura; the name Rādhā-Kṛṣṇa does not give us as much taste.” Others hold that “Gaura-bhakti” is worshipping Gaura in *madhura-rasa* (*sambhoga*) in the mood of “Nadīyā-nāgarī”! If, they say, Gaura is not worshipped in the mood of a *nāgarī*, what is the point of Śrī Gaurāṅga's separate avatāra? Such wicked views did not exist before. But as Kali grows, such grotesque ideas are spreading under the garb of Vaiṣṇavas, and the pure devotees grieve to see it. These people have become playthings of insurmountable *māyā* and consider Śrī Gaurāṅga a little greater than Śrī Rādhā-Kṛṣṇa. They reason: “Gaurāṅga is the united body of Rādhā and Kṛṣṇa, and so He is greater than Kṛṣṇa alone.” Others again bow at the feet of the mundane *smārtas* and the society of *pañcopāsakas*; they oppose Gaura, Gaura's abode, Gaura's *śakti*, and devotion to Gaura, and they invent the worship of Rādhā-Kṛṣṇa by the power of knowledge born of the material senses. Both these groups oppose the pure teaching of the six Gosvāmīs. They therefore lack devotion to Bhagavān; they are given to the senses, godless, and servants of Kali. Śrī Kavirāja Gosvāmī, all-seeing and all-knowing, foresaw this: in the future, proud people averse to Hari will try by force of imagination to pass themselves off as most dear to Śrī Gaurasundara. They will forget the real Gaura, abandon devotion to Rādhā-Kṛṣṇa, and extol a Gaura of their own invention, born from the womb of their wicked desires, to deceive unfortunate *jīvas*. Needless to say, in truth the only object of worship for those who have taken shelter at the feet of Śrī Gaurāṅga is the two feet of Śrī Gāndharvikā-Giridhārī.
