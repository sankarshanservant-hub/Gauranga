## 12

> Subheading: Śrī Advaita, full of auspiciousness —

Śrī Advaita Prabhu is Mahā-Viṣṇu. He is the ācārya. Viṣṇu's conduct is auspicious in His very nature as the agent. When the *jīva* sees the auspiciousness in His auspicious pastimes and in His very being, good comes to the *jīva*. He is the source of all auspiciousness. His conduct, turned toward service, brings good to everyone in the world. The worthless people of the world (“the world's encumbrance”) cannot understand this pure, eternal, complete and free auspiciousness, and so they fall away from *bhakti*, the natural function of the soul. Acts rooted in the wish to enjoy, the attainment of featureless liberation and every other inauspicious thing find no place in Śrī Advaita, who possesses spiritual qualities. Certain *jīvas* of demoniac nature, bewildered by *māyā*, failed to understand that He is the nondual Viṣṇu-*tattva*; holding to the devotionless doctrine of *kevalādvaita*, they only pretended to follow Him. The Ācārya punished these nondevotees under the guise of nourishing their self-conceit through His own *māyā*. Yet even this punishment is only an auspicious act. The Viṣṇu-*vastu*, by both the direct and the indirect way (*anvaya* and *vyatireka*), brings the *jīva* nothing but good. One who passes off the inauspicious as auspicious cannot grasp the Source of Viṣṇu's *māyā* in its material aspect. Some say that Advaita Prabhu had another name, “Maṅgala.” As a *naimittika-avatāra* (one who descends for a particular occasion), He instills into *prakṛti* the power of the material cause. He is not an inauspicious material object, nor is He the abode of inauspicious material qualities. By following His example the *jīva* attains good. When the *jīva* hears and chants His name, all that is inauspicious in it is destroyed. One must not attribute to the Viṣṇu-*vastu* any quality that is undesirable, inferior, limited or featureless. Only by gaining transcendental knowledge of His real being does the *jīva* attain the highest good (*niḥśreyasa*).

## 15

> Subheading: Kāraṇaśāyī creates in two forms —

When people try to determine the source of the visible world, two different methods of reasoning appear. According to one view, the world is created from the Reality full of being, consciousness and bliss (*sac-cid-ānanda*), but only secondarily; primarily, that Reality manifests Goloka, Vaikuṇṭha and the other abodes together with their associates. According to the other view, the source is the source of the unreal, the unconscious and the joyless: it is unknowable, unmanifest and material. The former view is upheld by Vedānta, the purpose of the Vedas and their final fruit. Sāṅkhya and other *smṛtis* put forward the opposite, latter view in order to oppose the teaching of the true Reality. The visible world is perceived, for the most part, as unconscious. In living beings there is a semblance of consciousness (*cid-ābhāsa*) bound up with the universe fashioned by *guṇa-māyā*. Some hold that even this conscious principle is produced from *prakṛti* by the *guṇas*, and on this ground they set their teaching on the material cause apart from Vedānta. But the source, the cause of all causes, is the Possessor of potency (*śaktimat-tattva*), and the potency abides in the Possessor of potency. Besides the potency from which the visible world arose, other potencies also abide eternally in that great, all-sustaining Reality. Those bound to serving the objects of the visible world perceive the worldly potency and think Bhagavān merely its possessor. They reach a false conclusion (*apasiddhānta*): that the Possessor of potency was born of a single potency, and that, since with mundane minds they see partial possessors of potency, the undivided Possessor of potency was also born of *prakṛti*. If one takes as the “source” what mundane sense-knowledge defines as knowable being and non-being, one will of course conclude that consciousness arises from the unconscious. But the actual truth rests in the true Reality, who possesses potency. There is a method that does not accept as the root cause the Reality who creates place, time and circumstance; it first takes up countless objects in all their variety and from them, relying on inference, ascends toward the One. This method is called *adhiroha-vāda*, the ascending method. In the descending (*avaroha*) method of reasoning, the Reality is the cause of all causes. Since infinite potency abides in Him, He is the Truth possessed of features (*saviśeṣa-tattva*). Even His featurelessness is but one among countless ways of considering His features. When people start from notions of unconscious objects, take Him to be an effect and search for His cause, there arises a mind like that of one who has kept company with intoxicants. In fact, the idea that inert *prakṛti* is the root cause is far from the real truth. The world, unmanifest and evolved from the unconscious potency (*acit-śakti*), comes from the power of glance (*īkṣaṇa*) of the Supreme Lord, the Possessor of infinite potencies. Only by receiving power from the All-powerful does *prakṛti* build the world, which lies in time and space and is accessible to the *jīva*'s material senses. The true Reality, who possesses infinite potencies, is perceived by the conditioned *jīva* only through the potency that builds the world. Such errors of reasoning arise from failing to discern how the Reality is related to His potency; they give rise in the *jīva* to *vivarta*, taking one thing for another. Until the truth is revealed, the *jīva* turned away from Bhagavān wanders in the world fit for enjoyment and does not find the true Reality.

Śrīla Baladeva Vidyābhūṣaṇa writes in the *Govinda-bhāṣya* (*Brahma-sūtra*, Chapter 2, *pāda* 2): “Kapila, the teacher of Sāṅkhya, gathered the *tattvas* as follows. *Prakṛti* is the state of equilibrium of *sattva*, *rajas* and *tamas*. From *prakṛti* comes the *mahat*; from the *mahat*, *ahaṅkāra*; from *ahaṅkāra*, the five *tanmātras*, both kinds of senses and the gross elements; and there is the *puruṣa*. Such is the set of twenty-five. *Sattva* and the other *guṇas*, abiding in equilibrium, are *prakṛti*. They are to be understood as happiness, sorrow and delusion respectively, for in the world, their effect, everything is seen to take the form of happiness and the rest. Thus a young woman who gives her husband happiness by love is *sāttvikī*; when she gives him sorrow by her pique (*māna*) she is *rājasī*; when she bewilders him by separation she is *tāmasī*. All states are to be viewed in this way. ‘Both kinds of senses’ means the ten external senses and the one internal sense, the mind — eleven in all. *Prakṛti* is eternal and all-pervading. The root has no root; therefore the root is rootless. The material cause of all is not limited: ‘All-pervading, because effects are seen everywhere,’ say the *sūtras*. The *mahat*, *ahaṅkāra* and the five *tanmātras* are the seven *prakṛti-vikṛtis*: they are *prakṛti* (that which produces) for *ahaṅkāra* and the rest, and *vikṛti* (that which is produced) for *pradhāna* and the rest. The eleven senses and the five elements are sixteen, and they are only *vikṛtis*. The *puruṣa* does not change; therefore he is neither *prakṛti* nor *vikṛti* of anything. *Prakṛti*, however, is ever transforming. Though itself unconscious, it is the cause of the enjoyment and the liberation (*apavarga*) of many conscious beings. Though utterly beyond the senses, it is inferred from its effects. It is one, yet its *guṇas* are unequal, and by its power of transformation it gives birth to the world with its varied design, beginning with the *mahat*. Thus it is both the efficient and the material cause of the world. The *puruṣa* is inactive and without *guṇas*, an all-pervading spirit; he is distinct in each body and is inferred from the fact that composite things exist for the sake of another. Because the *puruṣa* neither changes nor acts, he is neither agent nor enjoyer. Given this nature of *prakṛti* and *puruṣa*, their mere proximity leads to an exchange of properties: consciousness is superimposed on *prakṛti*, and agency and enjoyment on the *puruṣa*. Thus from non-discrimination comes enjoyment, and from discrimination, liberation. When *prakṛti* becomes indifferent, that is [liberation]. Kapila set out such points in *sūtras* with arguments. In this system he accepted perception, inference and scriptural testimony as the means of valid knowledge (*pramāṇas*): ‘The *pramāṇas* are three; when a thing is established by them, all is established; there is no further means of establishing.’ Among these, there is no great dispute about matters established by perception and scripture. But *pradhāna*, which he infers to be the cause of the world by *sūtras* such as ‘From limitation,’ ‘From uniformity,’ ‘From potency’ and others, must be refuted: by refuting it one refutes his whole doctrine. Here a doubt arises: is *pradhāna* the efficient and material cause of the world or not? [The opponent says:] *Pradhāna* is exactly that. The world is *sāttvika* and so on; therefore *pradhāna*, which consists of *sattva* and the other *guṇas*, must be inferred to be its material cause. We see that the material cause of effects like a pot is homogeneous with them — clay and the like. And just as we say ‘the tree bears fruit’ or ‘the water flows,’ so inert *pradhāna* too has the capacity to act. Therefore *pradhāna* alone is the material cause of the world and its maker. When this is proposed, [it is said] (*Brahma-sūtra*, Chapter 2, *pāda* 2):

“‘And because design is impossible, not that which is inferred’ (1).

“‘That which is inferred’ is what is inferred to be the cause of the world, namely inert *pradhāna*. It is neither the material cause of the world nor its efficient cause. Why? ‘Because design is impossible’: inert *pradhāna*, unless a conscious being directs it, cannot build the varied world. In our world, bricks and the like do not assemble into palaces and other buildings unless a conscious being directs them. The word ‘and’ adds the impossibility of concomitance (*anvaya*): external things such as pots are not endowed with the nature of happiness and the rest. Happiness and the rest are internal; pots and the like merely cause happiness and so on, and no one perceives them to be happiness themselves.

“‘And because of activity’ (2).

“Supply: ‘when the inert is directed by a conscious being.’ Clearly the activity belongs to the controller in whose presence the inert acts — as with a chariot and its driver. This also refutes ‘it bears fruit’ and the like: there too a conscious being is in control — this is known from the *Antaryāmi-brāhmaṇa*, and it will become clear later. ‘And’ here has a restrictive sense. Or else: activity is seen only in a conscious being — ‘I do’ — therefore the inert cannot be an agent. One may object: the mere proximity of *prakṛti* and *puruṣa* leads to a mutual superimposition of properties, and this explains the design of the world. We reply: is the proximity that causes superimposition simply their coexistence, or some change in *prakṛti* or in the *puruṣa*? Not the first, for then superimposition would occur even in the liberated. Not the second: a change in *prakṛti* is held to be an effect of superimposition and cannot be its cause, and a change in the *puruṣa* is not accepted at all.

“One may object: milk turns into curd by itself, and rainwater from a cloud, though it has a single taste, takes on varied tastes — sweet, sour and others — in the palm, the mango and other trees. In the same way *pradhāna*, because of the variety of the *puruṣas*' *karma*, takes the form of bodies, worlds and the rest. To this it is said:

“‘If [it is said:] like milk and water — there too’ (3).

“Milk and water, too, become active only when a conscious being directs them, and not by themselves: so we infer from the example of the chariot and the like. And that He directs them is established by the *Antaryāmi-brāhmaṇa*.

“‘And because there is nothing apart [from *pradhāna*], [there can be no talk of] independence’ (4).

“‘And’ has the sense of ‘also.’ Before creation, they say, there was no other cause apart from *pradhāna*, and *pradhāna* depended on nothing. Yet even then *pradhāna* alone cannot bring about its own transformation. They accepted that before the first creation there was no cause apart from *pradhāna* to impel it or to restrain it. But then they again needed such a cause: they accepted another cause, the proximity of consciousness. Thus the doctrine that the inert alone can be an agent collapses. Moreover, if there is no separate cause and proximity is always present, effects would arise even during the dissolution of the world (*pralaya*). Nor can one say that there are no effects then because the fruits of past deeds (*adṛṣṭa*) have not awakened, for their awakening would also have to be ascribed to that very time.

“One may object: grass, creepers, leaves and the like turn into milk by their own nature, without any other cause; so too *pradhāna* turns into the *mahat* and the rest. To this it is said:

“‘And not like grass and the like, because it does not happen elsewhere’ (5).

“‘And’ is for emphasis. This argument is not clever. Why? ‘It does not happen elsewhere’: grass and the like, when eaten by a bull or a similar animal, do not turn into milk. If grass and the like turned into milk by their own nature, the same would happen to grass fallen in a courtyard; but it does not. Therefore nature alone is not enough. This happens by the will of the Lord of all, through a connection with a particular being.

“It has been shown that *pradhāna* is inert and therefore does not act by itself. Now, to make you happy, let us grant that it does; even then nothing you wish for will follow. So it is said:

“‘And even if granted — because there is no purpose’ (6).

“‘Not’ is understood in the four [*sūtras*]. [Sāṅkhya] holds that *pradhāna* acts for the enjoyment and the liberation of the *puruṣa*: ‘The *puruṣa* will enjoy me, experience my faults and attain liberation, which is my indifference to him.’ The activity of *pradhāna* is for another's sake, since it does not enjoy anything itself, like a camel carrying saffron. And [Sāṅkhya] holds that the *puruṣa*, though not an agent, is an enjoyer: even one who does not cook enjoys the fruit, like an eater of food. But such activity is unreasonable. Why? If it is granted, it will bear no fruit. Its fruit would be the *puruṣa*'s enjoyment, that is, his beholding *prakṛti*, and his liberation, that is, the indifference of *prakṛti*. Enjoyment is impossible: before the activity begins, the *puruṣa* is pure consciousness, changeless and not an agent, and cannot undergo the change of beginning to behold *prakṛti*. Liberation is also impossible: he had it even before *prakṛti* became active, and so the activity would be pointless. And if mere proximity is the cause of enjoyment, then even the liberated will enjoy, for proximity is eternal.

“One may object: a blind man who can walk but cannot see moves through the proximity of a lame man who cannot walk but can see. Iron, though inert, moves in the presence of a magnet. So too *prakṛti*, though unconscious, in the presence of the *puruṣa*, who is pure spirit, becomes as if conscious through his reflection and acts in creation for his sake. To this it is said:

“‘If [it is said:] like a man and a stone — even then [the argument fails]’ (7).

“Even in this way one cannot prove that the inert acts by itself. The lame man, though he cannot walk, has his own capacities: he sees the road and points it out. The blind man, though he cannot see, also has capacities: he accepts directions. The magnet has nearness to the iron and so on. But the *puruṣa*, eternally inactive and without properties, undergoes no change at all. If creation is held to come from mere proximity, then, since proximity is eternal, creation would be eternal and there would be no liberation at all. Moreover, the lame man and the blind man are both conscious, while the magnet and the iron are both inert; the examples are plainly dissimilar.

“Some hold that the universe is created when one *guṇa* rises and the others decline, so that a relation of principal and subordinate arises among them. This is refuted as follows:

“‘And because principal status is impossible’ (8).

“The state of *pradhāna* is the equilibrium of *sattva* and the other *guṇas*. In that state none of them, independent by nature, can become principal: the other two are equal to it and cannot become subordinate. Thus no relation of principal and subordinate arises among the *guṇas*. Nor can its cause be Īśvara or time, for [Sāṅkhya] does not accept them. As Kapila says: ‘Because Īśvara is not proved’; ‘He would be neither liberated nor bound; therefore He is not proved’; and ‘Space and time come from ether and the rest.’ Nor can the *puruṣa* be that cause, for he is indifferent here. Therefore a creation caused by the imbalance of the *guṇas* does not take place. Furthermore, since there is no cause, the *guṇas* might lose their balance even at the dissolution of the world, and not lose it at the first creation.

“One may object: we should infer that the *guṇas* have different natures, according to their effects; then there is no room for the fault mentioned. To this it is said:

“‘And if inferred otherwise — because they lack the power of knowing’ (9).

“Even if one infers that the *guṇas* have various powers, there is no escape from the fault. Why? ‘Because they lack the power of knowing,’ that is, because the *guṇas* do not know: they have no thought ‘I create this, and in this way.’ From the inert, devoid of knowledge, there is no creation — just as with bricks — unless a conscious being directs it.”

Kapila, the teacher of Sāṅkhya, gathered the *tattvas* as follows. In his view, “*prakṛti*” is the state of equilibrium of the three *guṇas*: *sattva*, *rajas* and *tamas*. From *prakṛti* comes the *mahat-tattva*; from it, *ahaṅkāra*; from *ahaṅkāra*, the five *tanmātras*; from them, the senses of knowledge and the senses of action, and also the gross elements; and there is the “*puruṣa*.” These are the twenty-five *tattvas* in all. The three *guṇas*, *sattva* and the others, abiding in equilibrium, are *prakṛti*. These three *guṇas* are to be understood as happiness, sorrow and delusion respectively, for in the world, the effect of *prakṛti*, only states such as happiness are seen. An example: a young woman who gives her husband happiness by love displays a “*sāttvika*” mood; the same woman becomes “*rājasī*” when she causes sorrow, and “*tāmasī*” when she bewilders. The words “both kinds of senses” mean the ten external senses and the one internal sense, the mind — eleven senses in all. *Prakṛti* is eternal and all-pervading. At the root there is no root (no conscious principle); therefore the root (*pradhāna*) has no root, that is, it has no other cause. This *pradhāna* is unlimited and is the material cause of everything; this follows from *sūtras* such as “All-pervading, because effects are seen everywhere.” The *mahat-tattva*, the *ahaṅkāra-tattva* and the five *tanmātras* are the seven *prakṛti-vikṛtis*: they are *prakṛti* for *ahaṅkāra* and the rest, and at the same time transformations of *pradhāna*. The eleven senses and the five great elements are the sixteen transformations. The *puruṣa* does not change, and therefore he is neither *prakṛti* nor a transformation of anything. *Prakṛti* is ever transforming; though itself unconscious, it is the cause of the enjoyment and the liberation (*apavarga*) of many conscious *jīvas*; though beyond the senses, it is inferred from its effects. *Prakṛti* is one, but its *guṇas* are unequal, and so by its power of transformation it gives birth to a world full of varied creations, beginning with the *mahat*. Thus *prakṛti* is both the efficient and the material cause of the world. The *puruṣa* is inactive, without *guṇas* and all-pervading. He is spiritual by nature and distinct in each body; he is inferred from the way *pradhāna* is set in motion. Because the *puruṣa* neither changes nor acts, he is neither agent nor enjoyer. Such is the truth of *prakṛti* and *puruṣa*, and therefore their mere proximity leads to an exchange of properties: consciousness is superimposed on *prakṛti*, and agency and enjoyment on the *puruṣa*. While there is no such discrimination, there is enjoyment; with discrimination comes *apavarga*, that is, liberation. These and other points — for example, the *puruṣa*'s indifference toward *prakṛti* — are set out in *sūtras* with arguments. In his system the author of Sāṅkhya accepted three means of knowledge (*pramāṇas*): “perception,” “inference” and “scripture” (*āgama*). When something is established by them, all is established. (Comparison and the other *pramāṇas* are included in these three and are not separate *pramāṇas*.) There is no great dispute about matters established by perception and scripture. But now we must refute the claim that *pradhāna* is the cause of the world, which is inferred by *sūtras* such as “From limitation,” “From uniformity,” “From potency” and others; for by refuting this view one refutes all the other views of Sāṅkhya. The doubt here is this: is “*pradhāna*” the efficient and the material cause of the world or not? The *pūrvapakṣa* grants *pradhāna* both. The *pūrvapakṣa* says: *pradhāna*, consisting of *sattva* and the rest, is inferred precisely as the material cause of the world. The material cause is always homogeneous with the effect: thus for a pot and similar things the material cause is clay and the like, which is homogeneous with them. An inert tree bears fruit; equally inert water flows; seeing this, one concludes that inert, unconscious *pradhāna* too is able to create the world. So: “*pradhāna* is both the material and the efficient cause of the world.” To refute this conclusion of the *pūrvapakṣa*, the author introduces the first *sūtra*.

*Pradhāna* is unconscious; therefore inert *pradhāna* cannot be either the material or the efficient cause of the world. Looking at the varied design of this world, it is clear that inert *pradhāna* cannot build the visible world without the direction of a conscious being, and that it is unreasonable to infer so. In this world, bricks and the like have never yet assembled into palaces and similar buildings unless a conscious being directed them. The word “and” in the *sūtra* adds the impossibility of concomitance (*anvaya*). External objects such as pots are never endowed with the nature of happiness and the like. For happiness and the rest are internal properties, and therefore they cannot accompany external things. Above all, a pot and the like merely cause happiness and so on; they themselves are not perceived as happiness.

The second *sūtra*. Even if we look at activity, *pradhāna* cannot be the cause. Activity is seen only in what is inert and directed by a conscious being. Clearly that activity belongs to the one under whose direction the inert is set in motion. The best example is a chariot and its driver. The same holds for the example “the tree bears fruit” and similar ones offered in support of *pradhāna* as the cause. There too the direction of a conscious being is accepted, for the *Antaryāmi-brāhmaṇa* speaks of it; this will be explained later in this commentary. The word “and” in the *sūtra* has a restrictive sense. In the example “I am doing,” activity is seen only in a conscious being; therefore the inert cannot be an agent. You may say: the world is designed because, through the mere proximity of *prakṛti* and *puruṣa*, their properties are superimposed on each other. The answer: this cannot be said either. Very well — is the proximity that causes the mutual superimposition of properties the coexistence of *prakṛti* and *puruṣa*, or some change in *prakṛti* or in the *puruṣa*? The answer: it can by no means be their coexistence, for if we accept that, we must accept superimposition in all liberated *puruṣas* as well. Nor can this proximity be a change in *prakṛti*: a change in *prakṛti* is considered an effect of superimposition and so cannot be its cause. Nor can it be a change in the *puruṣa*: a change in the *puruṣa* cannot be accepted either. Therefore “*pradhāna*” cannot be the cause of the world.

You may say: as milk turns into curd by itself, and as the water from a single cloud, though it has one taste, becomes sweet, sour and of many tastes in the fruits of the palm, the mango and other trees, so the one *pradhāna*, according to the variety of the *puruṣas*' *karma*, transforms into bodies, worlds and the rest. In reply the author says:

The third *sūtra*. Unconscious things such as milk and water also act in their effects only when a conscious being directs them; they are not set in motion by themselves: so it is inferred from the example of the chariot and the like. And from the *Antaryāmi-brāhmaṇa* it is established that these two inert things are directed by consciousness.

The fourth *sūtra*. The claim that there is no cause apart from *pradhāna* had to be abandoned; therefore the claim that *pradhāna* alone is the agent is also untenable. The word *api* means the same as *ca*, that is, “and” (conjunction). The view that before creation there was no cause apart from *pradhāna* has been given up; therefore the view that *pradhāna* alone transforms itself is also refuted. The view that “before the first creation there is no other cause apart from *pradhāna* to impel it or restrain it” has been set aside, for at that point another cause is accepted — the proximity of the conscious principle. Therefore the doctrine that the inert alone is the agent is refuted. Moreover, under such a *pūrvapakṣa* effects would arise even during the dissolution of the world: since there is no cause apart from *pradhāna* and proximity to it remains, effects would arise at *pralaya* just as at creation. Nor can one say that there are no effects at *pralaya* because the *adṛṣṭa* has not awakened, for the awakening of that *adṛṣṭa* might also come at that very time.

You may say: as grass, leaves and the like, eaten by a cow or another animal, turn into milk by themselves, so *pradhāna* transforms into the *tattvas* beginning with the *mahat*. In reply the author says:

The fifth *sūtra*. Elsewhere grass does not turn into milk; therefore one cannot say that *pradhāna*, like grass, transforms by its own nature (by itself). The word “and” here is for affirmation. Such a *pūrvapakṣa* is untenable, for this is not seen elsewhere: grass eaten by a bull or a similar animal does not turn into milk, and so the transformation is not natural. Besides, if grass and the like turned into milk by their own nature, this would be seen in a courtyard too. Since it is not seen, nature alone cannot be called the cause of the transformation; its cause is the will of the Lord of all: “Let grass, in connection with particular living beings, turn into milk.”

It has been proved that *pradhāna* is inert and therefore does not come into action by itself. Next the author says: even if, to please you, I grant it, nothing you desire will follow.

The sixth *sūtra*. Even if one grants that *pradhāna* acts by its own nature, there is no purpose in it. In four *sūtras* the sense “not” is understood. It seems that *pradhāna* acts for the sake of enjoyment and liberation: “The *puruṣa* will enjoy me, feel my faults and attain liberation, that is, indifference toward me.” As a camel carries a load of saffron only for others and does not enjoy it itself, so *pradhāna* acts only for the sake of others. And the *puruṣa*, it seems, though not an agent, is an enjoyer. As an eater enjoys food though he has not cooked it, so the *puruṣa* tastes the fruits. But to hold, with the *pūrvapakṣa*, that *pradhāna* acts in this way is unreasonable: even if it is granted, no fruit is seen. The fruit of the activity is the *puruṣa*'s enjoyment, that is, beholding *prakṛti*, and liberation, that is, indifference toward *prakṛti*. Enjoyment from the activity of *pradhāna* is impossible, for then the *puruṣa* — pure consciousness, changeless and not an agent — would enjoy only because, by beholding *prakṛti*, he came into contact with it and underwent change. *Apavarga* from the activity of *pradhāna* is also impossible: *apavarga* existed even before the activity arose, and so the activity is useless. And if mere proximity is called the cause of enjoyment, then, since proximity is eternal, even the liberated will have to enjoy.

You may say: a blind man who can walk but cannot see begins to move near a lame man who cannot walk but can see; and inert iron also moves near a magnet. So too *prakṛti*, though unconscious, near the *puruṣa*, who is pure consciousness, under the influence of his reflection sets about creation and the rest for the *puruṣa*'s enjoyment, as if it were a conscious being. In reply the author says:

The seventh *sūtra*. Even if the *puruṣa* is like a magnet, inert *pradhāna* does not act by itself. Even so, one cannot prove that the inert acts by itself. The lame man cannot walk, yet he has his own capacities — to point out the road and to give directions. The blind man cannot see, yet he too has a capacity — to accept the lame man's directions. And a magnet can come near iron. But the eternally inactive *puruṣa*, devoid of properties, undergoes no change whatever. If proximity itself is accepted as change, then, since proximity is eternal, one must accept eternal creation and the absence of liberation. Above all, the lame man and the blind man are both conscious, while the magnet and the iron are both inert; the examples are plainly dissimilar.

Next the author refutes the view that the universe is created because the *guṇas* rise and decline and a relation of principal and subordinate arises among them:

The eighth *sūtra*. The principal status of a *guṇa* is impossible in itself; therefore this view is untenable. The abiding of the *guṇas* — *sattva* and the rest — in equilibrium is called “the state of *pradhāna*.” In that state the *guṇas* are independent by nature, and so none of them proves to be principal over another. For if one of the three *guṇas* is accepted as principal, the other two, being equal to it, cannot become subordinate. A relation of principal and subordinate among the *guṇas* never arises. Nor can Īśvara or time be called its maker, for no one [among them] accepts this. Kapila himself said: “Since [Īśvara] could be neither liberated nor bound — that is, since there is no proof — Īśvara is not proved.” Space and time arise from ether and the rest; the *puruṣa* is not their maker, for he is entirely indifferent to activity. Nor is an imbalance of the *guṇas* the cause of creation. Moreover, since there is no cause, the *guṇas* might lose their balance at every later creation, but not at the first.

You may say: one can infer that the *guṇas*, according to their effects, are of different natures; then there is no room for the fault mentioned. In reply the author says:

The ninth *sūtra*. Even if one infers otherwise, the inert does not act by itself. That is, even if one infers that the *guṇas* are endowed with such varied powers, there is no escaping the fault: the *guṇas* lack the capacity to know (consciousness); evidently they do not have even the thought “Here I am, and this is how I create.” Creation from inert matter devoid of knowledge is never possible. As bricks, wood and other unconscious things can do nothing unless a conscious being directs them, so the unconscious *guṇas* can do nothing unless the potency of the conscious Supreme Lord directs them.

(Chapter 2, *pāda* 1.) “The great sage Kapila, desiring liberation, composed the *smṛti* [Sāṅkhya] to elaborate the meaning of the *jñāna-kāṇḍa*. He duly accepted the *agnihotra* and the other rites enjoined by the *karma-kāṇḍa*, and the Śrutis recognize him as trustworthy: ‘the sage Kapila, born [in the beginning],’ and so on. By his *sūtras* ‘Now, the complete cessation of the threefold misery is the highest goal of man,’ ‘Not by visible means, for even after cessation [misery] returns’ and others, it is established there that the cause of the world is unconscious, independent *pradhāna* alone; [this is also said in the *sūtras*] ‘For the liberation of the liberated,’ ‘Or for its own sake — of *pradhāna*,’ ‘Though *pradhāna* is unconscious, its activity is like that of milk’ and others. If Brahman is accepted as the cause, this *smṛti* will be left without a subject, for the whole of it speaks of nothing but *pradhāna*. Therefore Vedānta should be interpreted so as not to contradict the *smṛti* of Kapila, who is entirely trustworthy. The *smṛtis* of Manu and others will not be left without a subject, for by setting forth *dharma* they elaborate the meaning of the *karma-kāṇḍa* and so have their own subject. When this is proposed, it is said:

“‘If [it is said:] there follows the fault that the *smṛti* has no room — no, for there follows the fault that other *smṛtis* have no room’ (1).

“‘Has no room’ means is left without a subject. If Vedānta is interpreted according to its single import (*samanvaya*), the Sāṅkhya *smṛti* incurs the fault of being left without a subject; so must [the texts of Vedānta] be interpreted in a sense contrary to the Śruti? If that is said — no. Why? ‘Other’ and so on. For then a great fault would arise: other *smṛtis* would be left without a subject — the *smṛtis* of Manu and others, which follow Vedānta and teach that Brahman alone is the cause. In them the Lord of all is shown to be the cause of the origin of the world and the rest, and by no means some other principle named by Kapila. Thus Śrīman Manu says: ‘This [world] was darkness — unknown, without marks, beyond reasoning, unknowable, as if wholly asleep. Then the self-born Bhagavān, unmanifest, manifesting this [world] — the great elements and the rest — appeared in full power, dispelling the darkness. He who is beyond the senses, imperceptible, subtle, unmanifest, eternal, containing all beings and inconceivable — He Himself appeared. Wishing to create various beings from His own body, He reflected and first created the waters, and placed a seed in them. It became a golden egg, shining like the sun; in it Brahmā himself was born, the grandfather of all the worlds,’[^15-1] and so on. Śrī Parāśara says: ‘From Viṣṇu the world arose, and in Him it abides; He is the one who maintains and dissolves this world, and He is the world’;[^15-2] ‘As a spider draws a thread from its heart through its mouth, plays with it and swallows it again, so does Janārdana,’ and so on. Others speak in the same way. Nor can one say that these *smṛtis* have their subject in elaborating the meaning of the *karma-kāṇḍa*. They enjoin *dharmas* for purifying the mind, so that knowledge of Brahman may dawn, and therefore their purpose is only to elaborate the meaning of the *jñāna-kāṇḍa*. That they purify the mind is seen from the Śruti: ‘Him [the *brāhmaṇas* seek to know] by the study of the Vedas,’[^15-3] and so on. Here and there one sees and experiences that they bring such fruits as rain, sons, heaven and the like. But this too, by creating trust in the scriptures, finally leads to Him, for it is said: ‘The goal that all the Vedas proclaim’[^15-4] and ‘The Vedas are devoted to Nārāyaṇa.’[^15-5] The Sāṅkhya *smṛti* cannot elaborate the meaning of Vedānta, for it teaches what contradicts the Śruti. Elaboration (*upabṛṁhaṇa*) is the clarifying of a meaning that agrees with the Śruti, and in it there is no such thing. Therefore the Sāṅkhya *smṛti*, which contradicts the Śruti, is invented out of its author's own head and is not trustworthy; and we do not fear the charge that it will prove useless. Nor is it right to favor that *smṛti* on the pretext that it is trustworthy. Many interpreters are declared trustworthy, and their *smṛtis* differ in meaning; if one favors one of them, the real meaning will never be settled. When *smṛtis* contradict one another, there is no other way to decide than to rely on the Śruti; therefore one should honor only the *smṛti* that follows the Śruti. You object by the strength of *smṛti* — by the strength of *smṛti* we shall refute you: thus the fault ‘other *smṛtis* have no room’ is pointed out. Some cite the *Śvetāśvatara-śruti* as proof that Kapila is trustworthy: ‘He who in the beginning endowed the born sage Kapila with knowledge.’[^15-6] But this is not so: those words speak of someone else, and one whose words contradict the meaning of the Śruti cannot be trustworthy. As for Manu's trustworthiness, the Taittirīyas recite: ‘Whatever Manu said is medicine.’ And of Śrī Parāśara tradition says that he understood the highest truth about the Deity by the grace of Pulastya and Vasiṣṭha. Kapila, the founder of the *smṛti* opposed to the Vedas, is a particular *jīva* of the line of Agni, deluded by *māyā*, and not Vāsudeva born of Kardama. For tradition says: ‘Kapila, called Vāsudeva, taught the doctrine of Sāṅkhya to Brahmā and the other gods, to Bhṛgu and others, and likewise to Āsuri; all of it was elaborated by the meaning of the Vedas. But another Kapila taught a doctrine opposed to all the Vedas’; ‘[he taught] Sāṅkhya to another Āsuri, elaborated with false arguments.’ Therefore, if the Sāṅkhya *smṛti*, untrustworthy because it contradicts the Vedas, proves useless, there is no fault in that.

“‘And because the rest is not found’ (2).

“‘And the rest,’ that is, what the Sāṅkhya *smṛti* speaks of, is not found in the Vedas; therefore it is not trustworthy. These are its tenets: the *puruṣas* are all-pervading and are consciousness alone; *prakṛti* alone binds and liberates them, and bondage and liberation belong to *prakṛti* alone; there is no Lord of all, a special Puruṣa; time is not a *tattva*; the five *prāṇas* and the rest are merely functions of the senses. Such tenets and others like them are to be seen in it.”

1. The Śruti mentions a trustworthy sage named “Kapila.” He duly accepted the *karma-kāṇḍa* enjoined by the Vedas. This same sage Kapila composed the Sāṅkhya *smṛti* in order to elaborate the *jñāna-kāṇḍa*. In *sūtras* such as “Now, the complete cessation of the threefold misery is the highest goal of man,” the Sāṅkhya *smṛti* calls the complete cessation of the threefold misery — *ādhyātmika* and the rest — “the highest goal of man,” or liberation. In it unconscious *pradhāna* alone is established as the independent cause of the world. If Brahman alone is called the sole cause of the world, this Sāṅkhya *smṛti* will be left without a subject, for from beginning to end it speaks of nothing else. Therefore the texts of Vedānta should be interpreted so as not to contradict the views of the entirely trustworthy sage Kapila. Then the *smṛtis* proclaimed by Manu and others will not be left without a subject either: by setting forth *dharma* they elaborate the *karma-kāṇḍa* and so have a subject. To refute this, the author introduces the first *sūtra*, “If [it is said:] there follows the fault that the *smṛti* has no room…”.

Absence of room is *anavakāśa*; the word *anavakāśa* means being left without a subject. If Vedānta is interpreted according to its single import (*samanvaya*), the Sāṅkhya *smṛti* incurs the fault of being left without a subject. So should the texts of Vedānta be interpreted in a sense opposite to what one hears in them? The answer: that is impossible. If they are interpreted so, a great calamity follows: other *smṛtis* — those of Manu and others, which follow Vedānta and teach that Brahman alone is the cause — will be left without a subject. In these *smṛtis* only the Lord of all is shown to be the cause of the origin of the world and the rest. The *tattvas* are not described in them as the sage Kapila describes them. Thus Śrī Manu says: “Before creation the whole universe was darkness — unknown, without marks, beyond reasoning, unknowable — and lay as if asleep. Then the self-born Bhagavān, though Himself unmanifest, appeared endowed with the potencies of the great elements and the rest in order to manifest this world, and dispelled that darkness. He is beyond the senses, imperceptible, subtle, unmanifest, eternal, containing all beings and inconceivable by nature. Having appeared Himself, He desired in His mind to create various beings from His own body, and first of all He created water. Then the Supreme Lord placed His seed in that water. From the seed arose a golden egg, shining like a thousand suns. In that egg Brahmā himself, the grandfather of all the worlds, was born.” And the sage Parāśara says: “The visible world arose from Bhagavān Viṣṇu and abides resting on Him. He maintains it and He destroys it. This world is a particular potency of His. As a spider draws threads out of its own body and later swallows them itself, so Bhagavān Viṣṇu creates the universe from His potency and later dissolves it again in His potency.” Other sages say the same. Nor can one say that the Sāṅkhya *smṛti*[^15-7] will have a subject by elaborating the *karma-kāṇḍa*: [these *smṛtis*] enjoin *dharma* for the purification of the mind, so that knowledge of Brahman may dawn. Therefore the purpose of these *smṛtis* is to elaborate the *jñāna-kāṇḍa*. It is also seen that these *dharmas* purify the mind: the words of the Śruti “Him [the *brāhmaṇas* seek to know] by the study of the Vedas” and so on are the proof. Here and there it is seen and confirmed that these *dharmas* (their performance) bring such fruits as rain, sons, heaven and the like. But this too is only to purify the mind by creating faith in the scriptures. The Śrutis “The goal that all the Vedas proclaim” and “The Vedas are devoted to Nārāyaṇa” express the same intention. Even if the purpose of the Sāṅkhya *smṛti* is to elaborate the *jñāna-kāṇḍa*, one still cannot accept that it elaborates the meaning of Vedānta, for the Sāṅkhya *smṛti* teaches what contradicts the Śruti. To clarify the meaning of the statements of the Śruti — that is what “elaboration” (*upabṛṁhaṇa*) means. No such clarification of the meaning of the Śruti is seen in the Sāṅkhya *smṛti*. It must therefore be judged contrary to the Śruti. And what contradicts the Śruti is invented out of one's own head and is therefore untrustworthy. So the uselessness of this untrustworthy Sāṅkhya *smṛti* is inevitable. Nor is it right to favor one *smṛti* while waiting for the untrustworthiness of another to be established. If one favors *smṛtis* that differ in meaning, then, faced with the many opinions of Gautama and many others who interpret in different ways, one will never settle the real meaning. When two *smṛtis* contradict each other, one cannot resort to any deciding proof other than the Śruti. Whatever one relies on must certainly follow the Śruti; if it does not follow the Śruti, it cannot be honored. Those who raise censure by the strength of *smṛti* will be refuted by the strength of that same *smṛti*; therefore pointing out the fault “other *smṛtis* will be left without a subject” is unavoidable. In the *Śvetāśvatara Upaniṣad* the words “the sage Kapila, born…” and so on do speak of a trustworthy sage Kapila. But that Kapila is not this one; he is another sage Kapila. Therefore, in calling Kapila, the author of Sāṅkhya, untrustworthy, we show no disrespect to the Śruti. The trustworthiness of Manu and Parāśara is well known from the Śruti and the *smṛti*. Thus with the words “Whatever Manu said is medicine” the Taittirīyas affirm Manu's trustworthiness. And the *smṛti* says that Śrī Parāśara understood the highest truth about the Deity by the grace of Pulastya and Vasiṣṭha. Kapila, the founder of the Sāṅkhya *smṛti* opposed to the Vedas, and Bhagavān Kapila, the son of Kardama, are not the same person. The first Kapila is a particular *jīva* of the line of Agni, deluded by *māyā*; the second, the son of Kardama, is an avatāra of Vāsudeva Himself. The *Padma Purāṇa* says: “Bhagavān Vāsudeva descended from the sage Kardama as Kapila and taught the doctrine of Sāṅkhya to the gods headed by Brahmā, to the sages headed by Bhṛgu and to a *brāhmaṇa* named Āsuri; the Sāṅkhya *smṛti* He taught is elaborated by the meaning of the Vedas. But another Kapila taught another Āsuri a different Sāṅkhya — opposed to all the Vedas, elaborated with false arguments and invented out of his own head.” Therefore there is no fault whatever in calling this latter Sāṅkhya *smṛti*, opposed to the Vedas and untrustworthy, useless.

2. Moreover, this Sāṅkhya *smṛti* speaks of certain matters that are not found in the Vedas. For this reason too it can be called “untrustworthy.” These are the matters: “The *puruṣas*, that is, the individual souls, are consciousness alone and all-pervading; *prakṛti* alone binds and liberates them. Both ‘bondage’ and ‘liberation’ are material. There is no *puruṣa* called ‘the Lord of all.’ ‘Time’ is not a *tattva* at all. The five *prāṇas* and the rest are merely functions of the senses.” These and certain other tenets opposed to Vedānta are seen in this Sāṅkhya *smṛti*.

## 26–28

Although Advaita Prabhu is the Viṣṇu-*tattva*, the one who is served, He has no other duty and no other conduct than this: to awaken in the *jīvas* the inclination to serve, for that is what securing their good means. Bhagavān Viṣṇu saw that if He displayed His pastimes only as the one who is served, the pleasure-seeking people of the world would imitate Him and become godless *kevalādvaita-vādīs* or *ahaṅgrahopāsakas*, who worship themselves as God. Therefore He manifested the pastimes of a servant; displaying this kind of ācāryaship is also one of His works. The Ācārya has no work other than conduct turned toward the service of Kṛṣṇa. He behaves as the servant of the One who is served — that is why He is the ācārya; this is a particular pastime of a *naimittika-avatāra*. Wicked people try to defile the Ācārya's holy place and His dress and, instead of serving Bhagavān, launch into a wild dance (*tāṇḍava*) of gratifying their own senses. The Ācārya condemns this utterly.

Śrī Advaita Ācārya is the guru of the whole Vaiṣṇava world, and everyone honors Him. Following His lotus feet, the Vaiṣṇavas devoted to Bhagavān adopt His conduct and serve Hari.
