# Chand Kazi
*Chand Kazi · @chand-kazi*

## The Ruler of Navadvipa
When Sri Chaitanya roused Navadvipa to the kirtana of the holy name, the town was ruled by a Muslim whom the original
sources call simply the Kazi. Krishnadasa Kaviraja also calls him by name, Chand Kazi (*chanda kaji*); Vrindavana Dasa
says only "the Kazi"; the “Bhakti-ratnakara” calls him "a yavana named the Kazi", and Govinda-deva in the
“Gaura-krishnodaya” "the yavana ruler" and "a mleccha". The biographies know him as Chand Kazi, and one of them,
mindful of the mercy he received, heads its account of him "Bhakta Chand Kazi", "Chand Kazi the devotee". His personal
name is given by Srila Bhaktisiddhanta Sarasvati in the “Anubhashya” in two ways: by some accounts he was called Maulana
Sirajuddin, by others Habibur Rahman.[^1] <!-- F1 F2 -->

In Navadvipa the Kazi was both judge and ruler. The “Charitamrita” calls him the magistrate; in their conversation the
Lord will say to him, "You are the Kazi; you have authority to oppose the Hindu religion," and the enemies of the
kirtana, "You are the lord of the whole town; all its people are yours." Srila Bhaktisiddhanta explains that the Kazi
was the *faujdar*, the head of the district. In those days the zamindars collected the revenue, while justice and
government were in the hands of the kazis, and both were under the *subadar* of Bengal; the parganas of Nadia, Islampur
and Bagoyan belonged to Hari Hor or to his descendant Krishnadasa Hor, but the Kazi had taken their government into his
own hands. The biographies speak of him as the local ruler who held power in his hands, and one of them also as the
local magistrate. The “Bhakti-ratnakara” relates that the yavana Kazi was very powerful, that Navadvipa and the country
around were under his sway, that the people of that land trembled before him, and that no one could freely worship God
(2.297–299). <!-- F3 -->

He was close to the ruler of all Gauda as well. The “Bhakti-ratnakara” says that the yavana king of Gauda loved him
dearly and no one dared disobey the Kazi (2.298). This king, according to the “Anubhashya”, was Ala-ud-din Saiyad Husain
Shah, at that time the independent ruler of Bengal (the “Anubhashya” gives the years of his reign as 1498–1511); the
same commentary says that Chand Kazi is said to have been the teacher (guru) of the Nawab Husain Shah. Srila
Bhaktivinoda Thakura in the “Amrita-pravaha-bhashya” and one of the biographies call the Padshah the Kazi's kinsman. And
the Lord Himself, threatening the Kazi, will say: let Me see what his king will do (CB, Madhya 23.122, 400). <!-- F4 -->

The Kazi's house stood in the village of Simulia, on the edge of Nadia: there, according to the “Chaitanya-bhagavata”,
the night procession came before turning towards his house (Madhya 23.348, 359, 379). Srila Bhaktisiddhanta writes in
the “Gaudiya-bhashya” that Simulia lies a mile or so (one and a half kilometres) from the northern boundary of
Ganganagara, and that in Simulia there still remains the lane of Vamana-pukura, where the Kazi's house stood, a house
preserved to this day. Srila Bhaktivinoda speaks of the old house of the Kazi's family in the village of
Brahmana-pushkarini, whose other part adjoins Taranavasa, formerly called Bilva-pushkarini. Jayananda too (in a
retelling) remembers "the village of Simulia", where the Kazi's house was broken and from which the yavanas fled.
<!-- F7 -->

Nothing more is known of his life in the world. Neither the original sources nor the biographies name his parents or
the place and year of his birth, nor do they say when he left this world. He has no days in the Vaishnava calendar of
the Sri Chaitanya Saraswat Math. He appears in the narrative only in these days: when he forbade the kirtana, and on the
night of the great procession to his house. The “Charitamrita” places these pastimes in the years between the Lord's
sixteenth year and His sannyasa; Srila Bhaktivinoda calls this night "the night of the first town kirtana"; and by our
timeline these are the years 1509–1510, when the Lord was twenty-three or twenty-four. <!-- F6 F50 -->

## Kirtana in Every House
After His return from Gaya the Lord opened the holy name to all the townspeople. Vrindavana Dasa gives His words:

“‘Hare Krishna Hare Krishna Krishna Krishna Hare Hare / Hare Rama Hare Rama Rama Rama Hare Hare.’ The Lord went on:
‘This is the maha-mantra. Chant it, all of you, a fixed number of times. <…> Five or ten of you may gather in a house and
hold kirtana, clapping your hands.’” (CB, Madhya 23.76–77, 79)

Srila Bhaktivinoda adds that the Lord first told those living near Srivasa to chant the name, clapping their hands;
then mridangas and karatalas began to sound, and from that time the sankirtana spread from door to door. The
“Charitamrita” says:

“When the Lord gave His order to the townspeople, they began to hold sankirtana in every house: ‘*Hari haraye namah,
krishna yadavaya namah, gopala govinda rama sri-madhusudana*.’ The great sound of sankirtana rose with mridangas and
karatalas, and nothing could be heard but ‘Hari! Hari!’” (CC, Adi 17.121–123) <!-- F8 Q1 -->

The Lord, who seeks no honour for Himself and honours everyone (*amani manada*), embraced each one and asked them to
sing the names. In the homes of pious families there were mridangas and conches: before, they had been played only in
autumn or in the month of Chaitra, at pujas to Mahamaya performed for passing worldly gains. Now the same instruments
served the unceasing Hari-kirtana, and worldly people, hearing them, took the singers for musicians "doing puja to
Mahamaya out of season" and mocked them. So everything that had served worldly ends found its meaning in the service
of the Lord. <!-- I1 -->

## The Broken Mridanga
One day the Kazi was passing along a road where the name was being sung. Vrindavana Dasa relates:

“One day, by the will of Providence, the Kazi was going along that road and heard the sound of mridangas, karatalas and
conches. When the Kazi heard the loud sound of the holy name on every side, he at once remembered his own scriptures.
The Kazi said, ‘Seize them, seize them! Today I shall do my work. Let us see what your Nimai Acharya will do today.’
The townspeople fled in confusion. <…> Whomever the Kazi caught, he beat. He broke the mridanga and made an outrage at
the door.” (CB, Madhya 23.101–105)[^2]

The “Charitamrita” tells it otherwise: the Muslims complained to the Kazi about the kirtana, and he came to one of the
houses.

“In the evening the Kazi came angrily to one house, broke the mridanga and said to the people: ‘All this time no one
here has dared to follow Hindu ways openly. Now you carry on with such zeal — on whose strength, I would like to know?
Let no one hold kirtana anywhere in the town. Today I forgive you and go home. But if I find anyone holding kirtana
again, I will take all he owns and strip him of his caste.’” (CC, Adi 17.125–128)[^3] <!-- F9 F10 Q2 Q3 -->

Srila Bhaktivinoda explains these words: from the coming of Bakhtiyar Khilji until Chand Kazi, Hindu ways had been
held down in Navadvipa, and believers would say "Hari" only in secret and fall silent. In Vrindavana Dasa the Kazi
threatens: "Nadia has turned Hindu; when I catch them, I shall punish them. Today I forgive you, since night has
fallen; another day I shall take away your caste" (CB, Madhya 23.106–107), and Srila Bhaktisiddhanta explains that he
was threatening to convert the townspeople to his faith by force.[^4] The ban delighted all the unbelievers, and they
went on jeering at the devotees. <!-- F11 -->

From then on the Kazi went about the town with his men every day, looking for kirtana — according to the
“Chaitanya-bhagavata”, "with thousands of his followers" (Madhya 23.108, 116) — and the townspeople hid from him. Srila
Bhaktisiddhanta explains that he was helped in his search by sinful townspeople who envied the devotees. On his side
too stood the unbelievers (*pashandis*), whom the acharya calls "so-called Hindus".[^5] They said, "The name of Hari
should be repeated in the mind," and rejoiced that the Kazi would "crush the pride of Nimai Pandita", and that "the
joy of Nityananda", who went freely about the town preaching the name, "would soon come to nothing" (23.110–113). The
frightened devotees came to the Lord:

“‘We can no longer hold kirtana, for we fear the Kazi. With thousands of his followers he prowls everywhere. Soon we
shall have to leave Navadvipa and go wherever our eyes lead us.’” (CB, Madhya 23.116–117) <!-- F12 -->

## "Today I Shall Hold Kirtana Throughout the Town"
According to the “Charitamrita”, the townspeople told the Lord everything, and He answered them as only He can who has
come to give the world sankirtana:

“The Lord ordered, ‘Go and hold kirtana! Today I shall destroy all the Muslims.’” (CC, Adi 17.130)

Vrindavana Dasa tells that when the Lord heard of the obstacle to the kirtana, He blazed with anger like Rudra: "Today I
shall hold kirtana throughout Navadvipa. Let Me see who will do anything to Me! Today let Me see — I shall burn the
Kazi's house and door. Let Me see what his king will do! Today I shall pour down a vast rain of prema-bhakti" (CB,
Madhya 23.118, 121–123). This anger is no worldly enmity, nor enmity towards Muslims. Srila Bhaktisiddhanta explains
that the teaching to be "humbler than grass" does not mean looking on indifferently while the service of Krishna is
opposed; and the rain of prema-bhakti that the Lord promises is, for the unbelievers, the very death of their
unbelief.[^6] <!-- F13 Q4 -->

The townspeople went home and went on singing, but fear of the Kazi did not leave their hearts. Seeing this, the Lord
called them together:

“‘Today I shall hold kirtana throughout the town. By evening, all of you, decorate the town. In the evening light lamps
in every house, and we shall see what Kazi comes to stop Me!’” (CC, Adi 17.133–134)

According to the “Chaitanya-bhagavata”, everyone who wished to see the power of Krishna was
to take a great torch, and the Lord heartened them all: "Countless universes are the servants of My servants. What is
there to fear, when I am with you?" (Madhya 23.125–127). The “Bhakti-ratnakara” recalls that every house held a
festival, hundreds of thousands of lamps burned, and the night became like day (2.303–304). <!-- F14 -->

## The Night Procession
In the evening the Lord went out of His house and divided the kirtana into three parts. The “Charitamrita” names them:

“Haridasa danced in the first group, and in the middle one Acharya Gosani danced in great joy. In the last group danced
Gaurachandra, and Nityananda Prabhu danced along with Him.” (CC, Adi 17.136–137)[^7] <!-- F15 -->

With the Lord went Gadadhara, Vakreshvara, Murari, Srivasa, Gopinatha and countless other servants of His; Vrindavana
Dasa names many of the singers (CB, Madhya 23.150–152). The biographies, telling of other associates, remember that on
that night the Lord was accompanied by Nityananda and all the Vaishnavas, by Srivasa, Vakreshvara, Advaita, who led one
of the parts of the procession, Chandrashekhara, Gangadasa, Nandana Acharya, Murari, to whom one of the leading roles in
the procession is given, Jagadisha, Govinda Ghosha, Vasudeva Datta, Haridasa at the head of one of the parts, and
Gadadhara Pandita.[^8] <!-- F16 -->

Krishnadasa Kaviraja says that Vrindavana Dasa described these pastimes at length (Adi 17.138), and himself gives only
the essentials:

“So, holding kirtana, He wandered through the town, and in His wandering He came to the Kazi's door. The people shouted
threats and made a great uproar: under Gaurachandra's protection they had grown wild with boldness. At the sound of the
kirtana the Kazi hid in his house, and hearing the threats and roars, he would not come out.” (CC, Adi 17.139–141)

Vrindavana Dasa traces the route of the procession from ghata to ghata along the Ganga: from the Lord's own ghata to
Madhai's ghata, past Barakona-ghata and Nagariya-ghata, through Ganganagara to Simulia (CB, Madhya 23.299–300, 348);
the “Anubhashya” lists them in the same way. <!-- F17 -->

In Vrindavana Dasa the Kazi, hearing singing and music from afar, first wonders, "Is it a wedding, or a kirtana of
ghosts?" and sends his servants to find out who has broken his ban. The servants come back in fear: millions of people
with blazing torches are shouting "Death to the Kazi!", and at their head is Nimai, now weeping, now so fierce that He
seems ready to swallow everyone. The Kazi thinks that Nimai Pandita is going to be married, and vows to strip of caste
all who have broken his ban — but then the thunder of the kirtana breaks out, and he and his servants run about not
knowing where to flee, "like a frog or a mouse trying in fear to escape a snake". Some of the servants threw off their
turbans, mingled with the crowd and, unrecognised, trembling with fear, danced in the kirtana (CB, Madhya 23.359–386).
Srila Bhaktisiddhanta explains that at first the Kazi took the procession for a wedding or some festivity. <!-- F18 -->

Then the crowd fell upon the Kazi's house:

“Unruly people broke into the Kazi's house and flower garden; Vrindavana dasa has described this at length.” (CC, Adi
17.142)

In Vrindavana Dasa the Lord in His anger demands that the Kazi be brought and orders his house to be broken and set on
fire, and the people smash rooms and doors, break the mango and jackfruit trees and tear up the flower garden; the
“Bhakti-ratnakara” too says that with the cry "Beat the Kazi!" the people broke his house, gate and flower garden
(2.308–309). But here too the Lord's anger is only His play. The devotees, with cloth wrapped round their necks, fell at
His feet and began to pray to Him, reminding Him that the Vedas call Him free from anger and ever blissful:

“‘You have punished the Kazi enough. If he obstructs the kirtana again, then You may kill him.’” (CB, Madhya 23.414)

“Hearing the prayers of His devotees, Mahaprabhu smiled. Then He left that place, chanting the name of Hari and
delighting in the bliss of the dance.” (CB, Madhya 23.417)

And so Vrindavana Dasa's account of the Kazi ends: the procession moves on.[^9] <!-- F19 -->

## A Guest at the Kazi's Door
What happened next Vrindavana Dasa did not describe, and the “Charitamrita” fills out his account (Adi 17.138, 142);
so Srila Bhaktivinoda too explains in his introduction to the chapter. The wrecking of the house and the devotees'
prayers we know from Vrindavana Dasa; the Lord's conversation with the Kazi, from Krishnadasa Kaviraja. <!-- F45 -->

“Then Mahaprabhu sat down at his door and sent respectable men to call the Kazi. The Kazi approached from a distance,
his head bowed, and the Lord received him with honour and gave him a seat.” (CC, Adi 17.143–144)

Srila Bhaktisiddhanta notes that the Lord honoured the Kazi according to worldly custom. The Lord, who seeks no honour
for Himself and honours everyone, approached the Kazi too not by force but by courtesy: He called him through
respectable men.[^10] <!-- F20 I2 -->

“The Lord said, ‘I have come to you as a guest, yet when you saw Me you hid. What kind of conduct is this?’ The Kazi
said, ‘You came in anger, so I stayed hidden to let You grow calm. Now that You are calm, I have come to meet You. How
fortunate I am to receive a guest like You!’” (CC, Adi 17.145–147) <!-- F21 -->

And the Kazi reminded the Lord of the kinship that joined them through their village:

“‘By village kinship Chakravarti is my uncle, and village kinship is truer than kinship of the body. Nilambara
Chakravarti is Your maternal grandfather, so by that relation You are my nephew. An uncle will bear his nephew's
anger, and a nephew does not hold his uncle's offences against him.’” (CC, Adi 17.148–150)

In the Bengali the Kazi says that by village kinship (*grama-sambandhe*) Chakravarti is his *chacha*, his paternal
uncle; the “Anubhashya” explains that Nilambara Chakravarti is meant. And Srila Bhaktivinoda explains where this kinship
came from: the old house of the Kazi's family stands in one part of the village of Brahmana-pushkarini, and its other
part adjoins Taranavasa, the former Bilva-pushkarini; this was one village, and so the Lord was the Kazi's "nephew".
Later the Lord too will call him "uncle" (*mama*, Adi 17.172).[^11] <!-- F5 -->

“So the two spoke in hints, and no one could grasp the inner meaning.” (CC, Adi 17.151)

What these hints concealed Krishnadasa Kaviraja does not say, and we shall not interpret them ourselves. <!-- F22 -->

“The Lord said, ‘I have come to ask you a question,’ and the Kazi replied, ‘Ask whatever is on Your mind.’” (CC, Adi
17.152) <!-- F23 -->
