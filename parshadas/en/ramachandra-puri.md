# Ramachandra Puri
*Рамачандра Пури · @ramachandra-puri*

## A Disciple of Madhavendra Puri
Ramachandra Puri was a sannyasi of the Puri order and an initiated disciple of Srila Madhavendra Puri. His name
entered the “Chaitanya-charitamrita” not through the glory of service but as a lesson. Krishnadasa Kaviraja Gosvami
tells of him in the eighth chapter of the Antya-lila: this disciple disregarded his guru, lost his mercy, and began
to revile everyone around him, and in the end Mahaprabhu Himself. That is why the biographies say that his life
teaches “by contraries”: it shows what one who seeks perfection must beware of. <!-- F2 I1 -->

The “Charitamrita” calls him Ramachandra Puri, and at times simply “Puri,” “Gosani” or “this sannyasi”; Vrindavana
Dasa Thakura in the “Chaitanya-bhagavata” and Kavi Karnapura in the “Gaura-ganoddesha-dipika” likewise call him
Ramachandra Puri. The biographies name him Sri Ramachandra Puri, Ramachandra Puri Gosani or Puri Gosani. <!-- F1 -->

Nothing is known of his life before sannyasa: the sources name neither his parents nor the place of his birth. The
years, days and places of his appearance and disappearance are also unknown, nor are they found in the Vaishnava
calendar of the Sri Chaitanya Saraswat Math. We know only that he was alive when Mahaprabhu came to Varanasi on His
way to Vrindavana, and later, when the Lord lived in Nilachala. <!-- F7 -->

He received initiation from Madhavendra Puri: Kaviraja plainly calls him the great Puri’s disciple (Antya 8.16–18).
But his discipleship was outward only. One of the biographies calls him a sannyasi who merely “considered himself a
disciple” of Srila Madhavendra, and Srila Bhaktisiddhanta Sarasvati Thakura, in his “Gaudiya-bhashya” on the
“Chaitanya-bhagavata,” calls him “a pretended disciple of Madhavendra Puri with a strong leaning towards mayavada.”
Another biography adds that, although he had such a guru, he kept close company with those who follow the path of dry
knowledge and was drawn to doctrines opposed to pure bhakti. Kaviraja himself speaks of him as a “dry knower of
Brahman” with no relationship with Krishna (Antya 8.25). <!-- F3 -->

Outwardly, however, he was a sannyasi of a recognised order — according to one of the biographies, of one of the ten
orders that trace themselves to Shankara — and lived the life of a wandering renunciant: the “Charitamrita” calls him
“a sannyasi” (Antya 8.5) and says that, detached by nature, he never stayed long in one place (8.36). This matters for
his whole story. As a sannyasi and a disciple of Madhavendra he was by custom entitled to respect, and the Lord,
keeping maryada — the proper order of honour — showed him that respect. <!-- F4 -->

## At the Bedside of His Departing Guru
What his discipleship came to, Kaviraja shows in the last days of Madhavendra Puri. The great Puri, the first sprout
of the tree of bhakti, was leaving this world chanting the names of Krishna and weeping that he had not reached
Mathura. This was not the worldly grief of a dying man. Madhavendra was in separation from Krishna — in the mood of
Srimati Radhika when Krishna left Her for Mathura — in the highest, transcendental separation, vipralambha. Both his
disciples were with him, Ishvara Puri and Ramachandra Puri.[^1]

“When his guru, Madhavendra Puri, was about to depart, Ramachandra Puri came to him. Madhavendra Puri was chanting the
names of Krishna, and at times he wept: ‘I have not attained Mathura.’ Then Ramachandra Puri began to instruct him;
though he was the disciple, he spoke to his guru without fear: ‘You are the full bliss of Brahman — remember Brahman!
You know Brahman; why do you weep?’” (CC, Antya 8.16–19) <!-- F8 Q4 -->

A dry knower (*shushka-jnani*) of Brahman, he overstepped the reverence due to the guru and was not afraid to teach
the one before whom a disciple ought to bow. He did not understand his guru’s mood and saw in him an ordinary man.
Srila Bhaktisiddhanta, in his “Anubhashya,” as the biographies report, explains: looking at his guru suffering in
separation from Krishna, Ramachandra Puri judged by worldly reasoning that he was grieving over some material lack,
and hurried to make him realise the impersonal Brahman. Here lies his whole error. Transcendental separation from
Krishna — the highest treasure of a devotee — he took for worldly sorrow, and as its cure he offered knowledge of a
Brahman in which there is no Krishna. <!-- F9 F11 Q15 -->

Madhavendra answered him in anger:

“Hearing this, Madhavendra Puri rebuked him in great anger: ‘Away, away, you sinner! I have not obtained Krishna’s
mercy, I have not reached Mathura; I am dying of my own grief — and this one has come to burn me still more. Do not
show me your face; go wherever you please. If I die looking at you, an evil end awaits me. Not having attained
Krishna, I am dying of my own sorrow — and this vile fool comes to teach me about “Brahman”!’” (CC, Antya
8.20–23)[^2] <!-- F10 -->

Seeing his disciple’s folly and his disregard for the guru, Madhavendra, in the words of Srila Bhaktisiddhanta, ceased
to wish him well, rejected him and drove him away. And Kaviraja tells what was born of this:

“Because Sri Madhavendrapada disregarded him, from that offence a ‘desire’ was born in him. A dry knower of Brahman,
without relationship with Krishna, he reviles all people and is set on reviling.” (CC, Antya 8.24–25)[^3]

His guru disregarded him — and a *vasana*, a desire, was born in him; Srila Bhaktisiddhanta, as the biographies
report, explains that it was the desire for dry knowledge. And from dry knowledge grew the reviling of devotees: in
the dry Brahman there is no relationship with Krishna, and a heart without that relationship clings stubbornly to
seeking out the faults of others. So the biographies say: from offence at the guru’s feet come fault-finding,
reviling of others and a taste for dry knowledge. <!-- F12 I7 -->

Quite otherwise did another disciple serve the guru — Ishvara Puri, the future guru of Mahaprabhu. With his own hands
he cleaned away Madhavendra’s stool and urine, constantly chanted the name of Krishna to him and told him of Krishna’s
pastimes, and Madhavendra embraced him and blessed him: “May you obtain the treasure of love for Krishna” (Antya
8.26–28). Kaviraja sums it up:

“Thus Ishvara Puri became an ocean of prema, and Ramachandra Puri a reviler of all. These two are witnesses to the
mercy and the disfavour of a great soul; through these two Madhavendra Puri taught the people of the world.” (CC,
Antya 8.29–30)

The Bengali says *anugraha* and *nigraha*: the mercy and the disfavour of a great soul. One disciple served — and
became an ocean of prema; the other proudly instructed his guru — and became “a reviler of all.” One of the
biographies even calls him “a mine of offences.” <!-- F13 Q5 -->

Madhavendra himself departed reciting the verse “*ayi dina-dayardra natha*” — “O Lord, tender towards the wretched, O
Master of Mathura, when shall I see You?..” — and in leaving he planted in this world the sprout of love for Krishna;
the tree that grew from it was Sri Chaitanya Mahaprabhu Himself (Antya 8.31–34). <!-- F14 -->

## The Matha in Varanasi
When Mahaprabhu, on His way to Vrindavana, came to Varanasi, He did not reveal Himself to the sannyasis there.
Vrindavana Dasa Thakura explains: sannyasis devoid of devotion cannot see the Lord. The sannyasis of Kashi were
learned in the “Vedanta,” lived austerely and were greatly honoured, but in expounding the “Vedanta” they said
nothing of loving service to Vishnu. Therefore the Lord, the Supersoul of all, who knows everything, did not give
them His darshana (CB, Madhya 19.99–104):

“Coming to Varanasi, He stayed for two months, hidden in the matha of Ramachandra Puri.” (CB, Madhya 19.105)[^4]

And two days before the festival of Vishvarupa-kshaura the Lord left secretly, unnoticed by anyone (19.106). This is
the only mention of Ramachandra Puri in the “Chaitanya-bhagavata.” <!-- F43 Q13 -->

Srila Bhaktisiddhanta, in his “Gaudiya-bhashya” on this verse, explains: in fact Sri Gaurasundara lived in Varanasi
in the house of Chandrashekhara, a vaidya by caste whom people called a shudra. Vrindavana Dasa knew that the Lord
“hid” in the matha of Ramachandra Puri — and Ramachandra Puri was a pretended disciple of Madhavendra Puri with a
strong leaning towards mayavada. The Lord openly announced that He was living in his matha, while He Himself lived
elsewhere, with the devotees of Krishna. Ramachandra Puri was a sannyasi of a recognised order,[^5] and a sannyasi’s
staying in his matha gave the outside world no cause for reproach; in the words of one of the biographies, the Lord
thus avoided the censure of non-devotees. The maryada of sannyasa was kept outwardly, while the Lord’s heart was with
His devotees. <!-- F44 Q15 -->

## In Nilachala
Years passed. Mahaprabhu was living in Nilachala with His devotees, immersed in the waves of love for Krishna, when
Ramachandra Puri came there to see Him and Paramananda Puri (CC, Antya 8.4–5). Both received him as a disciple of
Madhavendra:

“Paramananda Puri bowed at the feet of Ramachandra Puri, and he embraced him firmly. Mahaprabhu too offered him
obeisances, and he embraced Him as well, remembering Krishna. The three of them spent some time in talk about God;
then Jagadananda Pandita invited him.” (CC, Antya 8.6–8)[^6] <!-- F15 F16 F18 -->

One of the biographies explains here the custom of sannyasis: they answer an obeisance with the words “*om namo
bhagavate narayanaya*” and remember Krishna, for it does not befit a sannyasi either to bless a living being or to
bow to one. <!-- F17 -->

Jagadananda brought Jagannatha’s prasada for his guest. Ramachandra Puri ate his fill — and, as Kaviraja remarks, did
so in order to have something to censure afterwards (Antya 8.9). When he had eaten, he told Jagadananda to finish the
remnants, sat him down himself, insistently served him himself and again and again pressed him to eat more
(8.10–12). And when Jagadananda had at last washed his hands, the sannyasi began to reproach him:

“‘I had heard that the followers of Chaitanya eat a great deal; it is true — now I have seen it myself. To feed a
sannyasi like this is to ruin his dharma; and where is the renunciation of a renunciant who eats so much?’” (CC,
Antya 8.13–14) <!-- F19 Q3 -->

He took the truly detached companions of Gaura for men without detachment. Nor was this a chance reproach; it was his
habit:

“Such was Ramachandra Puri’s nature: first to feed someone to the full, and then to censure him.” (CC, Antya 8.15)

Later Paramananda Puri would say the same: “You must love to eat your fill — where do you get so much money?” — so,
by his account, Ramachandra Puri used to rebuke those whom he himself had just overfed (8.71–73). <!-- F20 -->

One of the biographies sees in this story a deliberate contrast. Before us is Jagadananda Pandita, whose whole life
is sincere love for the Lord, and beside him the hypocrisy of Ramachandra Puri: he was Madhavendra’s disciple only in
form, had no inclination to serve, and for his insincerity and show of piety in the end lost the mercy of his guru.
<!-- F21 -->

## Fault-Finding
Ramachandra Puri stayed on in Nilachala and set himself to seeking out the faults of others. He ate wherever he
could, coming without invitation, and inquired where the sannyasis lived, what they did, and who ate how much (CC,
Antya 8.36–37); according to one of the biographies, he especially rebuked the renunciants for eating sweets.
<!-- F22 -->

In Nilachala there was at that time the following custom. Every day the devotees invited the Lord to their homes,
and what was offered for one invitation sufficed for three — Mahaprabhu, Kashishvara and Govinda. One who did not
cook for the Lord himself but wished to pay for His meal gave four panas of cowries, that is, three hundred and
twenty shells, and with them Jagannatha’s prasada was bought (8.38–39). <!-- F23 -->

All this Ramachandra Puri now set about finding out concerning the Lord — His way of life, His rules, how much He ate
and slept:

“Seeking only faults, he could not touch the Lord’s virtues; he searched for a fault and found none anywhere. Then he
invented a charge: ‘How can sannyasis who eat sweets control their senses?’” (CC, Antya 8.41–42)

He did not value the Lord’s glory and saw in Him an ordinary man. He reviled Him before everyone — and yet came to Him
every day (8.43). <!-- F24 -->

But the Lord answered him with reverence:

“The Lord, regarding him as a guru, showed him reverence and honour; but he went about looking for faults — that was
his business. The Lord knew of all the reviling he did, yet He received him with great reverence.” (CC, Antya
8.44–45)

The Bengali says *guru-buddhye*: the Lord saw a guru in him. One of the biographies explains that He always bowed to
him as His guru’s godbrother. <!-- F25 -->

One morning Ramachandra Puri came to the Lord’s dwelling and saw ants there:

“‘Last night there was something of sugar-cane here, and so the ants are crawling about. Alas! Such sensual craving in
renounced sannyasis!’ Saying this, he got up and left.” (CC, Antya 8.47)

Kaviraja gives these words in Sanskrit prose; the “sweet” in the original is *aikshavam*, something made from sugar
cane — molasses. Before, the Lord had heard of his reviling from others; now He heard it Himself (8.48). And the
charge was invented: ants crawl everywhere, and Ramachandra Puri raised the matter only in order to accuse (8.49). One
of the biographies says: finding no fault in the all-auspicious Gaurasundara, he saw a line of ants in His room for
bhajana, suspected that the Lord had been eating sweets, slandered Him — and fell, as every reviler of the guru and
the Vaishnavas falls. <!-- F26 -->

## “Out of Fear of Ramachandra Puri”
Kaviraja opens the eighth chapter of the Antya-lila with this verse:

“I worship that Krishna Chaitanya who, out of fear of Ramachandra Puri, reduced His own alms-food in His worldly
eating.” (CC, Antya 8.1) <!-- Q2 -->

Hearing about the ants, the Lord did just that:

“Hearing this, the Lord felt hesitation and fear in His mind. He called Govinda and spoke to him: ‘From today this is
the rule for My alms: one quarter of a pinda-bhoga pot, and vegetables for five gandas. If you take anything more for
Me, you will not see Me here.’” (CC, Antya 8.50–52)

The word “fear” is the word of the “Charitamrita” itself. The opening verse says *ramachandra-puri-bhayat*, “out of
fear of Ramachandra Puri”; verse 8.50 says *sankocha-bhaya*, “hesitation and fear”; and Kaviraja recalls the “fear of
Ramachandra Puri” in other places of the book as well (Madhya 1.266, Antya 10.156, 20.115).[^7] The word must be
rendered exactly — and understood rightly. The Lord is the Master of maya, and fear has no power over Him. One of the
biographies says so plainly: fear and hesitation arose in Mahaprabhu, the Teacher of the world who had taken the role
of an acharya. Nor is it by chance that the verse specifies that the Lord reduced His alms *laukikaharatah* — in His
“ordinary” eating, the eating visible to the world. This was the play of the Teacher: He was teaching humility and
forbearance under reproach, reverence for an elder in whom He saw a guru, and the dharma of a sannyasi, which knows no
greed of the tongue. And the “Gaura-ganoddesha-dipika,” as we shall see, reveals in this pastime yet another, hidden
cause. <!-- F27 -->

Govinda announced the new rule to the devotees:

“Govinda told all the devotees of Mahaprabhu; hearing it, they felt as if a pitiless thunderbolt had struck their
heads. All of them reviled Ramachandra Puri: ‘This sinner has come to take away everyone’s life!’” (CC, Antya
8.53–54) <!-- F28 -->

That same day a brahmana invited the Lord. Govinda took only one *chauthi* — a quarter-pot of rice — and vegetables
for five gandas, and the brahmana in his grief beat his head. The Lord ate half of the rice and vegetables and Govinda
received the other half; so both of them now ate half. Seeing this, the devotees gave up food and drink, and the Lord
told Govinda and Kashishvara, considering them His own, to take their meals elsewhere so as not to go hungry (CC,
Antya 8.55–59). <!-- F29 -->

Kaviraja returns to these days more than once. In the summary of the Madhya-lila he says that the Lord reduced His
alms out of fear of Ramachandra Puri, and then, seeing the sorrow of the Vaishnavas, kept half of what He had taken
before (Madhya 1.266); in the review of the Antya-lila, that the eighth chapter describes the coming of Ramachandra
Puri and how out of fear of him the Lord reduced His alms (Antya 20.115). And telling of the devotees who fed the
Lord, he recalls:

“At first the fixed sum was four panas of cowries; out of fear of Ramachandra Puri the price of an invitation was
lowered.” (CC, Antya 10.156) <!-- F41 -->

## A Lesson on the Dharma of Sannyasa
So several bitter days passed. Hearing of what had happened, Ramachandra Puri came to the Lord to teach Him the dharma
of a sannyasi. The Lord bowed at his feet, and he said with a smile (CC, Antya 8.60–61):

“‘To gratify the senses is not the dharma of a sannyasi; he fills his belly with whatever comes. I hear that You now
eat half; I see that You have grown thin. Such dry renunciation is not the dharma of a sannyasi either. A sannyasi
eats as much as is fitting, but does not enjoy the objects of the senses; so he attains perfection in jnana-yoga.
“Yoga is not for one who eats too much, Arjuna, nor for one who does not eat at all, nor for one who sleeps too much,
nor for one who keeps awake too long. For one who is moderate in food and recreation, measured in his deeds, measured
in sleep and waking, yoga becomes the destroyer of sorrow.”’” (CC, Antya 8.62–66; “Bhagavad-gita” 6.16–17)
<!-- F30 F31 Q6 -->

He preached “fitting renunciation” (*yukta-vairagya*) according to the “Gita” — but as the rule of a sannyasi on the
path of knowledge (*jnana-yoga*), not as service to Krishna: dry knowledge, like dry renunciation, is the opposite of
bhakti. And it was he himself who called “dry renunciation” what his own reproaches had brought about. But the Lord,
keeping amanitva — seeking no honour for Himself — answered humbly:

“The Lord said: ‘I am an ignorant boy, your disciple. That you give Me instruction is My good fortune.’” (CC, Antya
8.67) <!-- F32 -->

This humility too is a lesson to the world: the Lord, the Teacher of all teachers, calls Himself the disciple of one
who has come to lecture Him.

“Hearing this, Ramachandra Puri got up and went away. And Gosani heard that the devotees too were eating only half.”
(CC, Antya 8.68)[^8] <!-- F33 -->

## Paramananda Puri’s Plea
The next day Paramananda Puri, together with all the devotees, humbly approached the Lord (CC, Antya 8.69):

“‘Ramachandra Puri is a reviler by nature. What will be gained by giving up food because of his words?’” (CC, Antya
8.70)[^9]

His habit, said Puri, is first to overfeed and then to censure; he keeps constant watch over who eats how much and
what each one does, and does exactly what the scriptures forbid (8.71–75). For the “Bhagavata” teaches:

“‘One should neither praise nor censure the nature and actions of others.’” (CC, Antya 8.76; “Srimad-Bhagavatam”
11.28.1)

Of these two prohibitions — the earlier “do not praise” and the later “do not censure” — the later is the stronger:
such is the rule of nyaya that Puri cites (8.78). To praise is not so dangerous, but to censure is forbidden.
Ramachandra keeps only the first — he praises no one — and breaks the second by censuring everyone (8.77). According
to one of the biographies, Srila Bhaktisiddhanta, discussing this rule, remarks that the verse is cited here with
irony. And Puri went on:

“This offender does not see good qualities even in one who has hundreds of them; rather, he finds a way to call them
faults.” (CC, Antya 8.79)

One should not imitate him, said Puri, and he spoke of him only because his heart ached; and all of them begged the
Lord to eat as was fitting and to accept their invitations as before (8.80–81). <!-- F34 Q7 -->

The Lord answered as the Teacher of the people:

“The Lord said: ‘Why are you all angry with Puri? He speaks the natural dharma — what fault is there in him? For a
sannyasi, indulgence of the tongue is a great fault; a sannyasi should eat only so much as keeps him alive.’” (CC,
Antya 8.82–83)

“What fault is there in him?” is neither approval of his teaching nor a justification of his reviling. The Lord would
not let the devotees be angry with an elder and taught them forbearance, and by His own example taught that greed of
the tongue is unworthy of a sannyasi. <!-- F35 -->

When the devotees together pressed Him insistently, the Lord gave way and agreed to take half of what He had taken
before: from then on two panas of cowries, one hundred and sixty shells, were given for an invitation, and this was
usually enough for two, or even three (8.84–85). <!-- F36 -->

If the Lord was invited by a brahmana from whom cooked food could not be accepted (*abhojyanna-vipra*), prasada was
bought for two panas; but if by a brahmana from whom one could eat, he bought part of the prasada and cooked part at
home (8.86–87). <!-- F37 Q8 -->

But for His dearest ones there was no such rule:

“When Gadadhara Pandita, Bhagavan Acharya or Sarvabhauma Bhattacharya invited Him, even on a day when someone else had
invited Him, He accepted their invitation; He ate at their homes as they wished, for He was subject to the wishes of
His intimate companions.” (CC, Antya 8.88–89)[^10] <!-- F38 -->

## “Now Almost a Servant, Now a Blade of Grass”
How, then, did the Lord treat Ramachandra Puri? Kaviraja answers:

“Sometimes He behaved according to worldly custom, like an ordinary man; sometimes, independent, He displayed His
majesty. Sometimes He was almost a servant to Ramachandra Puri; sometimes He paid him no regard, looking on him as a
blade of grass. The character of the Lord is beyond the reach of the intellect; whatever He does at any time is all
charming.” (CC, Antya 8.91–93)[^11]

Now He was almost his servant, now He took no more account of him than of a blade of grass. The reverence that the
Lord showed by worldly custom to a sannyasi and a disciple of Madhavendra was His lila and a lesson in maryada, not a
recognition that Ramachandra Puri was right; and His disregard was a display of His independence. All the deeds of
the inconceivable Lord, say the biographies, are eternal, auspicious and beautiful at all times and in all
circumstances. <!-- F39 Q9 I9 -->

Having lived for a while in Nilachala, Ramachandra Puri went away:

“After staying a few days in Nilachala, Ramachandra Puri set out again on pilgrimage. All the devotees felt as though
a heavy stone had been lifted from their heads. Once more the Lord freely accepted invitations and danced and sang as
before, and everyone freely partook of prasada.” (CC, Antya 8.94–96)

One of the biographies says that he left the shelter of the Lord, while the devotees, whose life is Gaura, gave up
the worldly rule of dry renunciation and again served the Lord with their whole being. The sources tell nothing more
of him: where he went and when he left this world is unknown. <!-- F40 -->

## The Lesson
Kaviraja closes his account of him thus:

“When the guru disregards one, such is the fruit: step by step one comes to offend even the Lord Himself.” (CC, Antya
8.97)

And he adds: although the Lord, seeing a guru in him, did not take his offence to heart, through its fruit He taught
the people (8.98).[^12] The biographies unfold this lesson: disregard of the guru breeds in the jiva enmity towards
Vishnu and godlessness, and step by step the offence reaches the Lord Himself; Mahaprabhu showed this to the world
“through the conduct of the offender Ramachandra.” <!-- F42 Q10 -->

Not all the disciples of one true guru stand on the same step, the biographies continue. One may outwardly take
shelter and receive the mantra and still not become a disciple. A true disciple understands the guru’s severity as a
blessing for himself and for others. As one of them puts it, the guru rewards the disciple who has given himself to
loving service, and only such a disciple is able to recognise the guru’s mercy: not for nothing is service to the
guru with trust (*vishrambhena guroh seva*) among the most important of the sixty-four angas of bhakti. <!-- I5 -->

Whoever transgresses the reverence due to the guru and the Vaishnavas puts obstacles on his own path of bhakti. A
jiva with anarthas, intoxicated by daivi-maya, thinks himself higher and more learned than others and in his pride
undertakes to correct and instruct the guru and the Vaishnavas. But the Lord does not tolerate a breach of maryada:

“‘Jagadananda Pandita is dear to Me, but you, Sanatana, are dearer still. Yet I cannot bear it when anyone breaks the
maryada.’” (CC, Antya 4.166) <!-- I2 Q12 -->

Most ruinous of all for bhakti is the seeking out of others’ faults, and above all the reviling of Vishnu and the
Vaishnavas. A sadhaka should look at his own failings — then he will advance in bhajana. The “Charitamrita” warns:

“But if an offence against a Vaishnava arises, it is like a mad elephant: it uproots or tears the creeper, and its
leaves wither and dry up.” (CC, Madhya 19.156) <!-- I6 Q11 -->

The guru’s godbrother is bound to the guru and is therefore worthy of honour like the guru. Even if his words seem
wrong, one may not treat him rudely or reproach him; one must keep the outward etiquette and not address him with
open criticism. So Mahaprabhu taught by His conduct towards Ramachandra Puri. <!-- I4 -->

And to grow in bhakti one must associate with Vaishnavas of like mind (*samashaya*) and serve them: the inclination
to serve Vishnu and the Vaishnavas grows in the company of those in whom it is stronger. <!-- I3 -->

And lastly: it is not enough to call oneself a follower of Gaura. Jagadananda Pandita says in the “Prema-vivarta”:

“‘I am Gora’s, I am Gora’s’ — words alone will not do: it bears fruit if one adopts Gora’s conduct and Gora’s
teaching.” (“Prema-vivarta” 8.6) <!-- I8 Q14 -->

So the Lord, the Teacher of the world, was teaching the people when He reduced His food, when He humbly called
Himself Ramachandra Puri’s disciple, and when He defended him before the devotees: He taught amanitva, the dharma of
a sannyasi who does not indulge the tongue, and where offence against the guru leads. <!-- I10 -->

## Vibhishana
Who Ramachandra Puri is in the eternal pastimes, Kavi Karnapura tells us:

“He who was formerly Vibhishana is considered to be Ramachandra Puri; therefore Gaurahari said: ‘The cause of this is
not Rama.’ For the sake of the work, Jatila, Radhika’s mother-in-law, entered into him; and therefore Mahaprabhu then
reduced His alms and so on.” (“Gaura-ganoddesha-dipika” 92)[^13] <!-- F5 Q1 -->

Vibhishana is Ravana’s brother and a great devotee of Lord Ramachandra. And into Ramachandra Puri, Karnapura says,
“for the sake of the work” (*karyatah*) Jatila, the mother-in-law of Srimati Radhika, entered — and so came about the
pastime in which the Lord reduced His food. This explains the pastime but does not justify the reviling: the lesson
that Kaviraja draws from the life of Ramachandra Puri, and the instruction of Srila Bhaktisiddhanta, remain in force.
<!-- F5 -->

The Vaishnava vandanas also remember him. The Sanskrit “Vaishnava-vandana” attributed to Jiva Gosvami praises
“Ramachandra Puri, [he] to whom the Lord always yielded” (1.58; the reading of this line is unclear); Devakinandana
praises his feet, for “the Lord said of him that he belonged to Sri Rama’s host” (2.45); Devakinandana’s
“Vaishnavabhidhana” names him in the list of sannyasis, between Nrisimha Tirtha and Brahmananda Puri (19); and the
Bengali vandana in tripadi, in the words: “seeing his vehemence, the Lord gave up everything” (42). The vandanas name
him among the sannyasis around the Lord — and no more. <!-- F6 -->

## Significance
Ramachandra Puri — the disciple of a great guru, a sannyasi of a recognised order, a man whom the Lord honoured as a
guru — lost everything through one thing: he did not see in his guru a devotee weeping in separation from Krishna,
and set out to teach him dry knowledge. Disregard of the guru took away his mercy, and from dry knowledge grew
reviling — first of the devotees, and then of the Lord Himself. <!-- F12 F42 I7 -->

Beside him Kaviraja places Ishvara Puri: service to the guru made him an ocean of prema. Two disciples of one guru —
the mercy and the disfavour of a great soul. And the Lord, the Teacher of the world, did not answer reviling with
anger: He honoured the elder, called Himself his disciple, did not let the devotees be angry with him — and through
his own deeds taught the world where offence against the guru and the Vaishnavas leads. <!-- F13 I1 I2 I6 I10 -->
