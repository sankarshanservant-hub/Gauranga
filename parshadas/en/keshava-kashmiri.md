# Keshava Kashmiri
*Кешава Кашмири · @keshava-kashmiri*

## The Conqueror of All Directions
In Sri Chaitanya's youth, when Nimai Pandita was the foremost teacher of Navadvipa, a famous scholar came to the town —
a *digvijayi*, a “conqueror of all directions.” He had travelled all over India defeating pandits in debate and had
come to defeat the celebrated scholars of Navadvipa as well; he left having given himself to the Lord's feet. The
“Chaitanya-bhagavata” and the “Chaitanya-charitamrita” do not name him: in their verses he is only “the digvijayi,”
“the great digvijayi,” “the poet,” “the brahmana.” The name comes from the commentaries. In the
“Amrita-pravaha-bhashya” Srila Bhaktivinoda Thakura calls him Keshava Mishra of Kashmir; Srila Bhaktisiddhanta
Sarasvati in the “Anubhashya” calls him Keshava, a pandit from Kashmir, and in the “Gaudiya-bhashya” Keshava Bhatta, or
Keshava Kashmiri. In the “Bhakti-ratnakara” he is Keshava Kashmir and also Laghu-Keshava, the “younger Keshava”; in
Nabhadasa's “Bhaktamal” and in the Vallabhite “Varta” he is Keshava Bhatta with the byname “Kashmiri.” The biographies
call him the digvijayi pandit Keshava Bhatta, “the invincible pandit,” and “the famous digvijayi Keshava
Kashmiri.”[^1] <!-- F1 -->

That the digvijayi of the “Chaitanya-bhagavata” is Keshava Kashmiri Srila Bhaktisiddhanta gives in the
“Gaudiya-bhashya” (on CB Adi 13.19) as the opinion of some, noting that on grounds of chronology opinions differ here;
in the “Anubhashya” (on CC Adi 16.25) he calls the digvijayi Keshava without any reservation. It is this tradition of
the Gaudiya Vaishnavas that we follow.[^2] <!-- F2 Q7 -->

Keshava came from Kashmir — “a most noble brahmana,” as the “Bhakti-ratnakara” says. The “Gaudiya-bhashya” names his
teacher in the Nimbarka sampradaya as Gangala Bhatta, while the “Bhakti-ratnakara” calls Keshava the “beloved disciple”
of Gokula Bhatta.[^3] The same “Bhakti-ratnakara” names Keshava Kashmiri the twenty-ninth in the line of disciples of
Nimbaditya, that is, Sri Nimbarka; Srila Bhaktisiddhanta in the “Anubhashya” lists all twenty-nine names of this line —
from Shrinivasacharya and Vishvacharya to Gopinatha, Keshava, Gokula and, last, Keshava Kashmiri. The sources give
neither the year nor the place of his appearance, and his days are not found in the Vaishnava calendar of the Sri
Chaitanya Saraswat Math. <!-- F3 F4 F5 -->

## Sarasvati's Blessing
Keshava was devoted heart and soul to Sarasvati and chanted her mantra without cease. She gave him her blessing: “You
will conquer the three worlds!” All the sciences opened within him, his power grew, and going from land to land he
defeated the scholars, so that he became renowned as a digvijayi. The “Bhakti-ratnakara” says the same: from the mantra
of Sarasvati all knowledge came to him, and he became a conqueror of all directions. <!-- F6 Q5 -->

But what is Sarasvati's blessing? Vrindavana Dasa Thakura says that Sarasvati is the very form of devotion to Vishnu;
she is not different from Lakshmi and dwells on the Lord's chest. Her merciful glance can give a person devotion to
Vishnu — so what difficulty is there for her in giving a scholar victory in debate? (CB Adi 13.21–23). The true
Sarasvati is the highest knowledge, the Lord's internal energy; she hides herself from those blinded by pride and by the
wish to be the doer and the enjoyer, and turns to them only through her shadow, which serves the Lord: such people
receive worldly gifts from her and are deluded by them. But to one on whom her guileless glance falls she gives the
highest good — devotion to Vishnu. The worldly fame of a world-conqueror is a trifle to her. So it was with Keshava:
the blessing he took for his goal became only the road to the true fruit of his mantra. <!-- I1 -->

With this gift Keshava wandered through the various lands of Bharatavarsha, wishing to defeat the pandits by his
talent. Later he himself would list for the Lord where he had been: Gauda and Trihuta, Delhi and Kashi, Gujarat and
Vijayanagara, Kanchi, Anga, Bengal, Andhra and Orissa — and everywhere the scholars could not even understand his
words, let alone defeat him (CB Adi 13.160–162). <!-- F9 F13 -->

## The Digvijayi in Navadvipa
Having defeated the assemblies of pandits in every kingdom, Keshava heard of the glory of Navadvipa, whose scholars
were famed throughout India, and came in great pride to overcome them too. He did not come alone: he had servants,
horses and elephants with him, and he carried a certificate of his victories over the scholars of every land of India
(CB Adi 13.28, 13.30). When the pandits of Navadvipa learned that the great digvijayi had come, they were thrown into
turmoil: alarm arose in every house and in every gathering of scholars. The “Bhakti-ratnakara” adds that the scholars
of Chakhandi, too, lost their peace. <!-- F10 F11 F12 -->

Krishnadasa Kaviraja places the meeting in time thus:

“Then came His marriage to Vishnupriya Thakurani, and after that the Lord's victory over the *digvijayi*, the
conqueror of all directions.” (CC Adi 16.25)[^4] <!-- F44 Q6 -->

The students told Nimai about the digvijayi, and He answered them in words that Vrindavana Dasa preserved in the
“Chaitanya-bhagavata”:

“When the Lord sees that someone is proud of his qualities, He takes away his cause for pride. A tree laden with fruit
and a man adorned with good qualities bow down to the ground — the tree from the weight of its fruit, the man from
humility. You have all heard of the great digvijayis of the past: Haihaya, Nahusha, Vena, Bana, Naraka and Ravana. Was
there ever one who was not stripped of his pride? Know that the Supreme Lord lets no one remain proud. Very soon you
will all see how here, in Navadvipa, the digvijayi's pride will be broken.” (CB Adi 13.44–48)[^5] <!-- F14 I2 -->

That evening the Lord sat with His students on the bank of the Ganga and thought to Himself not of how to defeat the
guest but of how to save him. If He overcame him before everyone, He reasoned, the disgrace would be like death to him:
people would mock him, plunder his goods, and he himself would die of grief. So the Lord resolved to defeat him away
from the crowd — to crush his pride and spare him unbearable pain (CB Adi 13.53–57). The full moon rose. Keshava came
to the bank, bowed to the Ganga, and learned from the students who this youth was, shining with extraordinary beauty:
“This is the famous Nimai Pandita.” And the conqueror of so many assemblies, seeing the Lord, felt timid (CB Adi
13.58–75; CC Adi 16.28–29). <!-- F15 F16 -->

## The Verses on the Ganga
The Lord received the guest with respect and gave him a seat of honour. But he, as the “Charitamrita” tells, answered
Him with disdain:

“You teach grammar, and Your name is Nimai Pandita. People praise Your skill in the science that children learn. Of
the grammars, I know, You teach the *Kalapa*, and I have heard of Your students' debates on its tricky points.”
(CC Adi 16.31–32)

The Lord humbly called Himself a child who had only just begun to study, and him an expert in every shastra and a poet,
and asked him to glorify the sacred Ganga in verse. The “Chaitanya-bhagavata” gives His request thus:

“O pandit, great is your poetic gift! There is no subject you could not sing. Please tell us in verse of the glory of
the Ganga; hearing of it, anyone may be cleansed of sin.” (CB Adi 13.77–78) <!-- F17 F18 Q1 -->

Keshava at once began to compose verses and recited them without pause, swift as the wind; his voice thundered like
thunder in the clouds, and all were struck dumb, amazed at his gift. According to the “Charitamrita,” within a single
*ghati* — some twenty-four minutes — he composed a hundred shlokas in praise of the Ganga.[^6] The Lord praised him:
in all the world there is no poet like him — and, to Keshava's astonishment, recited one of the hundred shlokas by
heart:

“This greatness of the Ganga ever shines beyond compare, for she is blessed by her birth from the lotus feet of Sri
Vishnu. Like a second Sri Lakshmi, she receives the worship of gods and men; and upon the head of Bhavani's husband
she reigns, endowed with wondrous qualities.” (CC Adi 16.41)

He asked him to explain the verse and then asked about its merits and faults. Vrindavana Dasa writes that no sooner
had the digvijayi begun to explain than the Lord pointed out flaws at the beginning, in the middle and at the end:

“The Lord said, ‘These ornaments of sound (*shabda-alankaras*) are contrived and strange; their use departs from what
the shastras accept. Tell us, what meaning did you put into these ornaments?’” (CB Adi 13.94–95)

The detailed analysis that Vrindavana Dasa does not give is supplied by Krishnadasa Kaviraja: in this one shloka the
Lord found five faults and five poetic ornaments (CC Adi 16.45–86).[^7] <!-- F19 F20 Q2 -->

The son of Sarasvati was bewildered. He answered at random, and Gaurasundara found a flaw in every argument he made:

“It seemed that his reason had left the digvijayi, for he himself did not understand what he was saying.”
(CB Adi 13.98) <!-- F21 Q3 -->

Keshava thought that Sarasvati herself was speaking through the youth's mouth, and asked how He, never having studied
poetics, could give such an explanation. The “Charitamrita” continues:

“Hearing this, Mahaprabhu, who dearly loved a jest, read his heart and answered playfully: ‘Whether My analysis of the
shastras is good or bad, I Myself do not know. I only speak the words that Sarasvati makes Me speak.’ Hearing this, the
*digvijayi* concluded, ‘The goddess has defeated me through a child.’” (CC Adi 16.93–95)

The Lord's words are a jest: He does not depend on Sarasvati — He is her Lord and Husband, as she herself would tell
Keshava that same night. Keshava, for his part, resolved to ask the goddess that evening why she had humbled him
through a child. And Krishnadasa Kaviraja reveals what had really happened:

“In truth, it was Sarasvati who had him compose a faulty verse, and when it was examined she clouded his
intelligence.” (CC Adi 16.97) <!-- F22 -->

The students could barely hold back their laughter, but the Lord did not let them laugh. He encouraged the guest in
every way: He called him the crest-jewel of poets, said that faults can be found even in Bhavabhuti, Jayadeva and
Kalidasa, asked him not to take His boyish impudence to heart, and told him to rest, look into his books during the
night and come again the next day (CC Adi 16.98–104; CB Adi 13.108–117). So the Lord dealt with everyone He defeated:
He offended no one, and the scholars of Navadvipa loved Him. <!-- F23 -->

This was no worldly contest of learning. Krishnadasa Kaviraja opens the chapter by saying that Sarasvati honoured the
youthful Chaitanya, taking His victory over the digvijayi as her occasion (CC Adi 16.3). Sarasvati herself made the
shloka imperfect, and the Lord, having prevailed, did not humiliate the defeated man. His victory was mercy.

But Keshava did not yet know this. Deeply ashamed and in great sorrow he thought: “I have defeated matchless masters of
the six darshanas — Nyaya, Sankhya, Yoga, Mimamsa, Vaisheshika and Vedanta — and today by ill fate I am beaten by a
young teacher of mere grammar, a science for children! Perhaps I have lost Sarasvati's blessing, or offended her
feet.” <!-- F24 -->

## Sarasvati's Revelation
Chanting Sarasvati's mantra, the sorrowful Keshava fell asleep, and that very night she appeared to him in a
dream.[^8] She revealed to him a great secret: Nimai Pandita is no ordinary mortal scholar but the all-powerful
Bhagavan Himself. And she who had given Keshava his victories is only a shadow (*chhaya-shakti*) of the highest
knowledge, the Lord's own internal energy (*svarupa-shakti*); this shadow Sarasvati is ashamed to appear before
Narayana and exists only in dependence on Him. Vrindavana Dasa gives her words:

“Know that the youth who defeated you is none other than the Lord of countless universes Himself. I am the eternal
maidservant of His lotus feet, and I am ashamed to appear before Him.” (CB Adi 13.129–130) <!-- F25 -->

She called this a secret of the Vedas and warned him that to divulge it would shorten his life. The youth, she said,
is the Lord of countless universes; He was Matsya and Kurma, Varaha, Nrisimha and Vamana, Rama; He is known as the son
of Vasudeva and of Nanda, and now He has come as the son of a brahmana (CB Adi 13.127–143). <!-- F26 -->

And she said further that now he had truly obtained the fruit of his mantra:

“The title of digvijayi is not the true fruit of your chanting and of your worship of me. The true fruit of your
chanting and worship is that you have been able to see the Lord, the Master of countless universes. Therefore, O
brahmana, go at once and take shelter at His lotus feet.” (CB Adi 13.145–147)

With these words she vanished. So Sarasvati herself turned her worshipper away from herself to the Lord: the fruit of
worshipping her is not the gifts of this world but shelter at the feet of the One whom she serves. <!-- F27 -->

## At the Lord's Feet
On waking, Keshava went straight to the Lord. He bowed down, and the Lord raised him and embraced him. With many humble
entreaties Keshava told Him his dream and Sarasvati's instruction, and said:

“You are the Supreme Person, Lord Narayana; yet You have appeared in the age of Kali as a brahmana. Who can know You?
… You defeated me three times, and yet You did not humiliate me. Is this possible for anyone but the Supreme Lord?
You are surely Lord Narayana Himself.” (CB Adi 13.155, 158–159)

You are the Husband and Lord of Sarasvati, he said, she herself told me so; and he prayed: “Free me by Your merciful
glance … instruct me, so that no material desire remains in my heart” (CB Adi 13.164–169). <!-- F28 F29 -->

The Lord, the Master of Sarasvati, instructed him:

“Knowledge should not be used to conquer the world; knowledge has value only in the service of the Supreme Lord. …
Therefore, O brahmana, give up your worldly ambitions and devote yourself wholly to the service of Sri Krishna's lotus
feet. … Know, then, that the fruit of true knowledge is unshakable faith that the real wealth of the heart lies hidden
at Krishna's lotus feet.” (CB Adi 13.173, 176, 178)

The higher knowledge, which leads to the Lord's service, is precious; the lower knowledge, pursued for victories and
worldly fame, is worthless. <!-- F30 Q4 I3 -->

With these words the Lord embraced the brahmana warmly, and he was freed from all bonds. “Give up your pride, worship
Krishna, be merciful to all living beings,” the Lord told him (CB Adi 13.180–182). The “Charitamrita” says the same
briefly:

“In the morning he came and took shelter at the Lord's feet, and the Lord showed him mercy and cut his bonds.”
(CC Adi 16.107)

And Vrindavana Dasa himself, listing the pastimes of the Adi-khanda, says that Gauranga defeated the digvijayi and then
freed him from all bonds (CB Adi 1.114). The Lord strictly forbade him to reveal to anyone what Sarasvati had told
him: secrets more hidden than the Vedas are not to be divulged (CB Adi 13.183–184). <!-- F31 F32 -->

By the Lord's mercy devotion, detachment and knowledge awoke in Keshava's heart all at once. Having attained the highest
devotion, he felt that his life had reached its goal, and not a trace was left of the digvijayi's pride: he became
“humbler than a blade of grass” (CB Adi 13.187–188). This humility is not worldly modesty but the fruit of mercy, as is
his detachment: his elephants, horses, palanquins and riches he gave away to worthy people and, wholly detached,
continued his wanderings (CB Adi 13.189–190). The “Bhakti-ratnakara” says that, leaving everything, he went away at the
Lord's command. The one the Lord defeated was not humiliated — he was set free. <!-- F33 F34 I4 -->

Krishnadasa Kaviraja sums it up:

“The *digvijayi* was fortunate, and his life bore fruit: through his learning he attained the feet of Mahaprabhu.”
(CC Adi 16.108) <!-- F35 -->

And the pandits of Navadvipa, seeing Nimai's extraordinary power, wished together to honour Him with the title
“Vadi-simha,” “lion among debaters,” and His fame spread everywhere (CB Adi 13.198–205; “Bhakti-ratnakara”
2.250–252). <!-- F36 -->

## How Others Tell It
Krishnadasa Kaviraja acknowledges that Vrindavana Dasa Thakura was the first to describe this story at length, but did
not set out clearly the merits and faults of the digvijayi's verse; therefore, bowing to him, the author of the
“Charitamrita” gives that analysis (CC Adi 16.26–27). <!-- F45 -->

In Narahari Chakravarti's “Bhakti-ratnakara” the victory over the digvijayi is recalled by Chaitanya Dasa, telling his
son Shrinivasa of the Lord's pastimes he had witnessed in his youth. The proud logicians of Navadvipa laughed at the
Lord, and Chaitanya Dasa prayed to the Creator to crush their pride; and then:

“Suddenly a digvijayi came to Navadvipa; before his might everyone trembled. The goddess Sarasvati is subject to his
devotion; therefore he is a great poet and expert in the shastras.” (“Bhakti-ratnakara” 2.237–238)

“At that time Narayana, the Husband of Sarasvati, named Nimai Pandita, was teaching grammar.” (2.241)

The digvijayi approached Him, laughing in the pride of his learning, and in a moment composed many shlokas; the Lord
pointed out errors in one of them,[^9] honoured the defeated man, Sarasvati revealed to him who the Lord was, and he
surrendered at His feet. After that, says Chaitanya Dasa, his teacher and all the other scholars ceased to regard Nimai
as a mere man (“Bhakti-ratnakara” 2.243–252). <!-- F46 -->

Govinda Deva in the “Gaura-krishnodaya” tells how on a moonlit night a learned digvijayi, a thunder-voiced poet full of
envy, came to the Lord; he praised the Ganga in a hundred verses, and the Lord, retaining one at a single hearing,
found in it many hidden faults and five evident ones. Returning to his lodging, the digvijayi fasted and at midnight
propitiated the goddess, and she, laughing, said:

“Why do you grieve at being humbled by the Lord of all? Hundreds of learned Sarasvatis serve Him; I am His maidservant;
what then of you!” (“Gaura-krishnodaya” 5.25)

At the end of the night the aged brahmana came to the Lord and took shelter of His feet, and the Lord, instructing him
in the truth about Himself, saved him (“Gaura-krishnodaya” 5.11–27). This is the only source that speaks of Keshava's
age: by then he was already old. <!-- F47 F41 -->

Other poets, too, mention this victory briefly. Jayananda writes in his “Chaitanya-mangala”: “Then the Lord defeated
the conqueror of the quarters and at last destroyed all his bonds.” In a pada of Narahari, Gaura is glorified as the
One who “conquered the digvijayi,” to whom the pandits of Nadia submit (“Gaura-charita-chintamani” 10.17). The
biographies recall Keshava in the stories of other companions as well: before His initiation at Gaya, Mahaprabhu was a
pandit of grammar, prosody and logic and defeated the invincible Keshava Kashmiri; He could hold of no account the
learning of all the famous pandits — not only His neighbours, but the renowned digvijayi Keshava Kashmiri himself.
<!-- F48 F49 F42 F43 -->

Keshava is remembered beyond Bengal too. Nabhadasa in the “Bhaktamal” (c. 1600) praises Keshava Bhatta with the byname
“Kashmiri”: with the firm axe of devotion to Hari he felled the trees of other dharmas, and in Mathura he overcame the
mlecchas in debate, so that the qazis, never before defeated, were filled with fear (chhappay 75). Of the meeting with
Mahaprabhu Nabhadasa is silent. Priyadasa, however, tells it in his kavittas on the “Bhaktamal”: Keshava comes to
Nadia with palanquin, elephants and horses; Mahaprabhu Himself approaches him on the bank of the Ganga; Keshava
composes a hundred shlokas; the Lord repeats one and analyses its faults and merits; Sarasvati appears to Keshava and
says, “He is my Lord, Bhagavan”; the Lord tells him, “Accept the fruit — bhakti — and defeat no one any more,” and
Keshava leaves his retinue and pomp behind. The next kavitta tells of his victory over the Muslims at Vishram Ghat in
Mathura.[^10] <!-- F50 F51 -->

The tradition of Vallabha's followers mentions Keshava as well.[^11] <!-- F52 -->

## In the Line of Nimbarka
Having been defeated by Sri Gaurasundara, Keshava understood who Sriman Mahaprabhu is and, in order to serve the Lord,
entered the Nimbarka sampradaya — so writes Srila Bhaktisiddhanta in the “Anubhashya.” Srila Bhaktivinoda Thakura in
the “Amrita-pravaha-bhashya” says that, having received instruction from Mahaprabhu, he became an acharya in the
sampradaya of Nimbaditya. <!-- F38 -->

In the “Gaudiya-bhashya” Srila Bhaktisiddhanta explains this further. Accepting Gaurasundara's instruction, Keshava
gave up his former conclusions drawn from the six darshanas and by the Lord's mercy remembered Nimbarka's ten verses,
the “Dasha-shloki.” The instruction to serve Radha-Govinda awakened in him the sentiments of his predecessors in that
line, to whom he had earlier, seeking the fame of a digvijayi, been indifferent (on CB Adi 13.175–177). The Lord,
Bhaktisiddhanta writes, crushed the pride of the Kashmiri digvijayi Keshavacharya, cleansed his heart of the worship of
worldly learning and instructed him in the highest truth — inconceivable oneness and difference (*achintya-bheda-abheda*),
whose essence is the service of Radha-Govinda; Gaurasundara honoured him with the rank of a Vaishnava acharya (on CB Adi
1.114). Keshava himself could compose verses instantly in any metre. <!-- F39 F40 -->

To him belongs the “Kaustubha-prabha,” a commentary on Shrinivasacharya's “Vedanta-kaustubha,” the exposition of
Nimbarka's bhashya on the “Vedanta-sutra,” the “Vedanta-parijata-saurabha.” According to Srila Bhaktivinoda, he wrote
commentaries on the “Vedanta-parijata” and other bhashyas. <!-- F7 -->

The “Krama-dipika” is a harder question. Srila Gopala Bhatta Gosvami in the “Hari-bhakti-vilasa” and the commentary
“Dig-darshini” cite many proofs from the works of Keshava Bhatta, the author of the “Krama-dipika.” In the
“Gaudiya-bhashya” on CB Adi 13.19 Srila Bhaktisiddhanta remarks that this Keshava Bhatta was later entered as an acharya
in the guru-parampara of the Nimbarka sampradaya; but had the author of the “Krama-dipika” belonged to Nimbarka's line,
the author of the “Hari-bhakti-vilasa” might well have said so. In other places of the “Gaudiya-bhashya” he names
Keshava outright as the compiler of the “Krama-dipika”: Sanatana and Gopala Bhatta saw in him a recipient of
Mahaprabhu's mercy and therefore took material from the “Krama-dipika” for the smriti of the Gaudiya
Vaishnavas.[^12] <!-- F8 -->

The “Bhakti-ratnakara,” in its twelfth wave, listing the line of Nimbaditya, says that the digvijayi Keshava Kashmir,
ashamed, … [was known] by the name “Laghu-Keshava,” and that the fortunate Keshava Kashmir was immersed in
inexpressible joy.[^13] His name also appears in the list in the “Vaishnava-manjusha” (issue 1). <!-- F37 -->

Of what came after, Srila Bhaktisiddhanta writes in the “Gaudiya-bhashya” as follows. Later Keshava and other scholars
from Kashmir, his followers, left the shelter of Mahaprabhu and went their own way, founding an independent
sampradaya (on CB Adi 13.175–177). And Keshava's present-day so-called followers claim that he was Mahaprabhu's
*vartma-pradarshaka-guru* — the one who first showed Him the path. That, says Bhaktisiddhanta, is why Vrindavana Dasa
writes that the Lord “destroyed all his bonds” (on CB Adi 1.114): it was not Keshava who gave knowledge to the Lord,
but the Lord — the Teacher of all the worlds — who set Keshava free. <!-- F40 -->

## His Departure
Of Keshava's later life the primary sources are silent. The time and place of his departure from this world are
unknown: no source names them, and his days are not found in the Vaishnava calendar of the Sri Chaitanya Saraswat
Math. <!-- F41 -->

## Significance
The story of Keshava Kashmiri is a lesson about knowledge. The Lord always takes away pride, while true worth is
humble, like a tree bowed down by the weight of its fruit. Knowledge that serves victories and worldly fame is
worthless; the fruit of learning is a heart and wealth offered at Krishna's feet. Keshava sought the fame of a
world-conqueror and found what is higher than all victories: “through his learning he attained the feet of
Mahaprabhu.” <!-- I2 I3 F35 -->

And it is a lesson about mercy. Sarasvati, whom he worshipped, herself sent him on to her Lord; the Lord defeated him
so as not to humiliate him, and with a single embrace gave him devotion, detachment and knowledge all at once. The one
the Lord defeats is not humiliated but set free. <!-- I1 I4 -->
