# Kurma Vipra
*Курма Випра · @kurma-vipra*

## A Brahmana of Kurma-kshetra
At the start of His journey through the South, Sri Chaitanya spent one night in the house of a brahmana of whom we know
little more than his name. Krishnadasa Kaviraja calls him “a Vedic brahmana named Kurma” (*kurma-name… vaidika
brahmana*); the biographies call him Kurma Vipra, that is, the brahmana Kurma. He bore the same name as the village where
he lived and as the Deity worshipped there.[^1] Other primary sources add words of praise to his name: Kavi Karnapura calls
him “most wise, a great soul” and “the first of excellent brahmanas,” Govinda-deva “a learned brahmana,” and Narahari
Chakravarti addresses him as “Sri Kurma Thakura, the dweller in Kurma.” <!-- F1 -->

He lived at Kurma-kshetra (Kurmasthana), in the very village where the Lord bowed down to the Deity of Kurma. Srila
Bhaktisiddhanta Sarasvati explains in his “Anubhashya” that this is Kurmachalam, or Srikurmam, in Ganjam district, eight
miles east of Chicacole Road station — the foremost tirtha of the Telugu-speaking people, where the Deity of Lord Kurma,
the Tortoise avatara, resides; his information about the place comes from a manual of Ganjam district.[^2] <!-- F2 F3 -->

In the same place Srila Bhaktisiddhanta relates a tradition of the “Prapannamrita”: in the eleventh century of the Shaka
era Sri Jagannathadeva carried Sri Ramanuja there, and Ramanuja, at first taking the Deity of Kurma for a form of Shiva,
fasted until he recognised Him as Vishnu; then he opened the worship of Kurmadeva.[^3] The Deity of Kurma is not an image
made by hands but Vishnu Himself in the form of the Tortoise; and the tradition does not tell how a Shaiva image became a
Vaishnava one, but how the Lord revealed Himself to His devotee. <!-- F4 -->

Nothing more is known of his worldly life. Apart from his name, Krishnadasa Kaviraja tells us only that he lived with his
family (*vamsha*): all of them served the Lord together with him. The worldly origins of many associates who descended to
take part in the Lord's pastimes are unknown, and historians are unlikely ever to establish them; nor is that what
matters — what matters are the lessons of their holy lives. The sources name neither Kurma's parents nor the days of his
appearance and departure, and they are absent from the Vaishnava calendar of the Sri Chaitanya Saraswat Math as well. Nor
is it known when he left this world. <!-- F5 I1 -->

## The Host
In the spring of 1510, at the beginning of the month of Vaishakha, Mahaprabhu resolved to go to South India to deliver the
people living there. At Nityananda's request He took as His servant the brahmana Krishnadasa and, distributing prema along
the way, came to Kurmasthana. The Lord was then twenty-four.[^4] There He bowed down to the Deity of Kurma, and there too
He blessed the brahmana who bore the name Kurma. The “Charitamrita” relates:

“In Kurma-sthana there lived a Vedic brahmana named Kurma, who invited the Lord with great faith and devotion. He brought
the Lord home and washed His feet, and he and his whole family drank that water. With great affection he fed the Lord
many kinds of food, and he and his family ate the Gosani's remnants.” (CC, Madhya 7.121–123) <!-- F6 F7 F8 F9 F10 Q1 -->

All this is not a custom of hospitality but service to the Lord. The water that washed His feet is *charanamrita*, the
remnants of His meal are prasada, and Kurma and his family receive them as sacred. The Lord Himself, won over by the
brahmana's exalted devotion, accepted every service from him: it was the host's ardent love that inclined Him to do so.
In Srila Bhaktisiddhanta's edition the verse is headed accordingly: “A brahmana named Kurma worships the Lord.” <!-- F8 F29 -->

And Kurma offered the Lord praise:

“Your lotus feet, on which Brahma meditates — those same lotus feet have come in person to my house! There is no telling
the limit of my good fortune. Today my birth, my family and my wealth have become glorious.” (CC, Madhya 7.124–125)

The Lord was pleased with the brahmana's praise. Kurma does not take his Guest for an ordinary sannyasi: he knows Whose
feet he has washed — those on which Brahma meditates — and sees in His coming the highest good fortune of his birth, his
family and his wealth. <!-- F11 -->

## “Take Me with You”
When the Lord was about to leave, Kurma could not bear the thought of separation and begged to be allowed to go with Him:

“Be merciful to me, Prabhu — let me go with You. I shall not be able to bear the waves of separation from You.” (CC,
Madhya 7.126)

In the Bengali he speaks of “the waves of separation from You” (*tomara viraha-taranga*). This is not a worldly longing
for a pleasant guest but the pain of a devotee who cannot bear to be parted from the Lord. So too the verse is headed in
Srila Bhaktisiddhanta's edition: “Kurma asks leave to follow the Lord.” <!-- F12 F29 -->

The Lord did not approve of his wish:

“The Lord said, ‘Never speak like that. Stay at home and chant the name of Krishna constantly. Whomever you see, teach him
about Krishna. By My order become a guru and deliver this land. Then the waves of worldly life will never obstruct you,
and here, in this very place, you will have My company again.’” (CC, Madhya 7.127–129)[^5] <!-- F13 F14 Q2 -->

The Lord does not reject Kurma, nor does He send him back to worldly affairs. He gives him a service: staying at home, to
chant the name of Krishna constantly and to speak of Krishna to everyone he meets. His home becomes the place of that
service, and Kurma himself an acharya — a teacher who chants the name himself and teaches others to do so. He becomes a
guru not by his own claim but *amara ajnaya*, “by My order,” by the Lord's grace; and he is told to teach one thing only —
what has been said about Krishna. Then, the Lord promises, “the waves of worldly life” (*vishaya-taranga*) will not
obstruct him. And to the pain of separation the Lord answers with consolation: “here, in this very place, you will have My
company again.” The heading to these verses in the same edition is “The Lord orders everyone to preach the name of
Krishna and bhakti as acharyas.” <!-- F13 F29 -->

## “Stay at Home”
Kurma was not the only one to make that request. Krishnadasa Kaviraja continues:

“In whatever house the Lord took His meal, the host spoke in the same way, and the Lord gave him this same teaching.”
(CC, Madhya 7.130) <!-- F15 Q3 -->

On this verse Srila Bhaktisiddhanta writes in the “Anubhashya”: to those who have resolved to give up everything and
serve the Lord, Gaurasundara, accepting their bhajana, says “stay at home.” That means: give up the conceit of one “wholly
given to bhajana,” and, living humbly at home, chant the name constantly and preach the pure name. To think, “I am the
best of Vaishnavas; if I accept disciples, pride will ruin my bhajana” is the same inflated conceit. One who humbly does
the work of a guru — chants the pure name himself and preaches it — will not be overwhelmed by the waves of worldly fame.
And one who regards as obstacles to bhakti the books and teachings of Rupa, Sanatana, Jiva and Raghunatha dasa and the
many disciples of Narottama, Madhva and Ramanuja offends the true *akinchanas*, devotees who have nothing but Krishna.
Such persons should ponder this order, cast off their petty, pride-filled show of humility and, without taking revenge on
those who have turned away from Hari, following Gaura, increase their bhajana. It is for this that Sri Gauranga, the guru
of the world and the acharya, gives this instruction.[^6] <!-- I2 -->

“Stay at home,” then, is neither a concession to attachment to home nor a refusal of intimacy. It is an order to serve the
Lord where He has placed one, without the conceit of the renunciant and without fear of a teacher's work. Yet not everyone
may call himself a guru: Kurma becomes one by the Lord's order and on one condition — that he himself chants the name
constantly and speaks of nothing but Krishna. <!-- I2 -->

Krishnadasa Kaviraja made Kurma the example through which he shows the whole of the Lord's journey in the South:

“On the way, whenever He stayed at a village temple, He would take His meal in someone's house; and with each such great
soul, His host, He dealt just as He had with Kurma. So He did everywhere, until the Gosani returned to Nilachala. That is
why I have described it here in detail; know that this is how the Lord acted everywhere.” (CC, Madhya 7.131–133)

In Srila Bhaktisiddhanta's edition these verses are headed: “until He returned to Puri, the Lord ordered everyone to
preach bhakti as acharyas.” “The Harmonist” — Srila Bhaktisiddhanta's journal, in which a biography of the Lord was
published under his editorship in 1928 — puts it the same way: at Kurma-kshetra the Lord accepted the worship of the
brahmana Kurma, showed him mercy and told him to preach the name of Krishna to others; and He told everyone to preach the
message of Krishna, themselves following what they preach — that is, to be acharyas. <!-- F15 F21 F29 -->

## Seeing the Lord Off
“He stayed there that night, and in the morning, after bathing, the Lord set out. Kurma followed the Lord a long way,
until the Lord gently persuaded him to go home.” (CC, Madhya 7.134–135)[^7]

In Srila Bhaktisiddhanta's edition these verses are headed “A night in Kurma's house” and “In the morning, on the road
again.” Kurma went a long way with the Lord, and the Lord had to take pains to send him back. <!-- F16 F29 -->

That same night a brahmana of the same place, the leper Vasudeva, heard of the Lord.[^8] In the morning he came to Kurma's
house to see Him, but learned from Kurma himself that the Lord had already gone (CC, Madhya 7.138–139). Then the Lord
appeared again, embraced Vasudeva and healed him. Vasudeva, fearing that pride would now arise in him, prayed to the Lord
for protection from it (7.144–146), and the Lord gave him the same order He had given Kurma: to chant the names of Krishna
constantly and to teach everyone about Krishna (7.147–148). So in one Kurma-kshetra two brahmanas received one and the
same order — to deliver the jivas by instructing them in the name of Krishna. The Lord is won only by undivided devotion
(*ananya-bhakti*), not by externals: He accepted a householder's service and embraced a leper. <!-- F17 F18 Q4 I4 -->

When the Lord had vanished, the two were left together:

“Having said this, the Lord disappeared, and the two brahmanas wept in each other's arms, remembering the Lord's
qualities.” (CC, Madhya 7.149)

In Srila Bhaktisiddhanta's edition its heading is “Remembering the Lord's mercy, Kurma and Vasudeva weep.” The biographies
add that, in each other's arms, they glorified Mahaprabhu's qualities and were immersed in prema.[^9] Krishnadasa
Kaviraja closes the chapter: so began the Lord's journey — with His visit to Kurma and the deliverance of Vasudeva
(Madhya 7.151). And the brief summary of the journey given in the biographies says: at Kurmasthana the Lord delivered
the brahmana Kurma and gave everyone the order to preach devotion to Krishna, and then He delivered Vasudeva.
<!-- F19 F20 F29 -->

The Lord's words to Kurma and Vasudeva and Srila Bhaktisiddhanta's comment show the mercy of the Lord and of His devotees,
who see the sufferings of conditioned souls: those whom He meets the Lord makes teachers, so that they may deliver others.
More on Vasudeva is told in his own account. <!-- I3 -->

## How Others Tell It
Other primary sources also remember the Lord's meeting with Kurma, and each tells it in its own way.[^10]

Murari Gupta writes that at Kurma-kshetra, where Janardana dwells in the form of the Tortoise, the best of brahmanas,
named Kurma, set about honouring the Lord and with faith fed Him prasada; the sight of the Lord was a great festival for
him, and he counted that day well spent. Then, seeing both His devotees, Kurma and Vasudeva, filled with love, the Lord
said to them, “By My command lead the people to devotion to Krishna — easily and joyfully,” and vanished (“Kadacha”
3.14.13–16, 19–20). <!-- F22 -->

Kavi Karnapura in his mahakavya relates that Kurma, on seeing the Lord, bowed down to Him again and again and, very
timidly, said: “Today my birth will bear fruit, for the dust of His two lotus feet will fall upon this house!” Then he
clasped His feet, led Him into the house and washed His feet; the Lord rejoiced in his house, accepted alms and left.
When Vasudeva came, Kurma told him: “The Lord was right here: He accepted alms and showed mercy to one such as me. Had you
come only a moment earlier, you would have seen the Master right here.” And when Vasudeva fell senseless, and the Lord
returned and healed him, Kurma too fainted and, rising, this “first of Vaishnavas” wove a garland of laments: “Right here
such good fortune dawned: the Master of the whole world came — and not for a moment did I recognise Him as God!” A priceless
jewel, he grieved, came right into one's hand — and was not kept; cattle do not know the taste of nectar — so the deluded,
though touching the philosopher's stone, do not know what stone it is. Unable to bear the separation, he lost his senses
and fainted again and again (“Chaitanya-charita-mahakavya” 12.102–105, 108–109, 113–117). <!-- F23 -->

These laments are the speech of humility and separation, not real blindness. According to the “Charitamrita,” Kurma from
the first glorified the Lord's feet as the feet on which Brahma meditates; in Lochana Dasa both brahmanas recognise Gaura
as Krishna. So a devotee in separation reproaches himself for not having served the Lord as he should. <!-- F23 -->

In Karnapura's drama “Chaitanya-chandrodaya” a brahmana returning from the South tells Sarvabhauma and the king that at
Kurma-kshetra the Lord bowed down to Lord Kurma, offered Him praise and stayed in the house of “the best of the
twice-born, named Kurma”: he at once invited Him, and the Lord took His meal with him (act 7). The drama plays on the
shared name of the Deity and the host. <!-- F24 -->

Govinda-deva in the “Gaura-krishnodaya” calls Kurma a learned brahmana, untiring in dharma, compassionate, a doer of great
deeds. Bowing down with love, he led the Lord to his home, wishing to cross over the hell of rebirth; seeing the Lord's
greatness, beside himself with prema, he gave Him alms and wished to leave with Him, but the Lord, knowing this, showed
him favour and forbade him. In the morning Kurma accompanied the Lord a little way and forced himself to return home,
lamenting: “O Gaurachandra, ocean of nectarean qualities, protect me!” Before Kurma's eyes the Lord embraced Vasudeva,
and afterwards the two, weeping in separation from Gaura, became honoured together with the townspeople (9.4–6, 9, 12).
<!-- F25 -->

Lochana Dasa in the Shesha-khanda writes that the Lord, on His way to Setubandha, saw in the town called Kurma a brahmana
named Kurma; Vasudeva lived in the same village, and the Lord met both in one place. By the sight of the Lord they became
pure, recognised Gaurachandra as Krishna and fell at His feet. The Lord embraced them both, asked them for what purpose
they had come into the world and what they followed, and instructed them: “In the Kali-yuga the dharma is the
sankirtana of Hari's name… Dance, dance — let the people be freed from their bonds!” When the Lord hurriedly left, they
lost all awareness of themselves (46.4–14). Srila Bhaktisiddhanta's summary of the chapter names only Vasudeva.
<!-- F26 -->

## “Here You Will Have My Company Again”
The Lord had promised Kurma that here he would have His company again. On the way back from the South He went by the
same road:

“The Lord went back by the same road by which He had come, seeing all the Vaishnavas along the way.” (CC, Madhya 9.336)

The heading to this verse in Srila Bhaktisiddhanta's edition is “To show mercy to the devotees who had become Vaishnavas,
the Lord goes back by the same road.” Kurma is not named here, but he was among those whom the Lord had made Vaishnavas
on the way south; so, it seems, the promise “here you will have My company again” was fulfilled.[^11] <!-- F28 -->

## In Narahari's Prayers
In the “Namamrita-samudra” Narahari Chakravarti asks each of the Lord's associates to reveal to him one of Gaura's
pastimes. To Kurma, who is bound up with the Lord's southern journey, he says:

“Grant this, Sri Kurma Thakura, dweller in Kurma: let me sing the pastimes of the Lord's journey through the South.”
(“Namamrita-samudra” 166)
<!-- F27 -->

## Significance
Kurma is one of the householders at whose homes the Lord took His meals on the southern journey, and Krishnadasa Kaviraja
chose him to show how the Lord acted everywhere. He received the Lord with faith and love, served Him with his whole
family, could not bear the thought of separation — and received the order the Lord gave to all: staying at home, without
pride, to chant the name of Krishna constantly and, by His order, to teach others to do the same. Kurma's worldly life is
almost unknown, but the lesson of his life is clear: one whom the Lord has blessed serves Him where He has placed him, and
delivers others by His name. <!-- F15 F21 I1 I2 I3 -->
