# Jnanadasa
*Джнянадас · @jnanadasa*

## A Branch of Nityananda
Listing the branches of the tree of Nityananda, Krishnadasa Kaviraja names Jnanadasa too:

“Pitambara, Madhavacharya, Damodara Dasa, Shankara, Mukunda, Jnanadasa and Manohara.” (CC, Adi 11.52)

The “Charitamrita” says nothing more of him, and Srila Bhaktisiddhanta in the “Anubhashya” on this verse simply sets
his name in the general list of branches. Other primary sources, telling of Jahnava-devi and her retinue, call him by
the same name, Jnanadasa; the “Narottama-vilasa” adds the epithet “charming,” and the “Bhakti-ratnakara,” speaking of
his village, calls him “Sri Mangala Jnanadasa.” The biographies spell the name in its Bengali form, Gyanadasa or Sri
Gyana Dasa. Later padas composed in his honour give him further names as well: “Madana-mangala,” “Manohara,” “Mangala
Thakura.” <!-- F1 F2 Q1 -->

Tradition and the biographies take this Jnanadasa to be one and the same person as the poet from the village of Kandra,
Jahnava's disciple, whose songs are still sung in kirtana today. We follow that tradition here, although no primary
source says so in as many words.[^1] He received initiation from Sri Jahnava, the consort and shakti of Nityananda
Prabhu — according to one account, while still a child. <!-- F3 -->

## Kandra
His birthplace is given as the village of Kandra; one of the padas about him names it together with its neighbour, as
Kandra-Mandra. The biographies place Kandra in the district of Birbhum, other sources in Bardhaman, west of
Katwa.[^2] Narahari Chakravarti in the “Bhakti-ratnakara,” turning to the story of Jayagopala, a *kayastha* of that
village whom Virachandra rejected, says:

“In Radha-desha there is a village named Kandra; there is the abode of Sri Mangala Jnanadasa.” (“Bhakti-ratnakara”
14.180)

The story says nothing more about Jnanadasa. Why he is called “Mangala” here, the sources do not explain.[^3]
<!-- F4 F6 Q2 -->

He was born, by scholars' estimates, at the close of the first quarter of the sixteenth century, around 1525; the
primary sources themselves give no year.[^4] The day of his appearance is unknown too, nor is it found in the Vaishnava
calendar of the Sri Chaitanya Saraswat Math. By birth he was a *brahmana* of the Radhi class, and by one account of the
“Mangala” lineage. <!-- F5 F8 -->

He never married: in his youth — by one pada, from his childhood — he took the vow of renunciation. So he left no
descendants of his own; but in the village of Kotulpur, in the district of Bankura, live the descendants of his
kinsmen. Several families in Kotulpur bear the title “Gosvami”: according to the “Vishvakosha,” people used to call
Jnanadasa himself “Gosvami,” and ever since his kin have added the title to their names. *Brahmanas* of the same
lineage live in Hooghly and Bankura as well. <!-- F9 F10 -->

His memory lives on in Kandra. Jnanadasa's *thakura-bari*, the house of his Deities, still stands there; other sources
call the place his *matha*. Every year on Pausha-purnima, the full moon of the month of Pausha, a great festival of
sankirtana is held in Kandra in his honour: a fair goes on for three days, and, as one pada sings, the *mahantas*
gather. <!-- F7 F11 -->

## Jahnava's Disciple and His Friend
Together with Jnanadasa, his friend and fellow disciple Baba Aul Manohara Dasa, the compiler of the collection of padas
called the “Padasamudra,” studied under Jahnava; by another source he also compiled the “Niryasa-tattva.” The two, it is
said, were inseparable: wherever one went, the other went too. The name “aul” means “beside oneself,” “intoxicated” with
love of God.[^5] <!-- F12 -->

One of the biographies holds that Manohara Dasa was with Jnanadasa at Kheturi as well, and finds him in the list of
Jahnava's companions in the “Narottama-vilasa.” But in the “Narottama-vilasa” itself *manohara* is an epithet: “Murari
Chaitanya, the charming Jnanadasa” (6.187).[^6] <!-- F13 -->

## In Jahnava's Retinue
When Narottama Thakura was preparing his great festival at Kheturi, a letter reached Kharadaha, and Nityananda's
companions grew eager to go. Jahnava's messenger, arriving at Kheturi, told Srinivasa Acharya:

“There were the most generous Krishnadasa, whose elder brother is Suryadasa Sarakhela, Shrila Raghupati Upadhyaya,
Mahidhara, Murari Chaitanya, the charming Jnanadasa…” (“Narottama-vilasa” 6.186–187)

The “Bhakti-ratnakara,” telling how Jahnava set out from Kharadaha, names in her retinue “Sri Minaketana Ramadasa, the
charming one, Murari Chaitanya, Jnanadasa, Mahidhara” (10.374).[^7] The “Prema-vilasa” too names him among those who
came to the festival (19.311). According to one of the biographies, his friend Manohara Dasa went with him; so a later
pada sings as well: Baba Aul went with him to the festival at Kheturi. <!-- F14 Q3 -->

On the second day of the festival, when the guests were fed in the courtyard of Jahnava's lodging, the
“Narottama-vilasa” names him among those seated at the meal: “Sri Minaketana Ramadasa, Mahidhara, Murari Chaitanya, the
charming Jnanadasa” (8.50). And when the festival was over and Jahnava left Kheturi at dawn, he went with her:

“Raghupati Vaidya, the charming Upadhyaya, Jnanadasa, Mukunda and others, oceans of virtue. Who could describe their
power? They are supremely skilled in subduing wicked pashandis.” (“Narottama-vilasa” 8.270–271)

The same list is found in the “Bhakti-ratnakara” (10.742–746). <!-- F15 F16 -->

The “Nityananda-vamsha-vistara” says that, intending to go to Vrindavana, Jahnava called four to her side — Ramadasa,
Ramai, Sundara and Jnanadasa — and first went with them to Ekachakra, Nityananda's birthplace (5.5). The same book
names him among Virachandra's companions too: in the procession of Nityananda's son through East Bengal, “Jnanadasa,
Krishnadasa, Ramadasa, Nityananda Dasa and Ramai went surrounding the palanquin” (7.60). <!-- F17 F18 -->

He was also among the *mahantas* who gathered at Kantaka-nagara, that is, Katwa, where Srinivasa Acharya and
Raghunandana Thakura came: the “Bhakti-ratnakara” names there “Ramasena, Damodara, Jnanadasa, Gopala the dancer,
Pitambara” (9.401). Likewise the “Prema-vilasa,” among the branches of Nityananda at the memorial festivals in Katwa and
Shrikhanda, names “Ramasena, Jnanadasa and Damodara” (19.147). The biographies do not mention this. <!-- F19 -->

## A Poet of Kirtana
Above all, Jnanadasa is remembered as a poet. His padas for kirtana are full of rasa and go straight to the heart; that
is why the biographies value him first of all as a singer. He sang of Gaura and Nityananda, of Radha and Krishna; he
composed songs both in Bengali and in Brajabuli. <!-- F20 I1 -->

One of his padas is quoted by Narahari Chakravarti in the “Bhakti-ratnakara,” in the account of Saptagrama and the house
of Uddharana Datta. It opens with the cry: “Ah, my Nityananda Raya!” Nitai Himself dances and Himself sings, making
everyone repeat “Gauranga!”; around Him are Ramai, Sundara, Gauridasa and other companions; and Jnanadasa sings day and
night of Nitai's virtues (12.3745–3749). The same pada is included in the “Gaura-pada-tarangini” as well. <!-- F21 -->

The great collection of padas, the “Padakalpataru,” contains, by its editor's count, one hundred and eighty-six of his
songs, in Bengali and Brajabuli; there is also a separate edition of his *padavali*. The “Gaura-pada-tarangini” has padas
on Gaura bearing his signature. Widely known is his pada on Sri Radha's *purvaraga*, “rupa lagi am̐khi jhure…” — “for
His form my eyes weep.” The editor of the “Padakalpataru” even believed that many of Jnanadasa's finest Bengali padas
later came to circulate under the name of Chandidasa.[^8] <!-- F22 -->

One of the biographies quotes two of his padas on Radha and Krishna. The first is of Holi: Radha and Madhava swing on a
swing, the friends rock Them, the Divine Couple shower each other with coloured powder, the *sakhis* sprinkle sandal
and kunkuma, and at the sight of Them the maidservants swoon; and the poet, beholding it, has his wish fulfilled. The
second is of separation: a friend tells “heartless Kanu” that Radha in her grief has forgotten the honour of her family,
now swoons, now laughs, lies on the ground, her face faded like the moon on the fourteenth day; and Jnanadasa angrily
warns Krishna that the sin of killing a woman will fall on Him. <!-- F20 -->

This is not worldly love poetry. The pastimes of Radha and Krishna take place in the spiritual world and cannot be
measured by the standards of this one. “Forgot the honour of her family” is the gopi's complete self-surrender to
Krishna, when nothing is left to her but His joy; the poet's “anger” — he speaks here as Radha's friend — and his
reproach to Krishna are loving pique (*pranaya*), one of the colours of rasa; and the maidservants' swooning is the
ecstasy of the *manjaris*, whose highest happiness is to serve the Divine Couple. Srila Bhaktisiddhanta Sarasvati
strictly warned against discussing such songs without the proper qualification, as the *prakrita-sahajiyas* do:

“Unqualified people, who take the body for the self, are full of thirst for what is unworthy and burdened with
*anarthas*, may take the supramundane pastimes of Sri Radha and Krishna, worshipped by the supremely liberated, to be
the tasteless, vile amorous sports of a mundane hero and heroine, or something like them — and end up gratifying their
senses. Therefore any kind of discussion of the pastimes of Radha and Krishna is strictly forbidden to them.”
(“Anubhashya” on CC, Adi 13.42)

That is why we only briefly retell these padas and do not give them in full.

Later poets glorified Jnanadasa himself. A pada signed “Narahari Dasa” sings:

“His abode is in Sri Birbhum, the village of Kandra-Mandra: there Jnanadasa was born. Renounced from his youth, devoted
[to God] from childhood, he received initiation from Jahnava.” (“Gaura-pada-tarangini,” sec. 6.3, no. 44)

It goes on to say that in Kandra to this day a three-day festival is held in his honour at the full moon, that he was
called “Madana-mangala” and “Manohara,” and that Baba Aul went with him to Kheturi; the poet calls him the sun in the
race of poets, equal to Chandidasa, and his padas a stream of nectar. The pada is late, and who composed it has not been
established. It was not by chance that he was ranked with Chandidasa: Mahaprabhu Himself loved to hear Chandidasa's
songs:

“With Svarupa and Ramananda, Mahaprabhu night and day sang and heard the songs of Chandidasa and Vidyapati, Raya's
drama, the ‘Karnamrita’ and the ‘Sri Gita-govinda’ in supreme bliss.” (CC, Madhya 2.77) <!-- F23 -->

Another poet, Radhavallabha, exclaims: “Glory to the poet Jnanadasa, whose fame is manifest throughout Gauda-mandala!
His padas are steeped in nectar… he sang of the hidden rasa of Vraja; glory to Mangala Thakura, who obtained the
peerless gift of poetry.” So this pada too calls the poet Jnanadasa Mangala Thakura. <!-- F24 -->

Where these names came from has been judged in different ways. The compiler of the “Gaura-pada-tarangini” (1903) admits
that it cannot be decided whether the “Mangala” of the “Bhakti-ratnakara” and the “Manohara” of the “Charitamrita” are
his own names or other persons. By one reference work, his other name was Madana-mangala, and Manohara was his friend;
according to the “Vishvakosha,” he was called Mangala Thakura because in his country he preached the holy Name, which
is auspicious for the whole world (*bhuvana-mangala*), and Madana-mangala for his beauty. <!-- F25 -->

Narahari Chakravarti himself turns to him as well. The “Namamrita-samudra” has a series of verses in which he begs the
Vaishnavas for the gift of singing Krishna's childhood pastimes; among them is this:

“O Jnanadasa! [Grant that] I may ever sing of Krishna's endless, enchanting pranks.” (“Namamrita-samudra” 181)

The author does not say outright that this is the same Jnanadasa; but next to him he names Ramasena, Hariharananda and
“Gopala the dancer” — the very names that surround Jnanadasa in the lists of the “Bhakti-ratnakara” and the
“Prema-vilasa”; the “Charitamrita” knows only one Jnanadasa; and a plea for the gift of song is most natural when
addressed to a poet. <!-- F26 -->

## His Departure
When, where and how Jnanadasa left this world, no source says; the place of his samadhi is not named either, and his
day is not found in the calendar of the Sri Chaitanya Saraswat Math. The festival at Kandra on Pausha-purnima is a day
in the poet's memory, not the day of his departure. <!-- F27 -->

## Significance
Jnanadasa is a branch of the tree of Nityananda and a disciple of Jahnava. He went in her retinue to Kheturi and left it
together with her, accompanied her to Ekachakra, walked in Virachandra's procession and was among the *mahantas* who
gathered at Katwa. But his chief service was song. Renouncing the world from his youth, he gave his gift to glorifying
Gaura, Nitai and the pastimes of Radha and Krishna, and his padas, which were ranked with the songs of Chandidasa, are
heard in kirtana to this day. <!-- F2 F20 I1 -->
