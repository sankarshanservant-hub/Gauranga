# Jayadeva Gosvami
*Джаядева Госвами · @jayadeva*

## The Poet Whose Song the Lord Relished
Sri Jayadeva lived three centuries before Mahaprabhu appeared and was not His companion in Navadvipa or Puri. Yet in
the last twelve years of His pastimes the Lord relished his “Sri Gita-govinda” day and night, and Srila Bhaktivinoda
Thakura preserved a tradition according to which Mahaprabhu, long before His own appearance, revealed His golden form
to the poet. For this reason the biographies of the Lord's associates begin with him. He is called Sri Jayadeva and
Jayadeva Gosvami, and under this name the Vaishnava calendar of Sri Chaitanya Saraswat Math observes the day of his
departure. At the close of the poem he is also called “Jayadeva Sarasvati.”[^1] <!-- F1 -->

One of the biographies opens its account with an interpretation of the name itself. *Jaya* here is He who surpasses
all, Sri Krishna, and *deva* comes from the verb “to reveal, to illumine”: Jayadeva is the one who by the power of his
bhakti reveals Krishna to the world.[^2] So a poet-devotee discloses the Lord to others: his song leads to Krishna.
<!-- F2 I1 -->

## Time and Homeland
Opinions differ as to when he lived. According to Srila Bhaktisiddhanta Sarasvati in the “Anubhashya,” some place him in
the eleventh or twelfth century of the Shaka era, and the biographies repeat this. One thing is certain: he lived under
Lakshmana Sena, king of Bengal, that is, in the twelfth century, some three hundred years before Mahaprabhu
appeared.[^3] The day of his appearance is not in our calendar, nor do the sources give it. <!-- F3 -->

Nor is there agreement about his birthplace. Most hold that he was born in the village of Kendubilva (Kenduli) in the
district of Birbhum; others say Utkala (Orissa), still others the south of India. The “Anubhashya” lists the same three
opinions. Kendubilva stands on the bank of the river Ajay, twenty miles south of Suri. Here, it is said, his parents
lived: his father Sri Bhojadeva and his mother Vamadevi. <!-- F4 F5 F8 -->

With this river is linked the tradition of his Deities. Sri Radha-Madhava, whom he served all his life, Jayadeva, as
the tradition tells, found in the waters of the Ajay. And on the bank of the same river, at the shrine of Shiva
Kuleshvara, he is said to have rested and performed his bhajana.[^4] <!-- F6 F7 -->

## At the Court of Lakshmana Sena
Later Jayadeva lived for a long time in Navadvipa, then the capital of Bengal, on the bank of the Ganga, not far from
the royal palace. He was a court pandita, a poet, of Lakshmana Sena. The foremost scholar of the court was Govardhana
Acharya; the dictionary of Ashutosh Deb also calls Jayadeva a court poet of Lakshmana Sena. In the “Gita-govinda”
Jayadeva himself names other poets of that court — Sri Umapatidhara, Acharya Sri Govardhana and the one he honours as
“king of poets” (*kavi-kshmapati*); all were great poets and his friends.[^5] <!-- F9 F10 -->

Srila Bhaktivinoda Thakura in the “Navadvipa-dhama-mahatmya” tells how Lakshmana Sena came to know of him. While
Jayadeva was living near the palace, the king heard his “Dashavatara-stotra,” the hymn to the Lord's ten avataras, and
was amazed. Govardhana Acharya revealed to him who the author was. Then the king, laying aside his royal robes, came to
the poet unrecognized; seeing in him every sign of a great soul, he grew still more attached to him, revealed who he
was, and begged him to live in the palace. <!-- F11 -->

Jayadeva had no interest in worldly grandeur. He refused, saying he would go to Purushottama, to Puri. The king
entreated him not to leave Navadvipa and offered him for his bhajana the village of Champahati in Navadvipa-mandala;
formerly it was called Champaka-hatta, after a grove of champaka trees and a market where their flowers were sold.
There the king built him a hut and humbly promised never to come to him without his leave. <!-- F12 -->

## The Golden Form at Champahati
At Champahati, according to the testimony of the same Srila Bhaktivinoda Thakura, Jayadeva received what makes him
honoured among the associates of Gaura. Once, in this very place, Dvija Vaninatha had seen the Lord in a form the
colour of champaka.[^6] So too Jayadeva first beheld Sri Radha and Govinda, and then Mahaprabhu, Their united form,
golden like a champaka flower.[^7] This is theologically exact: Sri Chaitanya is Krishna Himself, who has taken the
feelings and lustre of Radha — Radha and Krishna become One:

“Sri Radha is the transformation of Krishna's love, His pleasure potency, *hladini-shakti*. Therefore, though Radha
and Krishna are one in Self, from time without beginning They have appeared in the world in two different forms. Now
these Two have become one and are manifest as Sri Chaitanya. I bow to Him, Krishna Himself, adorned with the sentiment
and the lustre of Sri Radha.” (CC, Adi 1.5) <!-- F13 -->

Having revealed Himself, Mahaprabhu told him to go to Purushottama. Showing His champaka-coloured form, the Lord
disclosed the future: He would appear in Navadvipa, accept sannyasa, come to Puri and there relish his “Gita-govinda.”
Jayadeva obeyed, though separation from Navadvipa-dhama, the future abode of his Lord, tormented him. <!-- F14 -->

Srila Bhaktivinoda Thakura also writes that the mood of Sri Gaurangadeva awakened in the hearts of pure devotees even
before He openly appeared in the world: in the hearts of Jayadeva, Bilvamangala, Chandidasa and Vidyapati. Their
writings are full of that very understanding of bhakti which Mahaprabhu later brought. That is why the Lord relished
their songs: in them sounded His own bhava. <!-- F15 I5 -->

## The “Gita-govinda”
The “Sri Gita-govinda,” also called the “Ashtapadi,” Jayadeva composed, according to these traditions, already in Sri
Jagannatha-kshetra, in Puri.[^8] It is a transcendental poem of separation, vipralambha-rasa. Srila Bhaktisiddhanta
writes of it in the “Anubhashya”:

“His book of poetry is called the ‘Gita-govinda,’ or ‘Ashtapadi.’ It too abounds in transcendental
*vipralambha-rasa*. It describes the *vipralambha-rasa* that nourishes the rasa of *sambhoga* — the separation that
arose when the prince of Vraja left the place of the rasa dance, as the ‘Srimad-Bhagavatam’ tells.” (“Anubhashya” on
CC, Adi 13.42) <!-- F16 F71 Q6 -->

The hymn to the ten avataras that so amazed Lakshmana Sena is held by one of the biographies to be part of the
“Gita-govinda” itself (*dashavatara-gita*); others name it separately, without saying whether it belongs to the poem.
<!-- F17 -->

Old books, too, bear witness to the poet's time. One of the biographies cites them from the preface to a Calcutta
edition of the “Gita-govinda”: in the “Alankara-shekhara,” written by order of King Manikya Chandra before the Muslim
conquest of Delhi, Jayadeva is called a court poet of the king of Orissa; Shridhara Dasa, son of one of Lakshmana Sena's
chief ministers, included many of his verses in his anthology “Sad-ukti-karnamrita,” referring to a certain work (in the
text, “Amiyabha-kavya”); and the colophon of one ancient manuscript says that Jayadeva was famed as a poet in the time of
Lakshmana Sena. <!-- F18 -->

For whom this song is meant Jayadeva said himself, at its very beginning:

“If your mind longs to remember Hari, if it is curious about the art of His pastimes, then listen to the sweet, tender
and lovely words of Jayadeva.” (“Gita-govinda” 1.3)[^9]

And again, in another song: “This most charming song Jayadeva utters in the service of Hari… Bow down to the merciful
Hari.” The poem discloses the intimate pastimes of Sri Radha and Govinda, full of shringara-rasa — madhurya, the
sweetness of conjugal love — and only the fortunate, who have sukriti, the piety of past lives, and a longing to
remember Hari, are fit to serve it. This is not mundane love poetry: shringara is the highest rasa of pure bhakti, and
to measure it by a worldly standard is an offence. <!-- F20 F21 I4 Q8 -->

Srila Bhaktisiddhanta says this plainly. The songs of Chandidasa, Vidyapati and Jayadeva were Mahaprabhu's most beloved
delicacy; but only the most fortunate person can follow them, one indifferent both to enjoyment and to renunciation who
eternally longs to serve Sri Radha. Lovers of mundane poetry and the sect of the prakrita-sahajiyas, fancying
themselves “raganuga,” impudently take up these songs, and as a result licentiousness and atheism grow in the world,
and they themselves are led to hell:

“Unqualified people, who take the body for the self, are full of thirst for what is unworthy and burdened with
*anarthas*, may take the supramundane pastimes of Sri Radha and Krishna, worshipped by the supremely liberated, to be
the tasteless, vile amorous sports of a mundane hero and heroine, or something like them — and end up gratifying their
senses. Therefore any kind of discussion of the pastimes of Radha and Krishna is strictly forbidden to them.”
(“Anubhashya” on CC, Adi 13.42) <!-- F71 -->

The secret of the poem is revealed in its very first lines. One of the biographies gives an interpretation of the word
“by night” (*naktam*) in the first verse (*meghair meduram ambaram…*): by it Jayadeva pointed to the hour at the end of
the night when Radha and Krishna are joined in an inseparable embrace and assume one form — the form of Sri
Gaurasundara; and at this very hour, the same biography says, Srila Bhaktisiddhanta Sarasvati left the world.[^10]
<!-- F22 -->

The “Gita-govinda” was known and honoured everywhere. When Srila Rupa Gosvami gathered the scattered verses of
Vaishnava poets in the “Padyavali,” he noted specially in its conclusion that he had not included verses from the works
of Jayadeva, Vishvamangala and others: the “Gita-govinda” already circulated as a whole book. And the twenty-one songs of
Ramananda Raya's “Jagannatha-vallabha-nataka” closely resemble the songs of the “Gita-govinda.” <!-- F23 F24 -->

## Padmavati
From his youth Jayadeva was detached from the world and had no thought of marriage; and yet, by the Lord's will, he had
to accept the virtuous Padmavati as his wife. The tradition tells it thus. Her father, a brahmana, at Jagannatha's
command brought his daughter to Jayadeva and asked him to take her as his wife. Jayadeva refused; the brahmana then left
his daughter and went away, and Jayadeva told her she was free to go wherever she wished. By another account, a
childless brahmana had prayed to Jagannatha for many years for a son, but a daughter was born — Padmavati; when she
grew up, her father brought her to give her to Jagannatha Himself, and the Lord told him: “I have a servant, Jayadeva,
who has left his home and sings My names. Give her to him.” Jayadeva for his part offered to take the girl wherever she
might say.[^11] <!-- F25 F26 -->

Padmavati humbly replied: “By Jagannatha's will my father has given me to you. You alone will I choose as my husband; I
will accept no other and will never break faith. Even if you abandon me, I will not abandon you and will always serve
your feet.”[^12] Jayadeva had no other way, and he accepted her. So the detached devotee became a householder — not by
yielding to the world, but by fulfilling the will of the Lord, who Himself gave him a devotee-wife so that together
they might serve Him. <!-- F27 -->

## The Line Written by Krishna
Jayadeva installed a Deity of Narayana in his home, and in his heart a river of love for Krishna began to flow; in its
waves he was writing the “Gita-govinda.” One day, the tradition tells, he came to the song in which Krishna appeases the
angry Radha. After the words “if You say anything at all…” (*vadasi yadi kinchid api*) there was to follow the line:
“Place on My head Your foot, tender as a shoot — the ornament that removes the poison of Kamadeva” (*smara-garala-
khandanam mama shirasi mandanam… dehi pada-pallavam udaram*, “Gita-govinda” 10.8). But Jayadeva did not dare to write
that Sri Krishna places Radha's foot on His own head. Leaving the song unfinished, he went to bathe.[^13] <!-- F28 F29 -->

Soon Padmavati saw Jayadeva return, write something in the manuscript, eat and go out again. She was eating what he had
left when the real Jayadeva came in and asked in surprise: “Why are you eating today before I have eaten?” Padmavati
answered that he had already eaten and gone, and before that had written something in the book. Jayadeva opened the
manuscript — and saw that the missing words “dehi pada-pallavam udaram” were written in his own hand, and the ink was
not yet dry. He understood: these words had been written, and the song completed, by Sri Krishna Himself, the foremost
relisher of rasa. His hair stood on end, and tears poured from his eyes.[^14] <!-- F30 F31 F19 -->

In a trembling voice he said to Padmavati: “You are most fortunate: you have seen Sri Krishna Himself and are eating His
prasada. I was not so blessed. I too will take your remnants” — and he ate what she had left. One of the biographies
adds that this tradition is generally accepted as authentic. <!-- F32 -->

Jayadeva's hesitation was no weakness of faith: it was reverence (*aishvarya-jnana*), which does not dare to describe
the Lord of all at Radha's feet. But Krishna writes this line Himself, because He is conquered by the love of Radha, the
highest of His devotees; this is no lessening of God, but the sway of pure prema over Him. And that Jayadeva took the
remnants of his wife's meal is no breach of custom, but the humility of a Vaishnava who recognized that she had received
a mercy he himself had not. <!-- I2 -->

## Jagannatha Listens to the “Gita-govinda”
In Puri another tradition is kept. A woman of the gardeners' caste (*malini*) used to sing the “Gita-govinda,” wholly
absorbed in its mood. Drawn by her singing, Jagannatha would come there, listen as long as she sang, and return to the
temple. One day the king of Utkala, coming for darshana, saw dust on Jagannatha's body and thorns in His cloth. The
pujari-pandas could not explain it and were frightened. That night Jagannatha told the king in a dream that no one was
to blame: He had gone to the gardener-woman to hear the “Gita-govinda,” and there, in the garden, the dust and thorns
had clung to Him. <!-- F33 F34 -->

Then the king sent a palanquin for her and had her sing the “Gita-govinda” before Jagannatha. Ever since, the women of
her family sing this poem daily in the temple before the Lord, and the devotees of Puri call them not gardener-women but
*devadasis*, servants of the Deity. So, according to the tradition, began the singing of the “Gita-govinda” for
Jagannatha. Jagannatha is Krishna Himself, not an image: the dust and thorns on His body are the play of the Lord, who is
drawn by the song of His devotee. The singing of a devadasi was later heard by Mahaprabhu as well; this is told below.
<!-- F35 -->

## The Lord Thatches the Roof
Many wonders are told of Jayadeva. With deep love he served Sri Radha-Madhava. Once, in the scorching heat, he was
thatching the roof of his hut, and someone below was handing him ropes and bundles of straw so that the work would go
faster. Jayadeva thought it was Padmavati, but when he came down he learned that she had been busy with something else
all the while. Entering the temple, he saw that Radha-Madhava's hands were covered with soot and dirt, understood who
had helped him, fell at Their feet and wept for a long time.[^15] As the devotee is devoted to the Lord, so the Lord is
devoted to His devotee. <!-- F36 F37 F38 I2 -->

## The Robbers
To gather means for the service and festivals of Radha-Madhava, the treasure of his life, Jayadeva travelled to other
lands, where his poetic gift brought him offerings. On the way back robbers fell upon him: they took his money, cut off
his hands and feet and threw him into a well. Enduring terrible pain, he loudly repeated the name of Hari. <!-- F39 -->

On the third day a king was hunting nearby. Hearing the name of Hari from the well, he was amazed, had the wounded
Jayadeva drawn out, brought him to the palace and surrounded him with care; through the efforts of the king and queen he
gradually recovered. Recognizing in him a great devotee and listening to his sweet singing of the “Gita-govinda,” the
king brought Padmavati to the palace as well. The king and queen received initiation into the Vishnu-mantra[^16] and
lived hearing krishna-katha from Jayadeva and serving the Vaishnavas. <!-- F40 F41 -->

One day those very robbers came to the palace disguised as Vaishnavas. Jayadeva recognized them but had them received
with every honour. Not understanding his noble intent and fearing exposure and punishment, they hastened to leave
without accepting the hospitality. Jayadeva understood their fear and asked the king to give them much money and send
escorts with them. Having gone a little way, the robbers said to the escorts: “No need to go further. But tell your
king in secret: we used to serve another king, and for some reason he ordered us to kill your mahanta-babaji. We cut
off his hands and feet. He is afraid the secret will come out — that is why he had us given money and quickly sent
away.” <!-- F42 F43 -->

Mother Earth could not bear this lie, and the robbers wondrously sank into the ground. For, as the “Bhagavatam” says:

“The Earth spoke thus: ‘There is no unrighteousness greater than falsehood. I can bear everything — only not a man
given to falsehood.’” (“Srimad-Bhagavatam” 8.20.4)

These words are spoken by Bali in answer to Shukracharya, who was dissuading him from his gift to Vamanadeva.
<!-- F44 Q7 -->

The king's servants, who had seen the wondrous punishment that befell the offenders at the feet of a mahabhagavata,
told the king everything in order. At the king's questioning, Jayadeva related what had happened to him on the road and
said: “Sadhus do not take revenge on those who wrong them, but try to satisfy them with courtesy. Yet by the inexorable
law of the Lord they had to taste the fruit of their deeds.” The sadhu forgives, but an offence at the feet of a great
devotee the Lord does not leave without its consequence. <!-- F45 I3 -->

## Padmavati's Test
Padmavati and the queen became close friends. In those days there was the custom of sati, in which a widow ascended
her husband's funeral pyre. Once, when the queen was grieving that her brother's widow would ascend the pyre, Padmavati
said to her: “When the husband leaves the world, the life of a faithful wife does not remain in the body.” <!-- F46 -->

Wishing to test these words, the queen one day told Padmavati that Jayadeva had left his body. The moment she heard it,
the faithful Padmavati herself left her body. The queen, thinking herself guilty, sobbed, and the king in tears
earnestly begged Jayadeva to restore his wife's life. Jayadeva poured the nectar of Krishna's name into Padmavati's ears
— and she came back to herself, as if awakening from sleep. Seeing the wondrous greatness of both, the whole royal
family with the king and queen bowed again and again at the feet of Jayadeva and Padmavati. The power of this miracle
lies in the holy name sounding from the lips of a great devotee; and in Padmavati's departure the tradition sees an image
of the perfect fidelity of a wife wholly given to her Vaishnava husband. <!-- F47 F48 -->

## In Vrindavana
Then Jayadeva was drawn to Vrindavana. Taking leave of the king and queen, he took his beloved Sri Radha-Madhava and set
out. In Vrindavana he served Them at Keshi-tirtha, the Keshi-ghata on the Yamuna. Hearing how sweetly he sang the
“Gita-govinda,” the residents of the dhama lost all outer awareness. A certain mahajana, a wealthy merchant, built a
temple for his Radha-Madhava above Keshi-ghata. <!-- F49 -->

## Last Years and Departure
Where Jayadeva spent his last days the traditions disagree: according to some, in Jagannatha-kshetra; according to
others, in Vrindavana; according to still others, in his native Kendubilva. Srila Bhaktisiddhanta Sarasvati Gosvami
Prabhupada said that he spent his last years in Jagannatha-kshetra; so he writes in the “Anubhashya” as well: “He spent
the last part of his life at Sri Jagannatha-kshetra.” This is the view we follow.[^17] <!-- F50 F51 -->

Another tradition says that after living long in Vrindavana, Jayadeva returned to Kendubilva and performed his bhajana
there. To bathe in the Ganga he had to walk a long way every day. Once he could not go and grew sad — and by the will of
Providence the goddess Ganga herself flowed to Kendubilva and bestowed her mercy on him. According to this tradition, it
was there, in his homeland, that his pastimes as a sadhaka came to a close; in his memory a fair, the Jayadeva-mela, is
held at Kendubilva every year to this day, on Magha-sankranti.[^18] <!-- F52 F53 -->

But even those who say he ended his days in Kendubilva admit that his Deities did not go there: nowhere is it said that
he brought Them to his homeland. After his disappearance, Sri Radha-Madhava, whose service was dearer to him than life,
were taken by the king of Jaipur and installed at a place called Ghati; They are served in Jaipur to this day.
<!-- F54 -->

He left this world on krishna-shashthi, the sixth day of the dark fortnight of the month of Pausha. In our calendar this
is Madhava-masa, krishna-shashthi; in 2026 the day of his departure fell on 9 January, and in 2027 it falls on 28
January.[^19] <!-- F55 -->

## Mahaprabhu Relishes the “Gita-govinda”
Jayadeva's song sounds in the “Charitamrita” from the Lord's early pastimes to the last. Already in Navadvipa, having
defeated the digvijayi Keshava Kashmiri in debate, the young Nimai consoles the poet by saying that flaws are found even
in the greatest:

“Even in the poetry of Bhavabhuti, Jayadeva and Kalidasa faults appear.” (CC, Adi 16.101)

Srila Bhaktisiddhanta in the “Anubhashya” on this verse refers the reader to his account of Jayadeva at Adi 13.42.
<!-- F70 -->

Verses of the “Gita-govinda” sound also in the Lord's conversation with Ramananda Raya on the bank of the Godavari. When
Ramananda tells how Krishna left the rasa dance and sorrowfully searched for Radha alone, the “Charitamrita” quotes:

“Placing Radha in His heart — the chain that bound His desire for the very essence of the rasa — Krishna, the enemy of
Kamsa, left the other beauties of Vraja.” (CC, Madhya 8.105; “Gita-govinda” 3.1)

It is followed by verse 3.2 — how Krishna, full of remorse, searched for Radha and sank down in despair in a grove on the
bank of the Yamuna (Madhya 8.106); and the spring song of the “Gita-govinda” (1.11) — “Friend, see how Sri Hari sports in
spring!” — the “Charitamrita” quotes in the same conversation (Madhya 8.143), and earlier still at Adi 4.224. Verse 3.1
appears also at Adi 4.219; there Srila Bhaktisiddhanta explains that Krishna left the place of the rasa and went to
Radha, the chief shelter of the rasa-lila. <!-- F66 -->

When Svarupa Damodara came to Puri, the Lord gained one who knew His heart. Krishnadasa Kaviraja writes:

“Anything contrary to the conclusions of bhakti, or any *rasabhasa* (a mere semblance of rasa), brought the Lord's heart
no delight when He heard it. Therefore Svarupa Gosani examined everything, and only if it was pure did he let the Lord
hear it. With the songs of Vidyapati and Chandidasa and the ‘Sri Gita-govinda’ — these three — he delighted the Lord.”
(CC, Madhya 10.113–115)

Bhaktivinoda Thakura in the “Amrita-pravaha-bhashya” calls the “Sri Gita-govinda” the famous poem of Sri Jayadeva, full
of Sanskrit songs of krishna-rasa. <!-- F57 Q2 F72 -->

Above all the Lord relished it in His last twelve years, when, steeped in the bhava of Radha, He lived in the Gambhira in
unceasing separation from Krishna:

“Just as Sri Radha raved on seeing Uddhava, so He raved in His divine madness night and day. With Ramananda and Svarupa
He relished the songs of Vidyapati, Jayadeva and Chandidasa.” (CC, Adi 13.41–42) <!-- F56 Q1 -->

“With Svarupa and Ramananda, Mahaprabhu night and day sang and heard the songs of Chandidasa and Vidyapati, Raya's drama,
the ‘Karnamrita’ and the ‘Sri Gita-govinda’ in supreme bliss.” (CC, Madhya 2.77)

In the “Anubhashya” on this verse Srila Bhaktisiddhanta explains that the “songs” here are the singing of verses of
Chandidasa, Vidyapati, Ramananda, Bilvamangala and Jayadeva.[^20] <!-- F60 Q5 F72 -->

Svarupa and Ramananda consoled the Lord in His separation:

“Those two consoled the Lord: Svarupa sang songs, and Raya recited verses. With the verses and songs of the
‘Krishna-karnamrita,’ of Vidyapati and of the ‘Sri Gita-govinda’ they gave the Lord joy.” (CC, Antya 15.26–27)
<!-- F61 -->

One night the feelings of the gopis who searched for Krishna on the night of the rasa dance awoke in the Lord, and
Svarupa sang for Him a verse from the “Gita-govinda”:

“My mind remembers Hari, who here in the rasa dance enjoyed His pastimes and His jests.” (CC, Antya 15.84;
“Gita-govinda” 2.3)[^21] <!-- F62 -->

So it was day after day:

“Whatever feeling arose in the Lord, Svarupa Mahashaya would sing a song to suit it. And Raya Ramananda would recite
verses suited to His feelings from Vidyapati, Chandidasa and the ‘Sri Gita-govinda.’” (CC, Antya 17.5–6)

And on one such night, when the Lord, coming back to Himself for a moment after deep trance, asked, “Svarupa, sing
something sweet,” Svarupa sang songs of Vidyapati and of the “Gita-govinda,” and they soothed Mahaprabhu's ears
(Antya 17.62).[^22] <!-- F58 Q3 F59 Q4 -->

A song of the “Gita-govinda” once nearly drew the Lord through a thorny thicket. Walking to the temple of Yameshvara, He
heard a devadasi singing the “Gita-govinda” in the raga gurjari and, forgetting everything, ran towards the voice,
unaware of the thorns piercing His body; His servant Govinda managed to hold Him back (Antya 13.78–88). Another time,
roaming the Jagannatha-vallabha garden, the Lord had His associates sing the spring song of the “Gita-govinda,” “Lalita-lavanga-
lata,” and danced to it (Antya 19.84). <!-- F67 F68 -->

And at the very end of the “Charitamrita,” summing up the Lord's last pastimes, Krishnadasa Kaviraja writes:

“Whatever verses of Jayadeva, of the ‘Bhagavata,’ of Ramananda Raya's drama and of the ‘Karnamrita’ suited each mood, He
would recite in that mood and relish, absorbed in that emotion.” (CC, Antya 20.67–68) <!-- F69 -->

## His Memory Among the Gaudiya Vaishnavas
Later generations of devotees too honoured Jayadeva's poem. The commentary “Krishna-vallabha” on the
“Krishna-karnamrita,” known under the name of Gopala Bhatta Gosvami, quotes the “Gita-govinda”; among the works of
Prabodhananda Sarasvati a commentary on it is also named. In the “Pada-kalpataru” of Vaishnava Dasa there is a song,
“Sri Jayadeva-vandana,” signed with the name “dasa Raghunatha”: it glorifies the merciful Jayadeva, beloved of Padmavati,
whose form is the very rasa of love for Radha-Madhava, who composed the sweet “Gita-govinda” — his beautiful abode, the
song says, is Kendubilva; and it glorifies the feet of Padmavati as well.[^23] <!-- F63 F64 F65 -->

## Significance
Jayadeva was not, in the narrow sense, a companion of the Lord in His earthly pastimes. He belongs to those pure
devotees in whose hearts, in the words of Srila Bhaktivinoda Thakura, the bhava of Gaura lived even before His
appearance; and the Lord, when He came, relished their songs as His own innermost treasure. His poem is not mundane
lyric but a song of the transcendental separation and union of Radha and Govinda, and Mahaprabhu, Krishna Himself in the
mood of Radha, listened to it in the Gambhira to His last days. <!-- F15 I5 -->

Jayadeva's own life, as the traditions preserve it, is a lesson in bhakti. His name is explained as “the one who reveals
Krishna”; the Lord completes his song and thatches the roof of his hut, because He is devoted to His devotee. Maimed, in
a well, he repeats the name of Hari; he answers those who wronged him with honour; and the name of Krishna on his lips
brings Padmavati back to life. But the “Gita-govinda,” as he himself warns and Srila Bhaktisiddhanta after him, opens
only to those whose hearts long to remember Hari and to serve Sri Radha. <!-- I1 I2 I3 I4 -->
