# Minaketana Ramadasa
*Минакетана Рамадас · @minaketana-ramadasa*

## The Avadhuta's Servant
Listing the branches of Nityananda's tree, Krishnadasa Kaviraja names among them Minaketana Ramadasa:

“Gopala the dancer, Ramabhadra, Gaurangadasa, Nrisimha-chaitanya and Minaketana Ramadasa.” (CC, Adi 11.53)

He tells of him at length in the fifth chapter of the Adi-lila, where he recalls how he himself came to Vrindavana by
Nityananda's mercy. The story opens thus:

“Avadhuta Gosani had a servant who was an abode of love; his name was Minaketana Ramadasa.” (CC, Adi 5.161)

In the Bengali he is a *bhritya*, a servant of the Avadhuta Gosani, and an “abode of prema” (*prema-dhama*).[^1] Further
on in the same story Kaviraja calls him now Ramadasa, now Minaketana. In the “Bhakti-ratnakara” he is “Sri Minaketana
Ramadasa, the charming one,” in the “Narottama-vilasa” “Sri Minaketana Ramadasa Mahashaya.” The biographies also call
him Sri Minaketana or simply Ramadasa;[^2] one of them speaks of him as “a devotee of Nityananda,” another as Nityananda's
“dear associate” and “follower.” <!-- F1 F2 Q1 -->

Who his parents were, and where and when he appeared in this world, is not known. Nor does anyone say when or where he
left this world, and the Vaishnava calendar of Sri Chaitanya Saraswat Math has no days for him. Even the place where he
lived is not stated exactly: the “Chaitanya-charitamrita” says only that he came to Jhamatpur by invitation, while the
biographies suppose that he lived somewhere nearby or in Jhamatpur itself.[^3] <!-- F3 F4 -->

The “Charitamrita” calls Nityananda the Avadhuta — a name for a wanderer bound by no outward rules. His servant was an
avadhuta too. In the “Anubhashya” on this verse Srila Bhaktisiddhanta Sarasvati cites Sridhara Svami's gloss (on the
“Bhagavatam,” 3.1.19): an avadhuta is *asamskrita-deha*, one who takes no care of his body and bears no ritual marks on
it. The disciple of the Avadhuta Nityananda, Srila Bhaktisiddhanta explains, is likewise an eternally perfect
maha-bhagavata, a paramahamsa above varnashrama: his body bore the marks of no varna and no ashrama; he was absorbed in
the mood of Vraja and gave no thought to the body. One of the biographies calls him exactly that — a maha-bhagavata, or
paramahamsa — the words with which the “Anubhashya” heads the next verse. Here lies the key to all that happened later
in Jhamatpur: Minaketana's actions were neither oddities nor rudeness, but the rapture of a devotee for whom outward
rules no longer mattered. <!-- F8 Q6 -->

## The Other Sankarshana
Who he is in the Lord's eternal pastimes Kavi Karnapura tells. Speaking of Nityananda's associates, he writes:

“The *vyuha* of Sankarshana, called the One who lies on the Ocean of Milk, became Virachandra, whose form is
non-different from Chaitanya; into him, for the sake of the work, entered the brothers Nishatha and Ulmuka. Minaketana
Ramadasa is the other Sankarshana, the original *vyuha*.” (“Gaura-ganoddesha-dipika” 67–68)

Nishatha and Ulmuka are sons of Balarama; according to the verse, they entered Virachandra (Virabhadra), the son of
Nityananda, while Minaketana Ramadasa is one person, himself “the other Sankarshana.” This is how Srila Bhaktisiddhanta
too reads the verse in the “Anubhashya” on CC, Adi 11.53: Nishatha and Ulmuka entered Virachandra for a purpose, and
Minaketana Rama is another *vyuha* of Sankarshana.[^4] He is one of the intimate associates of Nityananda-Balarama, and
so whatever touched his Lord's honour touched him too. <!-- F5 F6 F7 Q4 -->

## Kirtana in Jhamatpur
Krishnadasa Kaviraja lived at that time in Jhamatpur, a village near Naihati, and in his house the Vaishnavas sang the
holy names day and night. He recalls:

“In my house there was sankirtana day and night, and he came there by invitation.” (CC, Adi 5.162)

According to the “Anubhashya,” “day and night” (*ahoratra*) means eight praharas, a full day and night; pure devotees
had the custom of sending letters of invitation to such kirtana festivals. One biography calls this festival an
eight-prahara sankirtana; another, a day-long kirtana arranged by Kaviraja himself. When it took place the sources do
not say; it is clear only that it was before Kaviraja left for Vrindavana. <!-- F9 -->

“Full of great love, he sat down in the courtyard, and all the Vaishnavas bowed at his feet. As they bowed, he would
climb on the back of one, and in his love strike another with his flute or slap yet another.” (CC, Adi 5.163–164)

His appearance and the signs of his love amazed all the guests, and nearly everyone came up to bow to him. And he,
forgetful of himself, would climb onto one — onto his shoulders, says one biography — slap another, and strike a third
with his flute. The flute in his hand was a cowherd boy's flute: the mood of Vraja in which he was absorbed showed even
in his outward form. Those blows were no impertinence, but the play of a maha-bhagavata whom love had raised above all
rules of courtesy. <!-- F10 F11 -->

“From those eyes, the very sight of which brings tears to one's heart, flowed an unbroken stream of tears.” (CC, Adi
5.165)

Srila Bhaktivinoda Thakura in the “Amrita-pravaha-bhashya” adds: one had only to look into his eyes, and tears would
rise of themselves. <!-- F12 -->

“At times I saw the hair on some limb stand on end like kadamba blossoms; one limb would stiffen while another
trembled. When he roared out ‘Nityananda!’, all who saw it were filled with wonder.” (CC, Adi 5.166–167)

One biography says that all eight sattvika-bhavas, the wondrous signs of divine love, appeared on his body; the
“Charitamrita” names among them horripilation, stupor and trembling, as well as tears. Another biography remarks that
whenever he uttered Nityananda's name these transformations came over his body, and people marvelled. Intoxicated with
prema, he called out “Nityananda!” in a thunderous roar (*hunkara*), and the hearts of all around opened to divine
bliss. <!-- F13 F14 -->

## “A Second Romaharshana”
In Kaviraja's house a brahmana named Gunarnava Mishra served the Deity:

“A respectable brahmana named Gunarnava Mishra was serving the Deity there.” (CC, Adi 5.168)

He served the Deity (*sri-murti*) with faith, but had no wish to give due honour to the Lord's devotee. Srila
Bhaktisiddhanta in the “Anubhashya” calls such a devotee a *kanishtha-adhikari*, a devotee of the lowest stage: he has
faith enough in the Deity, but cannot recognize a Vaishnava and honour him, and so offends his feet. One biography calls
Gunarnava the pujari of the house, another a priest (*purohita*) who performed the puja of the Deity.[^5] <!-- F15 -->

“Ramadasa sat in the courtyard, but the brahmana did not greet him; seeing this, Minaketana said angrily: ‘Here is a
second Romaharshana Suta, who did not rise to greet Baladeva when he saw Him!’” (CC, Adi 5.169–170)

Everyone bowed before Ramadasa, while the servant of the Deity did not so much as address him.[^6] Minaketana understood
that this brahmana had no faith in Nityananda and was slighting him precisely as Nityananda's servant — and lost all
regard for him. One biography remarks that he spoke the words about Romaharshana to teach the world. <!-- F16 F17 F18 Q2 -->

The comparison comes from the “Bhagavatam” (10.78.22–28), as both the “Anubhashya” and the “Amrita-pravaha-bhashya”
point out. In Naimisharanya Romaharshana Suta, a disciple of Vyasa, was reciting the “Bhagavatam” at the sages' request,
seated on the vyasasana. When Baladeva arrived, everyone rose to meet Him; only Romaharshana did not come down from his
seat or greet Him — and for this Baladeva punished him, taking his life. Gunarnava acted just as unfittingly: to fail to
honour Nityananda's servant is to fail to honour Balarama Himself. One biography draws a lesson from this: a proud man
is fit neither to speak the “Bhagavatam,” which is non-different from Krishna, nor to serve the Deity. <!-- F19 I2 -->

Can Minaketana be called proud for this anger? Bhaktivinoda Thakura answers: devotees do not blame him and do not call
him proud. It was anger not for himself but for his Lord, the anger of Balarama's servant and friend — and how little
worldly resentment there was in it the “Charitamrita” shows at once:

“Having said this, he danced and sang and rejoiced; but the brahmana went on with his service to Krishna and showed no
anger.” (CC, Adi 5.171)

The “Anubhashya” heads this verse: even when a Vaishnava is offended, he does not look for faults in others. Nor did the
brahmana answer anger with anger: he quietly went on serving Krishna.[^7] <!-- F20 F21 I4 -->

## The Broken Flute
The festival ended, and Ramadasa, having blessed everyone, made ready to leave. Then came what Kaviraja goes on to
describe:

“At the end of the festival he was leaving, having given his blessings, when a dispute arose between him and my
brother. My brother had firm faith in Chaitanya Prabhu, but towards Nityananda only a semblance of faith.” (CC, Adi
5.172–173)

In the Bengali, the brother had towards Nityananda only *vishvasa-abhasa*, “a semblance of faith.” He had seen how the
guest behaved, and he started an argument with him; according to one biography, this was after Gunarnava had left. The
brother's name, according to the “Gaudiya-vaishnava-abhidhana,” was Shyamadasa. <!-- F22 F23 -->

“Knowing this, Ramadasa was grieved at heart, and so I rebuked my brother: ‘The two brothers are one body, equally
manifest. If you do not accept Nityananda, you will be utterly ruined. You have faith in one but show the other no
respect: your standard is the logic of half a hen. If you accept neither, you are an atheist; and to accept one and not
the other is hypocrisy.’” (CC, Adi 5.174–177)

The brother's unbelief wounded Ramadasa deeply. But Kaviraja took his side and rebuked his brother before the guest had
gone:[^8] Nityananda is Baladeva Himself, and it is wrong to think Him lower than Chaitanya. Faith in Chaitanya without
faith in Nityananda is not devotion but hypocrisy; and so one cannot say of the brother that he was a good devotee who
lacked only one thing: the Two Brothers are one body.[^9] <!-- F24 F25 F27 Q3 -->

“In anger Ramadasa broke his flute and went away, and at that very moment my brother was ruined.” (CC, Adi 5.178)

The cowherd's flute he had brought to the festival was broken — thus Minaketana left a house where his Lord was not
honoured. According to one biography, he simply left, displeased. The Bengali says of the brother: “at that very moment
my brother met with utter ruin” (*sarva-nasha*). One biography explains that though the brother was devoted to Gaura,
the offence against a devotee destroyed him and he fell; another, that he lost all his devotion. Ramadasa did not curse
him: he only went away, and the ruin was the fruit of the offence against a Vaishnava and of unbelief in
Nityananda.[^10] <!-- F26 F28 -->

## Mercy for the Defender
Kaviraja tells this story not to condemn his brother. He sums up:

“Thus I have told of the power of His servant. Now I tell of something more — the nature of His mercy. Because I had
rebuked my brother — and He counted this as a virtue — that night the Lord appeared to me. In a village called
Jhamatapura, near Naihati, Nityananda-Rama appeared to me in a dream.” (CC, Adi 5.179–181)

Kaviraja had only stood up for Nityananda's servant — and this small thing was enough for the Lord. In the dream
Nityananda revealed His form to him, surrounded by His associates in the dress of cowherds, and said:

“‘O Krishnadasa, Krishnadasa, do not be afraid! Go to Vrindavana: there everything can be attained.’ Saying this, He
touched me with His hand and sent me forth; and the Lord disappeared with His associates.” (CC, Adi 5.195–196)

So he gained a life in Vrindavana, and, according to one biography, the service of the feet of Radha and Krishna as
well. Another biography tells the dream thus: Nityananda with His associates encouraged him and told him to go to
Vrindavana for his highest good. <!-- F29 -->

The lesson is twofold. An offence against a devotee ruins even one who is faithful to Gaura; and, conversely, merely
defending Nityananda's servant was enough to win His mercy and Vrindavana. One biography concludes that by serving
Nityananda's associates one soon obtains His mercy. Kaviraja's story also shows how dear Minaketana is to Nityananda;
and such are all His followers: they deliver the most fallen and fulfil every desire. <!-- I1 I3 -->

## In Jahnava's Company
Later we see Minaketana Ramadasa in the company of Jahnava-devi, Nityananda's shakti. When a great festival was being
prepared in Kheturi, Narottama's birthplace, word came to Khardaha, and Nityananda's associates asked Vasudha and
Jahnava for leave to go there. The “Narottama-vilasa” lists them; among them:

“Sri Minaketana Ramadasa Mahashaya, Nakadi, Sri Balarama and others full of prema. All submitted at the feet of the two
Ishvaris that they wished to go to Kheturi.” (“Narottama-vilasa” 6.190–191) <!-- F32 -->

Narahari Chakravarti tells in the “Bhakti-ratnakara” how Jahnava set out from Khardaha:[^11]

“With her went the maha-bhagavatas, at the sight of whom the three worlds are purified: Krishnadasa Sarakhela, Madhava
Acharya, Raghupati Vaidya Upadhyaya, most noble, Sri Minaketana Ramadasa, the charming one, Murari Chaitanya, Jnanadasa,
Mahidhara.” (“Bhakti-ratnakara” 10.372–374)

The words that the mere sight of these Vaishnavas purifies the three worlds are said of the whole company — and so of
Ramadasa too. With Jahnava also went Kamalakara Pippalai, Sri Shankara and many others;[^12] her way lay through
Ambika-Kalna, Navadvipa and Shantipur. The “Prema-vilasa” (19.306–311) also names Minaketana Ramadasa among those who
came with Jahnava from Khardaha. <!-- F30 F31 F35 Q5 -->

On the second day of the festival in Kheturi, in the courtyard of the house where Jahnava stayed, he sat down to the
meal with her other companions (“Narottama-vilasa” 8.48–50). And when Jahnava set out from Kheturi for Vrindavana, she
told him, Vrindavana, Kamalakara Pippalai and a few other Vaishnavas to return to Khardaha:

“Sri Minaketana Ramadasa, Vrindavana, Kamalakara Piplai and some others — to all these the Ishvari gave the command to
go to Khardaha; having taken leave, no one could be calm.” (“Narottama-vilasa” 8.152–153) <!-- F33 F34 -->

Nothing more is known of him.

## Significance
Minaketana Ramadasa is a servant of the Avadhuta Nityananda, an “abode of prema,” one of the branches of His tree, and
in the eternal pastimes “the other Sankarshana.” His rapture of love — tears that made everyone around him weep, the
thunderous call “Nityananda!”, the blows of his flute that puzzle those who measure a Vaishnava by outward rules —
shows what Nityananda's associates are like. His anger at disrespect to Nityananda did not make him proud: devotees do
not blame him, and he himself at once danced and sang. Through him Krishnadasa Kaviraja received the lesson that began
his journey to Vrindavana: the Two Brothers are one body, and to slight Nityananda's servant brings ruin, while to
defend him brings His mercy. <!-- F2 F7 F28 F29 I1 I3 I4 -->
