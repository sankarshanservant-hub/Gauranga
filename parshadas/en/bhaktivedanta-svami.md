# A. C. Bhaktivedanta Swami Prabhupada
*His Divine Grace A. C. Bhaktivedanta Swami Prabhupada · @bhaktivedanta-svami*

## The Founder-Acharya
His Divine Grace A. C. Bhaktivedanta Swami was a disciple of Srila Bhaktisiddhanta Sarasvati and the founder-acharya
of the International Society for Krishna Consciousness (ISKCON); his followers call him Srila Prabhupada. One of the
biographies glorifies him as a rasika-acharya — a teacher who knows the taste of pure devotion just as Sri Chaitanya
gave it to the world. Such a gift is rare, and the biographer recalls the words of the “Srimad-Bhagavatam”: “Among
many millions of the liberated and the perfected, O great sage, a peaceful devotee of Narayana is rarely found”.
(Srimad-Bhagavatam, 6.14.5) <!-- F1 F2 Q1 -->

His whole life grew out of a single command. In 1922, at their very first meeting, Srila Bhaktisiddhanta told him
to preach the teaching of Sri Chaitanya throughout the world; forty-three years later, at nearly seventy, the
disciple sailed for America alone, without money or companions, to carry that command out. <!-- F28 F59 -->

## Childhood and Youth
Srila Bhaktivedanta Swami appeared in this world on 1 September 1896 in Calcutta — on Nandotsava, the day after
Janmashtami[^1]. The boy was named Abhay Charan De. His father, Gour Mohan De, and his mother were Vaishnavas who
worshipped Krishna. His father’s house, as Srila Govinda Maharaj recalled, stood on “Hazen Road”, in one of the best
quarters of the city. <!-- F18 F12 F20 F113 -->

Following Bengali custom, the parents called an astrologer to cast the child’s horoscope. Among other things he
foretold that when the boy reached seventy he would cross the ocean, become a great preacher and open a hundred and
eight temples. The book “Our Teachers”, published by our mission, says that this prediction came true in
full. <!-- F19 -->

Abhay grew up in Vedic culture, and from the age of about six he was already serving the Deities of Sri Radha and Sri
Krishna himself. When the time came for study, his father sent him to the Scottish Church College in Calcutta. The
times were troubled: Hindus and Muslims were at odds, the movement for independence from Britain was gaining
strength, and the college schooled young men in Western ways, its professors often disparaging the ancient culture
of India without any ground. Abhay, raised in a Vaishnava family, saw the narrowness of such an education, yet he
went on studying because his family wished it. <!-- F20 F21 -->

Absorbed in his studies, he gave up the worship, and the Deities were put away in a box for a time. Then Krishna
appeared to him in a dream and asked: “Why have you put Me in a box? Why do you not worship Me?” Abhay returned to
his service. Many years later he wrote to one of his disciples: “There has never been a moment in my life when I did
not remember Krishna. I have always known Him, from my very birth.” <!-- F22 F23 -->

While still a student Abhay was married; the match was arranged by his father. Like many of his generation he
answered Mahatma Gandhi’s call: in 1920 he sat the examinations of his fourth year and, in protest, refused his
diploma. What drew him to Gandhi was the conviction that spiritual values must rule the life of society, together
with loyalty to the Vedic tradition. He then entered the service of Dr. Kartik Chandra Bose, a well-known Calcutta
surgeon and pharmaceutical manufacturer; but the work occupied his thoughts far less than his spiritual
search. <!-- F24 F25 F26 -->

## Meeting His Guru
Abhay first heard of Srila Bhaktisiddhanta Sarasvati in 1922. He had seen many sadhus — his father welcomed them
warmly into his home — but some of those meetings had brought him nothing but disappointment. So when a friend
invited him to a festival of the Gaudiya Math to meet the Math’s acharya, Abhay was sceptical; still, the friend
persuaded him. <!-- F27 -->

The first words they heard from Srila Bhaktisiddhanta were a call: “You are educated young men. You must preach the
teaching of Lord Sri Chaitanya throughout the world.” That meeting turned Abhay’s life around. <!-- F28 -->

He put a pointed question: who would listen to such preaching while India was a dependent country? Should not
independence come first? Srila Bhaktisiddhanta answered: “Krishna consciousness does not depend on who is in power.
Humanity needs it so badly that we have no right to wait until India gains independence.” Every government is
temporary, he explained; Krishna consciousness alone is eternal, and a person’s true self is the soul. Then he quoted
in Sanskrit the verse of the “Bhagavad-gita” in which the Lord calls on us to give up all other duties and surrender
to Him alone (by its sense, 18.66). <!-- F29 Q12 -->

The guru, “Our Teachers” observes, is not an external authority or an office-holder to be obeyed blindly: he gives
convincing answers to spiritual questions and dispels doubts, and by hearing him a sincere disciple is freed from
every obstacle on the path. So it was with Abhay. Later he recalled that on that evening he had, in essence, accepted
Srila Bhaktisiddhanta as his guru: “Not formally,” he would say, “but in my heart. I understood that I had met a
real saint.” <!-- I5 F30 -->

The business of the pharmaceutical firm took Abhay and his family to Allahabad (Prayaga). There he rented a house and
a small shop and sold Dr. Bose’s products; Srila Govinda Maharaj recalled that Jawaharlal Nehru and Indira Gandhi used
to come to him as customers. In 1928 Bhakti Pradipa Tirtha, one of the leading sannyasis of the Gaudiya Math, opened a
branch of the mission in Allahabad and asked Abhay for financial help. He gave it gladly and often invited the
devotees of the Math to his home. <!-- F31 F113 -->

“Our Teachers” calls Gour Mohan De a great Vaishnava. Before his departure in 1930 he asked his son to support the
preaching of the Gaudiya Math in every way and to do all he could for Srila Bhaktisiddhanta’s disciples. Abhay loved
his father dearly and fulfilled his wish. <!-- F32 -->

In 1932 Abhay received initiation from Srila Bhaktisiddhanta — harinama and the gayatri mantra on the same day; he
had been recommended to the guru by Srila B. K. Tapasvi Maharaj. He still remained a family man and travelled on the
business of a pharmaceutical firm. He wished to become rich, but only so as to serve his guru’s mission with money.
He could not travel with Srila Bhaktisiddhanta or see him often, yet he planned his journeys so as to be in Calcutta
whenever his guru was there. The thirty years he was still to spend in India are the story of a single desire that
grew stronger in him year by year: to fulfil his guru’s will and preach Krishna consciousness throughout the
world. <!-- F42 F43 -->

## A Godbrother
Abhay first met Srila B. R. Sridhar Maharaj in 1930 in Allahabad. Sridhar Maharaj had not yet taken sannyasa: he had
left home and come to Allahabad as a vanaprastha, dressed in white. More than forty years later, at the opening of the
temple in Mayapur in 1973, Srila Bhaktivedanta Swami recalled those days: “Krishna and Prabhupada [Srila
Bhaktisiddhanta] chose him to prepare me. Sridhar Maharaj lived in my house for many years, and naturally our
relations were most intimate. I have always taken his instructions and advice very seriously, because I see in him a
pure devotee. I sought his company and tried to help him. We are very close spiritually.” <!-- F33 F34 -->

Though they were Godbrothers, he always addressed Sridhar Maharaj as “Your Divine Grace” and “Om Vishnupad”, as one
addresses only one’s guru. In this, “Our Teachers” says, lies the whole essence of their lila: he went beyond
etiquette, and such regard for a Godbrother has no precedent. For more than five years they kept close company,
often for five to seven hours a day, and he was sure that it was Sridhar Maharaj who had prepared him for preaching
in the West. Other disciples of Srila Bhaktisiddhanta felt the same towards Sridhar Maharaj — Bhakti Prajnana
Keshava, Bhakti Saranga Goswami, Bhakti Dayita Madhava, Bhakti Pramoda Puri: all of them regarded him as their
shiksha-guru. <!-- F35 I6 F10 -->

Sridhar Maharaj himself told of one of their talks. It concerned the verse of the “Bhagavad-gita” in which Krishna
speaks of the souls surrendered to Him: “dadami buddhi-yogam tam yena mam upayanti te” — “I give them the inner divine
inspiration that helps them come close to Me” (10.10). Sridhar Maharaj remarked that the word “upayanti” here points
to parakiya-rasa, and his companion agreed: “Yes, what is meant here is precisely the parakiya-rasa of Vraja. Nothing
else can be the highest conclusion of the ‘Bhagavad-gita’.” Another time Sridhar Maharaj asked what he thought of his
reading of several verses of the “Gita” in the light of madhura-rasa, and heard: “What can I say? Your interpretation
is absolutely correct.” <!-- F36 Q9 -->

They preached together, too. Abhay, then still a householder, opened an office in Bombay for his business; together
with Sridhar Maharaj he opened a temple of the Gaudiya Math there, and they formed two parties to collect donations.
One was made up of himself, Sridhar Maharaj and Bhakti Saranga Goswami; they called on his pharmacist acquaintances
and collected about five thousand rupees. “I made the introductions,” he recalled, “Sridhar Maharaj preached, and
Goswami Maharaj collected the donations.” <!-- F37 -->

Satsvarupa Dasa Goswami, one of his disciples, writes in his biography that Abhay often accompanied Sridhar Maharaj
and his assistants and played the mridanga at their programmes, and when Sridhar Maharaj was ill he led the party
himself: he led the kirtan, played the mridanga and lectured on the “Srimad-Bhagavatam”. He acknowledged Sridhar
Maharaj as “a pure Vaishnava, a pure devotee”, greatly valued his advice and discussed with him even what spiritual
benefit his failures in business and the discord in his family were bringing him. <!-- F38 F39 -->

Abhay always helped Sridhar Maharaj’s preaching and highly prized his rare gift for bringing out the very essence of
the scriptures. When Sridhar Maharaj compiled the book “Sri Sri Prapanna-jivanamritam” — a collection of Sanskrit
verses, his own and those of devotees of the past, on the six limbs of sharanagati, full surrender to Krishna — Abhay
took the cost of publishing it upon himself. Srila Govinda Maharaj recalled how he said to his sister Bhavini Didi,
whom the Math called Pishima: “‘Sri Sri Prapanna-jivanamritam’ is a much-needed and wonderful book. You have money.
Please help to publish this book.” And it was she who gave the money for the first edition. <!-- F40 -->

Once in Bombay the two of them arranged a meeting with a sannyasi of the Gaudiya Math who had returned from Europe. He
came without his tridanda, without a shikha and without sannyasi dress, and said of the West: “They asked such
questions as cannot be answered.” Abhay began to draw out of him what the questions were, and Sridhar Maharaj
answered them one after another. Then Abhay declared: “Today Asia has conquered Europe!” <!-- F41 -->

## “Print Books”
One of his most important meetings with his guru took place in Vrindavana in 1935. Abhay was walking with Srila
Bhaktisiddhanta and several Godbrothers along the bank of Radha-kunda, and the guru shared his sorrow with him: some of
his senior disciples were quarrelling over the rooms and property of the Calcutta Math — what would happen when he was
gone? This “fire of factional interests”, once it flared up, would destroy everything. “It would be good,” he said,
“to take the marble off the temples and print books with the money.” Abhay took no part in those quarrels. And,
turning directly to him, Srila Bhaktisiddhanta said: “If you ever have money, print books.” Abhay felt that these
words had changed the course of his life. <!-- F47 -->

For Srila Bhaktisiddhanta’s Vyasa-puja of 1936 Abhay offered his guru a poem and an essay, and read them at a
gathering of his Godbrothers in Bombay. Both were received very warmly and printed in the journal “Harmonist”. The
essay amazed many devotees of the Math: this little-known Bengali so deeply understood the siddhanta of the Gaudiya
Vaishnavas and the position of the guru. His thought runs thus: whoever interprets the divine sound, shabda-brahma,
by his own imperfect perception, without being trained under a genuine acharya, inevitably deviates from Vyasadeva,
as the Mayavadis do, and cannot be a guru. He quoted the verse of the “Padma Purana”: “sampradaya-vihina ye mantras te
nishphala matah” — a mantra received outside the disciplic succession is fruitless. The transcendent is not known by
observation and experiment; it can only be heard — as sound descending through Sri Gurudeva and Sri Vyasadeva. And
therefore, he concluded, “to remove the disagreements among us, born of our unruly nature, we must surrender this
very day at the feet of the representative of Sri Vyasadeva.” <!-- F44 Q2 -->

One of his Godbrothers nicknamed Abhay “kavi”, “poet-sage”, and the name stuck. But what mattered more was that Srila
Bhaktisiddhanta read the poem. It struck at the very spearhead of his preaching, aimed against impersonalism, and it
gave him great joy; the editor of the “Harmonist” received the order: “Print whatever he writes.” Abhay later
revealed his philosophical and literary gift in full: in a short time he produced more than sixty books on Vaishnava
philosophy — a feat of service to his guru. <!-- F45 F46 -->

Srila Bhaktisiddhanta left this world in January 1937. A month earlier Abhay wrote to him that, being a family man,
he could not give all his time to service, and asked: “Could you not give me some definite service?” Two weeks later
the answer came: “I am quite sure that, knowing English, you will be able to convey our ideas to people who do not
know the languages [Bengali and Hindi]… This will bring good both to you and to your listeners. I very much hope
that you will be able to preach successfully in English.” Abhay took this letter as a confirmation of the command
of 1922, and from then on he had no doubt about the purpose of his life. <!-- F48 -->

Before his departure Srila Bhaktisiddhanta charged his senior disciples to continue the preaching in the West. He
had sent his first envoys to London in his lifetime, but his departure and the Second World War that soon followed
cut short what had been begun. Among the great Vaishnavas whom Srila Bhaktisiddhanta trained, “Our Teachers” names
Srila Sridhar Maharaj, Srila Bhaktivedanta Swami, Bhakti Prajnana Keshava, Bhakti Vilasa Tirtha, Bhakti Dayita
Madhava, Bhakti Pramoda Puri, Bhakti Saranga Goswami and many others; but, the book says, only one of them succeeded
in fully carrying out his wish for the West — Srila Bhaktivedanta Swami. <!-- F109 -->

## “Back to Godhead”
Carrying out his guru’s command, Abhay began writing commentaries on the “Bhagavad-gita”, and then conceived the idea
of publishing at his own expense a journal, “Back to Godhead”, on the philosophy of the Gaudiya Vaishnavas. He
thought out, wrote and typed its articles himself, alone, in his flat. He was living in Calcutta then and rented two
houses on Sitakanta Banerjee Lane: in one lived his family, in the other was his pharmaceutical laboratory. The upper
rooms of the laboratory he gave to his dear Godbrother Sridhar Maharaj and his disciples — among them the young
Gaurendu Brahmachari, the future Srila Govinda Maharaj. Thus Sridhar Maharaj’s Calcutta centre was housed in his home
for many years[^2]. <!-- F49 F50 -->

It was Gaurendu’s guru who told him to study the “Gita” under Abhay. “Guru Maharaj told me to study the ‘Srimad
Bhagavad-gita’ under Srila Swami Maharaj at the times when he himself was away from Calcutta,” Srila Govinda Maharaj
recalled. Abhay brought out two or three issues of the journal, but there were no funds to go on; huge stacks lay in
his room, and no one offered to distribute them. Then he said to Gaurendu: “Take the journal and distribute it, going
from door to door.” “So I became the first distributor of that journal,” Srila Govinda Maharaj related. “I did not
know a word of English, but I was very proud of the chance to distribute that journal.” Even then, in Calcutta,
Abhay at once saw unusual spiritual qualities in the youth. <!-- F51 F102 -->

In those same years he was translating the “Bhagavad-gita” into English and writing a commentary on it, discussing it
with Sridhar Maharaj. “When Srila Bhaktivedanta Swami Prabhupada began his translation of the ‘Bhagavad-gita’, he
consulted me on many points,” Sridhar Maharaj said in March 1982. It was an enormous labour, but the manuscript was
burnt through an employee’s carelessness, and in America he had to translate the “Gita” and write the commentary all
over again. <!-- F52 -->

The times were hard: India had lived through the Second World War, the post-war crisis and partition into two states.
Educated people and officials took almost no interest in the journal. Abhay himself went to the press to read the
proofs, himself printed the journal and himself distributed it. He also tried to found a “League of Devotees” — the
forerunner of the future International Society for Krishna Consciousness — and grew ever more convinced that only a
spiritual revival could lead civilisation out of its crisis. <!-- F53 -->

Srila Govinda Maharaj recalled that they lived then in Abhay’s house among Hindus, while right next door the Muslim
houses began. Clashes raged nearby, and at night bandits attacked homes. Abhay was much alarmed and once told Gaurendu
that bottles of incendiary mixture were kept in the attic: if they were attacked, he was to throw them at the
attackers. “Such were the conditions we lived in then, but afterwards, by the Lord’s mercy, it all came to an
end.” <!-- F54 -->

Starting the preaching was not easy. “Since he was a grihastha at that time, a family man, and I was a brahmachari in
saffron,” Srila Govinda Maharaj related, “he taught me a few shlokas from the ‘Srimad-Bhagavatam’ and at public
lectures gave me the vyasasana (the seat of honour of the speaker). ‘Recite these verses,’ he would say, ‘and then I will explain them to the
audience.’ That is how he began his preaching. His eagerness to spread Krishna consciousness was
immense.” <!-- F55 -->

## Vrindavana and Sannyasa
The years went by. His children grew up and became independent, and the pharmaceutical business, once profitable,
declined. His senior Godbrothers told Abhay that in this way the Lord, by His mercy, was freeing him from worldly ties
so that he could give his whole life to the service of Krishna. By 1950 he had withdrawn from business, handing it
over to his sons, and settled in Vrindavana, in the temple of Sri Sri Radha-Damodara, whose Deity Srila Rupa Gosvami
had revealed[^3]. Here he began the chief work of his life — an English translation, with commentary, of the
many-volumed “Srimad-Bhagavatam”, eighteen thousand verses. He chose the “Bhagavatam” as the most important and
authoritative Vaishnava scripture: he needed books for preaching, and he wanted not only to publish them but to bring
them to the West. <!-- F56 -->

The title “Bhaktivedanta”, later known throughout the world, was given to Abhay Charan De by Srila Sridhar Maharaj.
After Srila Bhaktisiddhanta’s departure the Gaudiya Math split into several missions, and many senior Vaishnavas took
shelter of Sridhar Maharaj. People came to him for advice on difficult questions; almost all the senior disciples of
Srila Bhaktisiddhanta took sannyasa from him, and others asked him from whom they should take it. So Abhay Charan
Prabhu, too, turned to Sridhar Maharaj — and received the name “Bhaktivedanta”. <!-- F57 -->

In 1959 he was given sannyasa by Sripad Bhakti Prajnana Keshava Maharaj, one of the senior disciples of Srila
Bhaktisiddhanta and the first sannyasa disciple of Sridhar Maharaj. He kept for him the title “Bhaktivedanta” and gave
him the name “Swami”. So Srila A. C. Bhaktivedanta Swami began his great service. <!-- F58 -->

## The Only Passenger of the “Jaladuta”
Shortly before his departure he asked Bhakti Ballabha Tirtha to come with him to America; but that devotee, secretary
of the Sri Chaitanya Gaudiya Math, was serving his guru and humbly declined. <!-- F8 -->

On 13 August 1965, a few days before his sixty-ninth birthday, Srila Bhaktivedanta Swami sailed for America to open
the teaching of Sri Chaitanya to the people of the West. Sumati Morarji, owner of the Scindia shipping company, was
on friendly terms with him, and he was taken as the only passenger on a small cargo ship, the “Jaladuta”. With him he
had a suitcase, an umbrella, a little grain, forty rupees — about seven dollars — a few books, and faith in the mercy
of his guru. Among the books were the “Srimad-Bhagavatam” and Sridhar Maharaj’s “Sri Sri
Prapanna-jivanamritam”. <!-- F59 F60 F61 -->

The voyage proved very hard. A few days out the ship ran into violent storms; besides seasickness, the Swami suffered
two heart attacks, and on the next two nights the attacks returned, so that he understood this journey might cost him
his life. On the third night Krishna Himself came to him in a dream, encouraged him and promised him full protection.
There were no more attacks. <!-- F62 -->

On Friday, 10 September 1965, he wrote in his diary: “Today the ship is not rolling, and I feel somewhat better. But I
feel the pain of separation from Vrindavana and my Lords: Sri Govinda, Gopinatha and Radha-Damodara. My only
consolation is the ‘Chaitanya-charitamrita’, reading which I relish the nectar of Lord Chaitanya’s lila. I have left
Bharata-bhumi only to fulfil the desire of Sri Bhaktisiddhanta Sarasvati, in which the will of Lord Chaitanya is
expressed. Having none of the needed qualifications, I am nevertheless taking the risk, wishing to carry out the order
of His Divine Grace. Being far from Vrindavana, I depend only on Their mercy.” <!-- F63 -->

Three days later he wrote a prayer to Krishna. You have brought me here, he says, so You must have some business here.
The people here are in the grip of passion and ignorance, content with their lives and indifferent to the message of
Vasudeva; but by Your causeless mercy even the impossible becomes possible, and only Your will can free them from
illusion. The words of the “Srimad-Bhagavatam” are Your incarnation, and humble hearing of the “Bhagavatam” purifies
the heart: here he quotes the verses of the “Bhagavatam” (1.2.17–21) on how hearing and service to a pure devotee
drive passion, ignorance and lust out of the heart. I myself am unfortunate and fallen, he goes on, and I can convince
them only with Your blessing; success or failure is in Your will. And further: “O Lord, I am a puppet in Your hands.
And since You have brought me here to dance, then make me dance, make me dance, O Lord, make me dance as You wish. I
have neither devotion nor knowledge, but I have firm faith in the Holy Name of Krishna. I have been named
Bhaktivedanta, and now, if You wish, You will help me to justify the true meaning of that name.” <!-- F64 Q3 -->

The voyage lasted thirty-seven days. He arrived in America utterly alone: all his property was what he had taken on
board — no money, no friends, no followers. But he firmly believed that by the mercy of the Lord and his guru he would
fulfil his mission. He himself said that he came to America “as an aggressor” — to challenge illusion. <!-- F65 F5 -->

## New York: The Birth of ISKCON
In September 1965 he came to New York, and for a whole year he struggled alone to found a movement. He lived very
simply and spoke about Krishna at every opportunity; little by little he managed to interest a few people. His first
winter in the West, that of 1965–1966, was the hardest. He went on translating and thought about how to open a temple
of Krishna in New York. <!-- F66 F67 -->

Then he moved to the Lower East Side of Manhattan, renting an apartment and a small shop, “Matchless Gifts”, with a
shop-window on Second Avenue, number 26. Word spread among young seekers of truth that an Indian swami had come who
taught a “special process of yoga” — the chanting of the Hare Krishna mantra — and people began coming to see him and
ask questions. The Lower East Side was then the centre of the hippies: in 1966 thousands of young people wandered its
streets in search of the meaning of life. He was given a task which, in the words of “Our Teachers”, no teacher before
him had known — to transplant Vedic culture into the most unsuitable soil; and it was these young people who were
destined to become his flock. <!-- F68 F69 -->

Soon he registered the International Society for Krishna Consciousness — ISKCON — of which he had dreamt while still
in India. The words “Krishna consciousness” are his rendering of the phrase “krishna-bhakti-rasa-bhavita” from Srila
Rupa Gosvami’s “Padyavali”. Krishnadasa Kaviraja quotes this verse as well: “krishna-bhakti-rasa-bhavita matih /
kriyatam yadi kuto ’pi labhyate…” — “The sweetness of bhakti, in the unfolding of an individual relationship with
Krishna, cannot be attained even by performing pious deeds for millions of births in a row. Only one thing is
needed — an ardent desire to obtain without delay such unalloyed devotion, wherever it may be manifest.” (CC, Madhya
8.70) Thus Mahaprabhu’s living teaching received the name by which the whole world came to know it. The premises the
Swami rented with the help of his first followers came to be called the “temple”. <!-- F70 Q4 F7 -->

When registering the Society in 1966 he set out its seven purposes, and in them, “Our Teachers” says, one sees how
clearly he foresaw the future scope of the movement. The Society was to bring spiritual knowledge to people
systematically and teach them spiritual practice, so as to restore the balance of values in society, unite people and
establish peace; to preach Krishna consciousness as it is set forth in the “Bhagavad-gita” and the
“Srimad-Bhagavatam”; to bring its members closer to one another and to Krishna, helping each to realise that every
soul is an inseparable part of Krishna; to spread sankirtana, the congregational chanting of the Holy Name, according
to the instructions of Sri Chaitanya Mahaprabhu; to build, in one of the holy places of Krishna’s pastimes, a city
for its members and for all people; to unite its members into one family and teach them a simple, natural way of
life; and for all this to publish journals and books. <!-- F71 -->

To his followers he was father and friend. The biographer sees in him a shaktyavesha-avatara — an envoy invested with
a special power of the Lord — and calls him “nityanandavesha”: the power of Sripada Nityananda Prabhu worked through
him. Flawless humility and full surrender to the Lord were joined in him with high demands on his disciples; yet his
insistence, too, was for the Lord’s sake — the active humility of one who carries out His will. Perfection and
liberation can be reached even without humility, the biographer observes, but pure devotion stands on humility
alone; Srila Bhaktivedanta Swami had both. <!-- F3 F4 I1 -->

## Preaching in the West
For twelve years, until his departure in 1977, he travelled the world, transplanting into foreign soil what many had
thought to be merely an Indian faith. He showed in practice that the teaching of the Gaudiya Vaishnavas belongs to no
single culture: it is the dharma of the soul, the eternal nature of everyone. He named Mahaprabhu’s living teaching
“Krishna consciousness” and carried it farther than the Gaudiya thinkers of his time had thought possible. To attack
illusion, the biographer says, is the highest service to people: its price is humility in the face of ingratitude,
and its reward is the dignity of the soul restored to many. What some sought by the sword and others by wealth he
achieved with a humble heart, flooding the world with love of God — and a day will come when all nations sing his
glory. <!-- F6 F7 I2 I3 -->

In 1967, back from America, he came at the invitation of the temple’s custodians to the temple of Uddharana Datta in
Saptagram, bringing his American disciples. The whole suvarna-vanik community honours this temple, and as a child he
had visited it with his parents; he tells of this himself in his commentary on the “Sri Chaitanya-charitamrita”
(Adi 11.41). <!-- F9 -->

## His Godbrothers
Among the disciples of Srila Bhaktisiddhanta, Srila Bhaktivedanta Swami — like Bhakti Prajnana Keshava, Bhakti
Saranga Goswami, Bhakti Dayita Madhava, Bhakti Pramoda Puri and others — regarded Srila B. R. Sridhar Maharaj as his
shiksha-guru. In a letter he writes that he accepts Sripad Sridhar Maharaj’s instruction “on my head”: he is his
constant well-wisher, and after the departure of Srila Sarasvati Thakura it is fitting to follow his guidance; from
him, too, he received the instruction to live “in this country” for good. <!-- F10 F11 -->

Bhakti Vidhan Mahayogi Swami, translating into English one of the biographies of the Lord’s associates, calls both
Srila Bhaktivedanta Swami and Srila Sridhar Maharaj his beloved spiritual masters, and relies on Srila Bhaktivedanta
Swami’s translation of the “Sri Chaitanya-charitamrita” with commentary. He also writes that Lochana Dasa’s song
“parama karuna pahun dui jana”, on the mercy of Nitai and Gaurachandra, was especially dear to Srila Bhaktivedanta

## Departure
Srila Bhaktivedanta Swami left this world in 1977, on the Gaura Chaturthi of the month of Damodara. His appearance day
is celebrated on the Krishna Navami of the month of Hrishikesha — Nandotsava, the day after Janmashtami[^4].
<!-- F12 -->

## Legacy
To all his followers he left a legacy of love, not of law. He gave to everyone, yet searched for the one who would
truly understand his message. His inner form, the biographer says, is revealed only to those who accept the essence
of his teaching and example: one can see his true nature only by his will. Deep realisation is the ability to apply
spiritual truth to differing circumstances; an acharya does not freeze in time but flows out in love to everyone who
reaches, however slightly, for pure devotion. <!-- F13 F14 I4 -->
