# Devakinandana Dasa
*Девакинандана Дас · @devakinandana-dasa*

## The Singer of the Vaishnavas
Devakinandana Dasa is known above all as a singer of the Vaishnavas. His “Vaishnava-vandana,”
a Bengali poem of obeisance in which he glorifies by name the companions of Sri Chaitanya and Nityananda and all the
Lord's devotees, is, in the words of one of the biographies, famous; another calls it an important book, because it
glorifies the lives of a great many Vaishnavas. Almost everything we know about him we know from his own verses and from
the few lines later authors devoted to him. <!-- F13 I2 -->

The biographies call him Sri Devakinandana Dasa. He signed his Bengali vandana in the same way, “Devakinandana Dasa”; in
the colophon of the Sanskrit “Vaishnavabhidhana” he is named Devakinandana Kaviraja. Manohara Dasa in the
“Anuraga-valli” calls him Sri Devakinandana Thakura, Yadunandana Dasa in the “Karnananda” calls him Daivakinandana, and
the padas quoted in the “Bhakti-ratnakara” are signed simply “Devakinandana.”[^1] <!-- F1 -->

## Disciple of Purushottama
Nothing definite is known of his family or origin. He lived in Kumarahatta, today Halisahar. One of the biographies says
that he, like the two other chief disciples of his guru, came from a brahmana family.[^2]
<!-- F2 F3 F4 -->

His mantra-guru was Sri Purushottama Dasa (Nagara Purushottama), the son of Sadashiva Kaviraja. Krishnadasa Kaviraja
names father and son among the branches of Nityananda's tree:

“Sri Sadashiva Kaviraja was a great soul, and Sri Purushottama dasa was his son. From his birth he was absorbed in the
feet of Nityananda, and he ceaselessly played childhood games with Krishna.” (CC, Adi 11.38–39)

The biographies name three chief disciples of Purushottama — Madhavacharya, Yadavacharya and Devakinandana.[^3] And
that the author of the “Vaishnava-vandana” was Purushottama's disciple, the vandana itself plainly says. <!-- F5 -->

Purushottama was a parshada — an associate — of Nityananda Prabhu, and therefore his disciple Devakinandana belonged to
the family (*parivara*) of Sri Nityananda. This kinship is not by blood but by diksha: a disciple's place in the Lord's
family is determined by his guru, and since the mantra-guru is a companion of Nityananda, the disciple too enters His
family. <!-- F6 I1 -->

Devakinandana writes of his guru in the vandana with reverence and love:

“I praise my chosen master named Sri Purushottama — who can describe his incomparable qualities? Even one who has no
good qualities he shows mercy to by the power of his natural compassion. In his seventh year he had the madness of love
for Krishna; his dancing enchants the world, his power is unfathomable.” (Devakinandana, “Vaishnava-vandana,” 87–89)

For him the guru is his *ishta-deva*, his worshipful master: a dear servant of the Lord whom the disciple honours as the
Lord Himself, though the guru is not the Lord. And the madness of love for Krishna that seized Purushottama in his
seventh year is neither a childhood illness nor worldly derangement, but the ecstasy of an eternal associate, absorbed
from birth in Krishna's pastimes.[^4] <!-- F8 Q1 -->

In the same vandana he also glorifies his guru's father:

“With one heart I praise Sadashiva Kaviraja: ceaselessly mad with prema, he is unaware of the outer world.”
(Devakinandana, “Vaishnava-vandana,” 71) <!-- F9 Q4 -->

And he begins the Sanskrit “Vaishnavabhidhana” by bowing to his guru — “first of all to my guru Sri Purushottama, whose
merciful glance has purified the earth — a wish-fulfilling tree that grants every desire” (v. 1); and, listing
Nityananda's companions, he calls him “my supreme one — Srila Sri Purushottama” (v. 31). <!-- F5 -->

## The Words of Manohara Dasa
Later Manohara Dasa recalls Devakinandana and his guru:

“Sri Purushottama Mahashaya, dear to Nityananda — his disciple is Sri Devakinandana Thakura. In the ‘Vaishnava-vandana’
that he composed he wrote of the four sampradayas; and in it the Madhva-sampradaya is given in the same way; he set
forth the meaning of these shlokas in Bengali.” (“Anuraga-valli” 8.44–46)

One of the biographies, quoting these words, gives them somewhat differently: “he composed the great
‘Vaishnava-vandana.’”[^5] <!-- F7 Q2 -->

## The “Vaishnava-vandana”
In the vandana which a biography quotes as his famous one, Devakinandana first bows at the feet of all the Vaishnavas
who live in Vrindavana. Then, falling to the ground, to all of Mahaprabhu's companions in Nilachala; with love, to all
of Mahaprabhu's devotees in Navadvipa; with obeisance, to the devotees in Gauda; with arms upraised, to Gauranga's
companions wherever they may live; and at last, with a straw between his teeth, to all the Lord's servants, past and
future. Each of them, he says, has the power to deliver the universe; the Vedas and Puranas sing their virtues; all of
Mahaprabhu's companions are saviours of the fallen, and so he, a sinner, longing to be purified, has taken shelter of
them.[^6] <!-- F12 Q3 -->

To glorify them, he says, is beyond his strength: his darkened understanding makes the attempt mere presumption. This
is Vaishnava humility, not a confession of pride: one who sings the praises of the devotees deems himself unworthy even
to speak of them. And yet to sing of them is the good fortune of a mute and the joy of the heart; and he begs the
Vaishnavas to forgive his fault and make this lowly one their servant. He names the fruit of the vandana himself: all
desires are fulfilled, the bonds of Yama are cut, and the treasure of love, rare in the world, comes within reach — in
his image it is as if plundered, so freely do the Vaishnavas give it away to those who glorify them. Release from the bonds of Yama
and prema come only by the Vaishnavas' grace, and with this longing, he concludes, speaks Devakinandana Dasa. <!-- F12 -->

The short “Vaishnava-vandana” published in 1910 has a hundred and forty-seven couplets and ends thus:

“He will gain that prema-bhakti which even the gods find hard to attain — with this longing speaks Devakinandana Dasa.”
(Devakinandana, “Vaishnava-vandana,” 147) <!-- F1 -->

The biographies quote lines of the vandana as well. Thus one of them writes of Sundarananda that the
“Vaishnava-vandana” of Sri Devakinandana Dasa says:

“With great hope I shall praise Sundarananda Thakura: he made kadamba flowers bloom on a lemon tree.”
(Devakinandana, “Vaishnava-vandana,” 84)[^7] <!-- F14 Q5 -->

Later Vaishnava authors too refer to his vandana. Besides Manohara Dasa, Yadunandana Dasa writes in the “Karnananda”:

“Daivakinandana composed the ‘Vaishnava-vandana’ and wrote these words in it: ‘I shall honour Gopala Bhatta in
Vrindavana, who always abides with Rupa and Sanatana.’” (“Karnananda” 5.179–180)

And Narahari Chakravarti in the “Bhakti-ratnakara” quotes his padas and a shloka from the “Vaishnavabhidhana.”

## Padas and the “Vaishnavabhidhana”
Devakinandana was an outstanding composer of padas. Two of his songs about Gaura are preserved in the
“Bhakti-ratnakara” (ch. 12). One begins, “In the midst of Nadia — what a form! Golden Gauranga dances…”, the other,
“No, there is no one but Gauranga, the merciful Lord…”. In the second he sings that in former descents, as Rama, the
Lord took up weapons and slew the asuras, but now, taking up no weapon and slaying no one, He has purified the hearts of
all. <!-- F10 -->

Besides the Bengali vandana he composed in Sanskrit the “Vaishnavabhidhana” — fifty-three
shlokas, closed by a colophon bearing the name Devakinandana Kaviraja. From it the “Bhakti-ratnakara” quotes a shloka on
Nityananda's parents, Padmavati and Mukunda. <!-- F11 -->

## His Time
When Devakinandana appeared in this world, and when and where he left it, the biographies do not say; nor is his samadhi
named. Nor are the days of his appearance and departure in the Vaishnava calendar of Sri Chaitanya Saraswat Math. His
time can be fixed only approximately: his guru Purushottama was a companion of Nityananda; the oldest dated manuscript
of the short vandana was copied around 1654, and the “Anuraga-valli” (around 1696) already knows his vandana. Of his
life before he met his guru there is no reliable information.[^8] <!-- F16 -->

## Significance
Devakinandana Dasa was the disciple of a companion of Nityananda and through him a member of His family; his place
among the Vaishnavas was determined by diksha, not by birth. His life is almost hidden from us, but his voice lives on
in the “Vaishnava-vandana”: a humble servant who called himself a sinner and a mute left a song in which the names of the
Lord's companions are gathered for all who seek their grace. For the biographies he is above all a singer
of the Vaishnavas, and in this lay his service: by glorifying the devotees he opened to others the way to that prema
which, in his faith, only they can bestow. <!-- F12 I1 I2 -->

    “Devaki-nandana” (“the joy of Devaki”) is also borne by Krishna Himself, the son of Devaki.
[^2]: Only one of the biographies says that he came from a brahmana family; the others do not name his caste. And the
    colophon of the Sanskrit vandana calls him “Kaviraja,” a title usually borne by physicians of the vaidya caste and
    by poets. Nothing can be asserted here.
[^3]: One of the biographies calls the first of them “Madhvacharya”; this is Madhavacharya, the husband of Ganga,
    Nityananda's daughter, not Madhvacharya, the founder of the sampradaya.

    Bhadra (1903) “I shall praise” (*বন্দিব*): the author is glorifying his guru.
[^5]: Our edition of the “Anuraga-valli” has no such line. The word *bada* may also be understood as the “Large”
    vandana: in the view of scholars, Manohara Dasa means precisely the large vandana with the “four sampradayas,”
    known only in manuscript. The biographies make no distinction between the short and the large vandana.
[^6]: This text is not in the short vandana published in 1910, though the last line with Devakinandana's signature is.

[^7]: This miracle of Sundarananda is known only from the vandanas; the “Chaitanya-charitamrita” and the

[^8]: According to a late tradition, told in the preface to one manuscript of the vandana, the author in his youth
    reviled the Vaishnavas, taking them for ordinary men, and fell ill; when the Lord was returning from Kanai-Natshala
    to Shantipura, he prayed to Him from afar with a straw between his teeth, and the Lord told him to fall at the feet
    of Srivasa. Srivasa forgave him and gave him two instructions: to take shelter of Purushottama and, since the
    calamity had come from reviling the Vaishnavas, to purify his heart by glorifying the Vaishnavas — and thus, it is
    said, the vandana was born. Jagadbandhu Bhadra (1903) only conjectured that this was the Gopala Chapala of the
    “Chaitanya-charitamrita,” and B. B. Majumdar (1959) considered these “autobiographical” lines a late forgery.
    Neither the “Chaitanya-charitamrita” (Adi 17.37–58), where the brahmana Gopala Chapala receives Srivasa's
    forgiveness in Kuliya, nor the “Chaitanya-bhagavata” (Antya 4.346–385), where a nameless leper who had offended
    Srivasa comes to the Lord in Shantipura, names this man Devakinandana or connects him with the vandana; the
    biographies do not relate this tradition. The lesson of both stories is one: an offence to a Vaishnava is removed
    only by the forgiveness of the offended Vaishnava himself, and even the Lord does not forgive over his head — “You
    have offended Srivasa Pandita. Go to him, and if he shows you his grace, you will be freed from this sin, if you
    never act in such a way again” (CC, Adi 17.57–58). And the offence of which the tradition speaks — seeing the Vaishnavas as ordinary men — is the very
    error against which Srila Bhaktisiddhanta Sarasvati tirelessly warned.
