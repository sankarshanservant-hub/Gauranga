# Sripada Madhvacharya
*Madhvacharya · @madhvacharya*

## The Acharya of Tattvavada
Madhvacharya lived more than two centuries before Sri Chaitanya, and yet his name is inseparable from the Gaudiya
sampradaya. As a child he was called Vasudeva; on taking sannyasa he became Purnaprajna Tirtha, and from his guru he
also received the title Anandatirtha — the name under which he most often appears in the scriptures. Srila Jiva
Gosvami calls him “our former acharya, the eldest Vaishnava Anandatirtha.” <!-- F1 -->

Madhva's teaching is called dvaita-vada, the doctrine of difference (bheda-vada), or “pure dvaita” (shuddha-dvaita),
and his followers are known as Tattvavadis. The line he founded — the Madhva sampradaya, also called the Brahma
sampradaya — is one of the four Vaishnava sampradayas of our age, together with the Sri, Rudra and Sanaka sampradayas.
The Gaudiya Vaishnavas, too, descend in the line of Brahma: they call their succession the
“Brahma-Madhva-Gaudiya-Sarasvata sampradaya,” or simply “Madhva-Gaudiya.” <!-- F2 F3 F7 -->

## Birth and Childhood
According to inscriptions at Sri Kurma (Srikurmam) left by his direct disciple Narahari Tirtha, Madhva was born in 1238
and lived seventy-nine years, until 1317; the “Anu-Madhva-charita” confirms these dates. The biographies his disciples
composed soon after his departure name as his birthplace a settlement eight miles southeast of Udupi, in present-day
Karnataka.[^1] In our calendar the day of his appearance is Gaura-dashami of the month of Padmanabha, the festival of
Vijaya-dashami.[^2] <!-- F8 F9 F47 -->

His father's name was Madhyageha Bhatta; the family belonged to the Avalli brahmanas. The clan traced itself back to
Parashurama, the brahmana-warrior and incarnation of God, and this, it is said, is why Madhva was tall, strong and
sturdy of body. <!-- F10 -->

Wondrous tales of his childhood have come down, which one biography relates in the words of Srila Bhaktivedanta Swami.
When his father fell into debt, the boy turned tamarind seeds into coins to pay it off. At five he received the
sacred thread — and at that same age, with a toe of his left foot, he killed the demon Maniman, who lived nearby in
the form of a serpent. Whenever his mother grew anxious for him, he would reach her side in a single leap. His
learning, too, showed itself from his earliest years. <!-- F11 -->

## Sannyasa
At twelve, against his father's wishes, he left home and took sannyasa from Achyutapreksha.[^3] In his ashrama he
studied the commentaries of various acharyas on the Vedanta, beginning with the “Ishta-siddhi” of Vimuktatman. The
deeper he went into the scriptures, the more clearly he saw the weakness of the Advaitic interpretation, and he
resolved to restore the original meaning of the Vedanta, which affirms God as a Person. Soon he began to defeat
Achyutapreksha himself in debate, and his teacher, acknowledging his superiority, made him head of the ashrama and
gave him the name Anandatirtha. <!-- F12 F13 F14 F15 -->

As head of the ashrama Madhva began to teach, and he prevailed over scholars of every school — Buddhists, Jains,
Advaitins and other impersonalists, agnostics, logicians, adherents of ritual religion. His system had already taken
complete shape, but was not yet written down. <!-- F16 -->

## Journey through the South
From Udupi he set out south as far as Kanyakumari, and from there to Rameshvaram, Srirangam and other holy places,
everywhere defeating the scholars of Shankara's school. It is said that at Kanyakumari one of them told him, “Since
you disagree with your teacher, write your own commentary on the Vedanta,” and Madhva gave his word. At Srirangam he
came to know Ramanuja's vishishtadvaita, but it did not satisfy him: in his view it refuted Shankara's speculation
too weakly. So his resolve to write his own commentary grew firm. <!-- F17 F18 F19 -->

## With Vyasa at Badari
Before undertaking the work he wished to receive the blessing of Vedavyasa, who, it was said, is deathless and lives
in his ashrama at Badari, unseen by mortals. Madhva walked to the Ananta Math at Badrinatha and spent seven weeks
there in fasting, prayer and meditation. Then, following an inner prompting, he climbed higher, to Badarikashrama
(Upper Badari), the abode of Vyasadeva. There he met Vyasa and set before him his commentary on the
“Bhagavad-gita”; Vyasa approved it and gave him the shalagrama-shila “Ashtamurti.” <!-- F20 F21 -->

His conversations with Vyasa deepened still further his understanding of the scriptures. He stayed several months at
Badarikashrama until he had completed the commentary on the “Gita,” and on returning to the Ananta Math he dictated it
to his companion Satya Tirtha. At the same time he also wrote his commentary on the Brahma-sutras. <!-- F22 -->

## The Return
The way back led through Bihar, Bengal, Orissa and Andhra, and everywhere Madhva defeated scholars of various schools in
debate. According to the “Madhva-vijaya,” at Ganjam on the Godavari he met two outstanding scholars, Shobhana Bhatta and
Svami Shastri. Both accepted his teaching and became Padmanabha Tirtha and Narahari Tirtha — the chief acharyas of the
school after Madhva himself. Narahari Tirtha wrote commentaries on Madhva's “Gita-bhashya” and “Karma-nirnaya” and
from 1271 to 1293 was the first minister of Kalinga; Padmanabha Tirtha was the first to comment on many of his
master's principal works — the “Brahma-sutra-bhashya,” the “Anuvyakhyana,” the “Dasha-prakaranas.” <!-- F23 F24 F25 -->

Through Andhra, Maharashtra and Karnataka Madhva returned to Udupi. There he turned his former guru Achyutapreksha from
Shankara's Vedanta to Vaishnavism and received him among his followers: the guru became the disciple. Madhva's fame
grew, his commentaries on the “Gita” and the Vedanta won recognition, and followers flocked to him from all over India.
<!-- F26 F27 -->

## Krishna at Udupi
At Udupi Madhva used to bathe in the sea. One day, as he sat on the shore meditating on Krishna, he saw that a ship
bound for Dvaraka was about to run aground, and with signals he guided it safely to shore. The captain wished to reward
him, and Madhva asked only for a large block of gopi-chandana. When it was brought, it split open, and inside was a
large Deity of Krishna. Madhva received Him as the Lord's mercy and composed beautiful prayers to Sri Krishna. The
Deity was so heavy that thirty men could scarcely have moved Him, but Madhva, who possessed superhuman strength,
carried Him to Udupi himself. Soon Krishna was installed in a temple, where He resides to this day. <!-- F28 F29 F30 -->

Having installed the Deity, Madhva reformed the order of worship and gave his followers strict rules of ritual and
conduct, including a strict fast on Ekadashi. <!-- F31 -->

Two centuries later Sri Chaitanya came to Udupi. The “Charitamrita” relates:

“There too He saw the wondrously beautiful Deity of Dancing Gopala, who had appeared to Madhvacharya in a dream. One day
Madhvacharya found this Deity in a heap of gopi-chandana being carried on a boat. Madhvacharya brought the Deity to
Udupi and duly installed Him in the temple, where his followers worship Him to this day. Sri Chaitanya was
inexpressibly glad to see this Deity of Krishna. In the bliss of prema He sang and danced a great deal.” (CC, Madhya
9.246–249) <!-- F32 Q3 -->

## The Second Pilgrimage to the North
Later Madhva went north again, to Badarikashrama. The “Madhva-vijaya,” written by the son of one of his disciples, says
that in his preaching he was helped by his wit, his fearlessness and his knowledge of many languages, Turkic and
Persian among them. <!-- F33 -->

In those parts by the Ganga, enmity between Hindus and Muslims was such that no one dared to cross the river, and there
were no boats. Madhva and his disciples swam across, heedless of the Muslim guards, and all of them were seized. Brought
before Sultan Jalal ad-Din Khilji, Madhva spoke with him at length in Persian about devotion to God. The sultan
softened and offered him lands and money, but Madhva humbly declined — and the biographer cites this refusal as an
example of true renunciation. <!-- F34 I3 -->

Tradition tells that once he saved his disciple, the sannyasi Satya Tirtha, from a Bengal tiger, beating the beast off
with his bare hands, and that another time he easily fought off robbers. He lived a long life in good health, and
practised wrestling, swimming and mountain climbing. According to the “Madhva-vijaya,” the strongman Kadanjari, who had
the strength of thirty men, could not move even his big toe. Trivikrama Pandita writes that Madhva bore all
thirty-two marks of a great personality, had a deep, melodious voice and was a skilled singer; his recitation of the
verses of the “Bhagavatam” was renowned for its sweetness. <!-- F35 F36 -->

Madhva visited Badrinatha, Delhi, Kurukshetra, Benares and Goa, and afterwards travelled mostly in the south, around
Udupi. After Shankaracharya he was the second great acharya of the Vedanta to tour the whole of India. Disciples came to
him from many lands, and today his followers speak eight languages — Tulu, Kannada, Konkani, Marathi, Telugu, southern
Saurashtri, Bengali and Hindi. <!-- F37 F38 -->

## Writings
Madhva left commentaries on the “Bhagavad-gita” and the Brahma-sutras (the “Purnaprajna-bhashya”) and on the ten
principal Upanishads; ten treatises, the “Dasha-prakaranas”; the “Anuvyakhyana,” which many regard as his chief work; a
digest of the “Mahabharata”; and a commentary on the “Srimad-Bhagavatam,” the “Bhagavata-tatparya.” In his last years
he wrote the “Nyaya-vivarana,” the “Karma-nirnaya,” the “Krishnamrita-maharnava” and other books, and his final work
was a commentary on the “Aitareya Upanishad.” <!-- F39 -->

In his bhashya Madhva cites verses of the “Brahma-tarka” on the eternal non-difference of the whole and its parts, of
the energy and its Possessor, in Janardana. Srila Jiva Gosvami accepted these verses as authoritative, though he had
never seen the “Brahma-tarka” itself. <!-- F40 Q4 -->

## Opponents and Disciples
Madhva's learning and devotion to the Lord made him hateful to the followers of Shankara, the acharyas of the Sringeri
Math, who held themselves to be the only legitimate Vedantists; the biographer plainly calls their conduct tyranny.
They persecuted Madhva's disciples, tried to prove that he had no legitimate succession, and challenged him to debate.
Their champion, the scholar Pundarika Puri, was defeated, and then his accomplice, the sannyasi Padma Tirtha, in
revenge stole a collection of ancient Sanskrit manuscripts from Madhva's library. The books were recovered with the
help of Jayasimha, the raja of Kumbla. <!-- F41 F42 I1 -->

After meeting the raja, Madhva entered into debate with his court pandita, Trivikrama Pandita of Vishnumangala — the
foremost authority on Advaita Vedanta in Kumbla, and a poet. The debate lasted fifteen days in the Kidil temple, and in
the end Trivikrama acknowledged Madhva as his guru and became his disciple; at his bidding he wrote a commentary on the
Vedanta, the “Tattva-pradipa.” <!-- F43 -->

This conversion was a turning point in Madhva's preaching. Trivikrama's brother and seven other scholars took sannyasa
from Madhva and became the first heads of the eight Madhva mathas at Udupi. Trivikrama's son, Narayana Panditacharya,
wrote the “Madhva-vijaya,” the principal biography of Madhva. Sannyasa from him was also received by the learned
preachers Padmanabha Tirtha, Narahari Tirtha, Madhava Tirtha and Akshobhya Tirtha, who carried on his work.
<!-- F44 F45 -->

## Departure
By the end of his life Madhva had accomplished all he had set out to do: his teaching had been preached, his system
worked out, preachers trained, and his books went on to shape theistic thought in India right up to the twentieth
century. In 1317, on the eve of his eightieth year, having finished his commentary on the “Aitareya Upanishad,” he
departed for the eternal Vaikuntha. It was the ninth day of the bright fortnight — in our calendar, Gaura-navami of the
month of Madhava. <!-- F46 I2 -->

## Teaching
Baladeva Vidyabhushana in the “Prameya-ratnavali” sums up Madhva's teaching in nine principles (the verse “śrī-madhvaḥ
prāha viṣṇuṁ paratamam…”): Hari, Krishna, is the Supreme Truth; He is known through the Vedas; the world is real; the
jivas are distinct from the Lord; by nature they are servants of Hari; the jivas differ in their positions — some are
liberated, some conditioned; liberation is attainment of the feet of Vishnu, eternal service to Him; the way to it is
pure bhakti; the sources of true knowledge are perception, inference and the word of scripture. These same principles
were taught by Sri Krishna Chaitanya.[^4] <!-- F48 Q2 -->

Srila Jiva Gosvami says in the “Sarva-samvadini” that Ramanuja and Madhva hold the doctrine of difference, whereas ours
is achintya-bheda-abheda, inconceivable oneness and difference. Examining four kinds of teaching on bhakti, he notes
that for Madhva the jiva is distinct from Ishvara, yet devotion to Ishvara is its very nature. Baladeva Vidyabhushana
explains that the shruti contains texts on both oneness and difference; Shankara relied on the one set, Madhva on those
that support dvaita, and so neither absolute advaita nor dvaita, taken alone, agrees with the shruti as a whole —
whereas Baladeva reconciles them. <!-- F49 F50 -->

Together with Ramanuja, Vishnusvami, Nimbarka and Sri Chaitanya, Madhva fought Shankara's mayavada, the doctrine of
appearance (vivarta-vada); those who seek true good follow Vyasa's teaching of the transformation of the Lord's energy
(shakti-parinama-vada). Srila Bhaktisiddhanta Sarasvati said that only fools consider it a hindrance on the path of
devotion to teach through books, as Rupa, Sanatana, Jiva and Raghunatha Dasa did, or to have many disciples, as Madhva,
Ramanuja and Narottama did. <!-- F51 F52 -->

## Madhva and the Gaudiya Sampradaya
According to the “Gaura-ganoddesha-dipika” (22–24), the line of succession runs from Brahma through Narada to Vyasa and
Shuka; from Vyasa Madhvacharya received the Krishna mantra, and after him came Padmanabha, Narahari, Madhava-dvija,
Akshobhya, Jayatirtha and so on down to Lakshmipati; from Lakshmipati, Madhavendra Puri; from him, Ishvara Puri; and from
Ishvara Puri, Sri Chaitanya. The same line is given in the “Prameya-ratnavali” and by Gopala Guru Gosvami. Baladeva
Vidyabhushana, in five verses of the “Prameya-ratnavali,” derives the Gaudiya sampradaya from Madhva and lists the
acharyas from him to Sri Chaitanya, beginning with his direct disciples Padmanabha and Narahari; the list was evidently
taken from the “Gaura-ganoddesha-dipika,” as well as from Gopala Guru's “Sadhana-paddhati” and the writings of
Dhyanachandra, Chaitanya Dasa and Manohara Dasa. The Madhva acharya Vyasatirtha stands in this line fourteen
generations after Madhva; a Vallabhite tradition even tells that, as head of the “great seat” of the Madhva
sampradaya, he offered that seat to Vallabha. <!-- F4 F5 F6 Q1 -->

An old verse says: “Mantras received outside a sampradaya are fruitless”; in the age of Kali the earth is purified by
the Vaishnavas of four sampradayas — of Sri, Brahma, Rudra and Sanaka. Their first acharyas — Ramanuja, Madhva,
Vishnusvami and Nimbarka — took as their original gurus Lakshmi, Brahma, Rudra and the Sanat-kumaras respectively.
<!-- Q6 -->

Madhavendra Puri accepted the sampradaya of Sri Madhvacharya and Vaishnava sannyasa. Mahaprabhu accepted Madhva's
sampradaya because the eternal form of God, full of being, knowledge and bliss, which Madhva upheld, is the root of
achintya-bheda-abheda. Srila Bhaktivinoda Thakura and Srila Bhaktisiddhanta Sarasvati explain that in Madhva's line,
from Madhva himself down to Lakshmipati, devotion in the mood of conjugal love (shringara-rasa) and evidence of
prema-bhakti had not been manifest — the spirit of that line can be seen from Mahaprabhu's conversation with the
Tattvavadis at Udupi. Madhavendra Puri became the first sprout of the tree of divine love to rise from it. Something
was lacking in the conclusions of the earlier acharyas, and Sri Chaitanya completed them — Madhva's teaching of the
eternal form of God among them — by revealing the perfect doctrine of achintya-bheda-abheda. <!-- F53 I4 I5 -->

Srila Jiva Gosvami in the “Tattva-sandarbha” (4–5) names Madhvacharya among the ancient Vaishnavas who wrote of
Bhagavan, and says that he consulted Madhva, Shridhara Svami and Ramanuja. He knew that the foundation of
achintya-bheda-abheda lies in Madhva's “Bhagavata-tatparya,” and says himself that he followed the “Tatparya”; in the
supplementary commentary on the “Tattva-sandarbha” he examines the teachings of Madhvacharya and Ramanujacharya. Gopala
Bhatta Gosvami compiled the “Sat-kriya-sara-dipika” “out of kindness to the noble ones in the sampradaya of Madhva”; a
manuscript from Sanketa describing the abodes of the four sampradayas opens with obeisance to Madhvacharya as guru.
<!-- F54 Q5 F55 -->

Baladeva Vidyabhushana had earlier studied the teaching of Madhva's followers in Mysore, at a Tattvavadi temple, took
sannyasa in their school and preached Tattvavada until he met Radha-Damodara. Later, at Galta near Jaipur, the
Ramanandis demanded that the Gaudiyas either prove their unity with Madhva by accepting his bhashya and order of
worship, or show a commentary on the Vedanta of their own. Baladeva explained that neither Sri Chaitanya nor the six
Gosvamis had accepted Madhva's bhashya in full as the Gaudiya teaching, yet they had written no commentary of their own
on the sutras; the Gosvamis quoted Madhva just as they quoted Ramanuja, Shridhara and others. The Gaudiya philosophy is
at its base Madhva's system, refined by the teaching of the “Bhagavatam,” but in many things the Gaudiyas go further,
and Baladeva brought out the distinctiveness and independence of the Gaudiya system. <!-- F56 F57 I6 -->

For Srila Bhaktisiddhanta, what matters most in an acharya is his firm grounding in the Absolute (brahma-nishtha), not
merely a formal link through initiation; therefore, he taught, the Madhva-Gaudiya succession in the spirit of Sri Rupa
was never broken by any “dark periods.” <!-- I7 -->

## Madhva and the Gaudiya Math
In 1898 Srila Bhaktivinoda Thakura published the “Bhagavad-gita” with Madhva's commentary in Bengali. Srila
Bhaktisiddhanta studied the schools of Ramanuja and Madhva from books in four South Indian languages brought to him by
Sundareshvar Shrauti, and collected Madhva works — the “Madhva-vijaya,” the “Purnaprajna-bhashya,” Jayatirtha's
“Nyaya-sudha,” Vyasatirtha's “Nyayamrita,” a formidable weapon against mayavada, and others. He visited Udupi more than
once and invited a swami from there to teach Madhva's philosophy at Mayapur. The head of the matha at Udupi and the
head of the Madhva seat at Salimabad received him as a spiritual teacher of the sampradaya. <!-- F58 F59 -->

On 13 October 1921 Srila Bhaktisiddhanta founded the “Madhva-Gaudiya Math” in Dhaka, and in 1924 he spoke there at
length about the life of Madhvacharya, the Madhva sampradaya and the differences between its teaching and the Gaudiya
one, especially Madhva's view of varnashrama-dharma. He planned the temple of the Chaitanya Math so that at its
corners would stand images of the founders of the four sampradayas — Lakshmi, Brahma, Rudra and the Kumaras — and of
their acharyas: Ramanuja, Madhva, Vishnusvami and Nimbarka. Following the example of Shankara, Ramanuja, Madhva and Sri
Chaitanya, he toured all of India preaching. <!-- F60 F61 -->

Srila Bhaktisiddhanta held the appearance day of Madhva in special honour and timed great undertakings to it: on
4 October 1919, the start of preaching in North and East Bengal; on another occasion, 9 October, the start of the
parikrama of Vraja-mandala over eighty-four kroshas; it was also celebrated at Chataka-parvata in Puri, together with
Govardhana-puja. The song “Sri Madhva janma-tithi” is among the songs for the festivals. <!-- F47 -->

## Significance
It is said that Madhva is an incarnation of Vayu. In a list of jagad-gurus whose position is not diminished by outward
appearance, he is named “Vasudeva (Madhvacharya), who appeared as a child.” For Gaudiya Vaishnavas he is the acharya
who restored to the Vedanta God as a Person, the eternal reality of the world and the eternal service of the soul to
the Lord — the founder of the line through which Madhavendra Puri, Ishvara Puri and Mahaprabhu Himself came to us.
