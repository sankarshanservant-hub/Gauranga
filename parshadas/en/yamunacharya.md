# Sripada Yamunacharya
*Yamunacharya (Alavandar) · @yamunacharya*

## Grandson of Nathamuni
Yamunacharya, known in the South as Alavandar, was the chief exponent of Sri Vaishnavism between Nathamuni and Ramanuja
and Ramanuja's direct spiritual forerunner. He was Nathamuni's grandson. Nathamuni's son, Ishvaramuni, died young while
his wife was expecting a child, and that child became Yamunacharya; Nathamuni himself, having lost his son, took
sannyasa. <!-- F1 F2 -->

Yamuna was born about 953 in Madura, the capital of the Pandya kingdom.[^1] His father was no longer living and his
grandfather had entered sannyasa, so the boy grew up with his mother and grandmother. An orphan, he studied brilliantly
and surpassed all his peers. His teacher, Sri Bhashyacharya, loved him for his diligence, and his companions for his
gentle nature and beauty. <!-- F3 F4 -->

## The Victor
In those years the king's court pandita, nicknamed Vidvajjana-kolahala, “he who throws the learned into confusion,”
exacted a yearly tribute from the panditas of the land, and those who did not pay he defeated in debate, shamed and
punished.[^2] Once his disciple came to collect the tribute at Bhashyacharya's ashrama, but did not find the teacher
there. Twelve-year-old Yamuna, regarding such a demand as an insult to his guru, refused to pay and sent word that “an
insignificant follower of Bhashyacharya” challenged the all-conquering pandita to open debate. <!-- F5 F6 -->

By royal order a day was fixed, and the boy was carried to court in a rich palanquin. The queen, charmed by his beauty,
took his side, the king that of his pandita, and they made a wager: if the pandita won, the queen would submit to the
king in everything; if the boy won, he would receive half the kingdom. <!-- F7 -->

The pandita began with fine points of grammar, and Yamuna answered flawlessly. Then Yamuna put forward three statements
and invited him to refute them: “Your mother is not barren,” “The king is righteous,” “The queen is chaste.” The pandita
was silent, and then accused the boy of treason and blasphemy. So Yamuna refuted them himself, from the “Manu-smriti”
with the commentary of Medhatithi. “To have one son is as good as to have none,” people say (9.61), and the pandita's
mother had borne only one son. A king who protects his subjects receives a sixth part of their piety, and one who does
not protect them a sixth part of their impiety (8.304); and in the age of Kali people are impious. Finally, in the body
of the king dwell the gods — Agni, Vayu, Surya, Chandra, Yama, Varuna, Kuvera and Indra — so that the queen is married to
more than one. <!-- F8 F9 Q1 -->

The queen embraced the boy and called him “Alabandaru,” “the victor.” The king delivered the defeated pandita into his
power and, keeping the wager, gave him half the kingdom: those lands are still called Alavandara-medu. <!-- F10 -->

## King and Monk
So Yamuna became the “boy-king” Alabandaru. Over the years he became ever more absorbed in affairs of state and nearly
forgot his grandfather's legacy: amid royal luxury and power little time remained for the spiritual. <!-- F11 -->

But Nathamuni, when dying, had charged his closest disciple Nambi to inspire his grandson to leave the kingdom and lead
the Sri Vaishnavas, for no one but he could take Nathamuni's place.[^3] Years later Nambi came to the palace, but the
beggar-monk was not admitted. So he began bringing the royal cook the greens called “tuduvalai,” which were said to
purify the mind and incline it to contemplation; the king came to relish them. One day Nambi did not bring them, and the
king, learning of the mysterious sadhu, had him brought before him. <!-- F12 F13 I2 -->

In private Nambi told the king of his grandfather's passing and of his concern: the sampradaya needed a learned defender,
while the grandson had become a king attached to luxury. After conversations with Nambi on the “Bhagavad-gita,”
Yamunacharya came to the temple of Srirangam, received the mantra from Nambi and, renouncing royal wealth, dedicated
himself to his grandfather's work. <!-- F14 F15 -->

## Acharya of Srirangam
Giving himself to austerity, contemplation and devotion, Yamunacharya soon became the intellectual and spiritual leader
of the Sri Vaishnavas, renowned for the depth of his realization and his learning. He united Nathamuni's teaching with
Vedic worship according to the Pancharatra. His unquestioned brahminhood helped establish the Pancharatrika Vedanta
against the protests of Shankara's followers; thus he raised the prestige of Vaishnavism and of devotion to Krishna,
showing their basis in scripture and their superiority to worldly caste pride. <!-- F16 F17 -->

He left the prayers to Vishnu called the “Stotra-ratna” (“Jewel of Prayers”), the “Gita-sangraha,” a view of the
“Bhagavad-gita,” the “Agama-pramanya,” which unites the Pancharatra with Vaishnava Vedanta, the “Siddhi-traya,” of which
only fragments survive, and the “Atma-siddhi.” He wrote mostly in Sanskrit, departing from the Tamil tradition of the
Alvars, in order to cast the southern tradition in a classical form intelligible to Vedic scholars; thereby he prepared
the ground for the recognition of Sri Vaishnavism as an orthodox sampradaya. Later, at Srirangam, Ramanuja was taught
from the “Gitartha-sangraha” and the “Siddhi-traya.” <!-- F18 F19 -->

Alavandar lived at the temple of Srirangam and directed the service of Ranganatha — a service later headed by Ramanuja and
Sudarshanacharya. He had many disciples, and history has kept the names of twenty; all were sincere and devoted, but
none had the learning and strength to carry on his work. His favourite disciple was Periya Tirumalai Nambi (Sri Shaila
Purna), who took sannyasa under his guidance and served him to the end — Ramanuja's maternal uncle. Kanchipurna, who was
not a brahmana by birth, and Mahapurna were also disciples of Yamunacharya. <!-- F20 F21 F22 -->

## Yamunacharya and Ramanuja
The story of how Yamunacharya and Ramanuja met — and did not meet — is told in detail in the article on Ramanuja; here,
in brief. It is said that Yamunacharya came to Kanchi to see the young Ramanuja, but, seeing him a pupil of Yadava
Prakasha, did not approach him, and only prayed from afar that he would become the establisher of the teaching
(darshana-pravartaka) of the Sri sampradaya. <!-- F23 -->

Very old, ill and near death, Yamunacharya learned that Ramanuja had left Yadava Prakasha and was serving Kanchipurna,
and sent his disciples to fetch him. Ramanuja hastened to Srirangam, but was too late: Yamunacharya had departed for
Vaikuntha, to the eternal service of Vishnu.[^4] Three fingers of his right hand were clenched — three unfulfilled
wishes. When Ramanuja made three vows — to open to the people the path of surrender to Vishnu through the
pancha-samskara, to comment on the hymns of the Alvars, and to write a commentary on the Vedanta-sutras — the fingers
opened one after another, and the teacher's face shone with peace. <!-- F24 F25 -->

After his departure his disciples at Srirangam called Ramanuja to lead them, and when Ramanuja faced his debate with
Yajnamurti, the Deity Varada told him in a dream to follow the arguments of Yamunacharya. The general shape of Sri
Vaishnavism and many of its details had been outlined by Yamunacharya; it remained for Ramanuja to complete and
establish what had been begun. So it was Ramanuja who fulfilled Yamunacharya's hopes. <!-- F26 F27 I1 -->

In 1898 Srila Bhaktisiddhanta Sarasvati wrote in the “Sajjana-toshani” about Sri Nathamuni, Yamunacharya and other
acharyas of the Sri sampradaya. <!-- F28 -->
