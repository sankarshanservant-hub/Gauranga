# Raghupati Upadhyaya
*Рагхупати Упадхьяя · @raghupati-upadhyaya*

## A Pandita from Tirhut
Raghupati Upadhyaya was a Vaishnava scholar and poet who, as far as we know, met Sri Chaitanya only once — but in such
a way that this meeting in the house of Vallabha Bhatta entered the “Chaitanya-charitamrita” for ever. Krishnadasa
Kaviraja calls him Raghupati Upadhyaya, and more often simply Upadhyaya or Raghupati; for Govinda-deva in the
“Gaura-krishnodaya” he is “Upadhyaya Raghupati,” and in Srila Rupa Gosvami's “Padyavali” his verses are signed
“Raghupati-upadhyaya.” The biographies call him Sri Raghupati Upadhyaya. <!-- F1 -->

He came from Tirhut (Trihut), also called Mithila — a region in the north of present-day Bihar; according to the
biographies, from the subdivision of Sitamarhi in the district of Darbhanga. The “Charitamrita” introduces him in just
this way — “a pandita from Tiruhita.” Srila Bhaktivinoda Thakura in the “Amrita-pravaha-bhashya” explains that Tirhut is
the land of Mithila, and Srila Bhaktisiddhanta Sarasvati in the “Anubhashya” that four districts now make up Tirhut:
Saran, Champaran, Muzaffarpur and Darbhanga.[^1] From the same region came Paramananda Puri as well, the Lord's close
companion in Nilachala. <!-- F2 F3 F5 -->

By birth Raghupati was a brahmana. The biographies remember him as a famous scholar, a great Vaishnava, a
mahabhagavata and a noble man; Kaviraja calls him a pandita, a great Vaishnava and a great soul. Nothing more is known of
his life in this world: the sources name neither his parents nor his family, nor the year and day of his appearance, nor
when and where he left this world. Nor are his days found in the Vaishnava calendar of the Sri Chaitanya Saraswat Math.
<!-- F4 F6 F7 -->

## A Disciple of Madhavendra Puri
The biographies, relying on Srila Bhaktisiddhanta Sarasvati's “Gaudiya-bhashya” on the “Chaitanya-bhagavata” (Adi
9.154), count Raghupati among the disciples of Sri Madhavendra Puri. In the lists of Madhavendra's disciples he stands
beside Pundarika Vidyanidhi, the Sanodiya brahmana and Keshava Bharati. Neither the “Chaitanya-bhagavata” itself nor the
“Charitamrita” says this; the information goes back to Srila Bhaktisiddhanta.[^2] <!-- F8 Q6 -->

## In the House of Vallabha Bhatta
Returning from Vrindavana, Sri Chaitanya stayed at Prayaga. There Sri Rupa and his brother Anupama came to Him, and
then the scholar Vallabha Bhatta, who lived in the village of Adail across the Yamuna, opposite Prayaga, invited the Lord
to his home and brought Him there by boat.[^3] Already in the boat the Lord held back the feelings that had seized Him.
Srila Bhaktivinoda Thakura explains why: that place was almost devoid of prema, and Vallabha Bhatta was much given to
argument (“Amrita-pravaha-bhashya” on CC, Madhya 19.83). Where and before whom to reveal His love, the Lord Himself
decides; His restraint is not weakness but His will. <!-- F14 F25 -->

In Adail Vallabha washed the Lord's feet and fed Him, and when the Lord lay down to rest, he himself began to massage
His feet. It was then, in the presence of Rupa and Anupama, that Raghupati arrived:

“At that time Raghupati Upadhyaya arrived, a pandita from Tirhut, a great Vaishnava and a noble soul. He came and bowed
at the Lord's feet, and the Lord said to him: ‘May your mind rest in Krishna.’ Hearing this, Upadhyaya was delighted at
heart. The Lord said to him: ‘Describe Krishna to Me.’” (CC, Madhya 19.92–94) <!-- F14 F16 Q5 -->

Govinda-deva in the “Gaura-krishnodaya” tells of these days differently:

“Him, like the hot-rayed [sun], staying there awaiting the arrival of Rupa, Vallabha Bhatta and Upadhyaya Raghupati
invited daily to their homes and served.” (“Gaura-krishnodaya” 14.63)[^4] <!-- F15 -->

## Two Verses
The Lord wished to hear about Krishna, and Raghupati gladly did as He asked:

“He recited a verse of his own about Krishna's pastimes, and hearing it, Mahaprabhu was overwhelmed with great love.

‘Let those who fear worldly existence worship — some the shruti, others the smriti, still others the Mahabharata. But
here I bow to Nanda, in whose courtyard the Supreme Brahman plays’ (Padyavali, 126).” (CC, Madhya 19.95–96)
<!-- F17 Q1 -->

Srila Bhaktisiddhanta in the “Anubhashya” shows whom the poet leaves behind: the “some” who worship the shruti are not
servants of Hari but seekers of liberation; the “others” who hold to the smriti are karmis, hungry for the fruits of
their rites; and the “Mahabharata” is gladly read by all the people of this world. What drives them all is fear of the
round of birth and death. And the Supreme Brahman who plays in Nanda's courtyard is Sri Krishna Himself. That is why the
poet bows not to the Vedas, nor to the law-books, nor to the epic, but to the Lord of Vraja in whose courtyard the
Absolute Truth plays: the Highest Truth is not the impersonal Brahman but the son of Nanda. <!-- Q1 -->

The Lord, filled with prema, wished to hear more, and Raghupati recited a second verse:

“When the Lord said, ‘Speak on,’ Upadhyaya continued, and Raghupati Upadhyaya bowed down before Him.

‘To whom can I tell it, and who would believe it now, that in the groves of the daughter of the Sun, the Yamuna, Brahman
is the lover of the young cowherd girls?’ (Padyavali, 98).” (CC, Madhya 19.97–98) <!-- F18 Q2 -->

The daughter of the Sun is Kalindi, the Yamuna. The word “lover” (*vita*; in the commentaries of Bhaktivinoda and
Bhaktisiddhanta, *lampata*) belongs to the language of rasa-shastra, where it names the hero-lover. What is meant is not
worldly passion but the transcendental prema of the gopis, the highest of rasas — and that is why the poet exclaims: who
would believe that the Supreme Brahman, Whom men seek in the Vedas, has given Himself to the young cowherd girls of
Vraja?[^5] <!-- Q2 -->

The Lord asked him to go on, and Raghupati recited verses on His pastimes. Then Upadhyaya saw what he had not expected:

“The Lord said, ‘Go on,’ and he recited verses on Krishna's pastimes. In the ecstasy of love the Lord's body and mind
were thrown into turmoil. Seeing such love, Upadhyaya was amazed. ‘He is not a human being,’ he concluded. ‘He is
Krishna.’” (CC, Madhya 19.99–100) <!-- F19 Q3 -->

Such love, Raghupati became firmly convinced, is never found in a human being. By its signs he recognised in Mahaprabhu
Vrajendra-nandana Himself, the son of the king of Vraja — the very One of Whom he had just been reciting his verses.
<!-- F19 I2 -->

## A Conversation about the Highest
Then the Lord, like a disciple before a teacher, began to question Raghupati, and between them there arose an
*ishtagoshthi* — an intimate talk about the degrees of excellence within the highest:

“The Lord said: ‘Upadhyaya, whom do you consider the best?’ ‘*Shyamam eva param rupam* — the dark form is the supreme
form,’ said Upadhyaya. ‘Which abode of that dark form do you consider the best?’ ‘*Puri madhu-puri vara* — Madhupuri is
the best of cities,’ said Upadhyaya. ‘Of childhood, boyhood and youth, which do you consider the best?’ ‘*Vayah
kaishorakam dhyeyam* — youth is the age to meditate on,’ said Upadhyaya. ‘Among all the rasas, which do you consider the
best?’ ‘*Adya eva paro rasah* — the original rasa is the highest,’ said Upadhyaya. The Lord said: ‘You have taught Me an
excellent truth.’ Saying this, He recited the verse in a voice choked with emotion:

‘The dark form is the supreme form; Madhupuri is the best of cities; youth (kaishora) is the age to meditate on; and the
original rasa, shringara, is the highest rasa’ (Padyavali, 82).” (CC, Madhya 19.101–106)[^6] <!-- F20 F21 F12 Q4 -->

The four questions lead ever higher. Of the countless forms of God — Krishna, Narayana, Rama, Nrisimha — the best is the
form of Shyama; of His abodes the best is Madhupuri, Mathura; of the ages — childhood (*balya*), boyhood (*pauganda*) and
youth (*kaishora*) — the best is kaishora; of the rasas, the original (*adya*). This last answer is the highest object
of worship. Srila Bhaktisiddhanta calls this original rasa transcendental shringara, the highest of the rasas: it is the
sweet love of the gopis for Krishna, madhurya-rasa, and that love is parakiya, not “conjugal.”[^7] <!-- F20 -->

The Lord's words “you have taught Me” are the play of One Who has taken the form of His own devotee: He asks about
Himself to taste the sweetness of what He hears and to teach the world through His devotee's lips — just as He once
questioned Ramananda Raya. It is not that there was something He did not know. Srila Bhaktivinoda Thakura, summing up
the chapter, says briefly: when Raghupati came, he and Mahaprabhu had many talks about rasa. <!-- F21 F24 I1 -->

## In the Lord's Embrace
“In His rapture the Lord embraced him, and Upadhyaya, intoxicated with love, began to dance. Seeing this, Vallabha
Bhatta was amazed.” (CC, Madhya 19.107–108)[^8] <!-- F22 F23 -->

So the learned pandita from Mithila, who had come to the Lord with respectful prayers, left Him dancing.

## His Verses in the “Padyavali”
Raghupati composed many verses full of bhakti-rasa, and Srila Rupa Gosvami, who had heard him himself in Adail, later
included several of them in his anthology, the “Padyavali.” Srila Bhaktivinoda Thakura too notes that the “Padyavali”
contains his verses. In the editions of the “Charitamrita” the verse “Shrutim apare…” is cited as “Padyavali” 126,
“Kam prati…” as 98, and “Shyamam eva…” as 82.[^9] <!-- F9 F10 -->

In the Bengali edition of the “Padyavali” of 1911 his name stands under other verses as well. One is a prayer: “Here our
Lord called the calves, here He played the flute” — so, with tears, may my days pass on the bank of the Yamuna (no. 88).
Another says that the shruti are like empty straw: the Supreme Brahman was long ago stolen away by the eyes of the
cowherd girls (no. 98). A third, that the fruit of others' austerities has fallen to the young cowherd girls (no. 302).
<!-- F11 -->

The biographies also cite Raghupati's verses heard in Adail among the arguments about when the “Padyavali” was
compiled: since Rupa included in it what he had heard at the meeting with the Lord in Prayaga, the anthology took shape
after that meeting. <!-- F13 -->

## Significance
We know little of Raghupati Upadhyaya, but that little is a summit. In Vallabha's house, in the presence of Rupa
Gosvami, the Lord by His questions drew from His devotee's lips the whole essence of the object of worship: Shyama,
Mathura, kaishora and the original rasa of the gopis. And the poet himself, seeing the Lord's prema, understood to Whom
he was reciting his verses. <!-- I1 I2 -->

    Puri” (CB, Adi 9.154); the “Charitamrita,” in its account of Raghupati (Madhya 19), is silent about his discipleship.

[^3]: One of the biographies places the meeting “in Prayaga-dhama, in the house of Vallabhacharya.” According to the

[^4]: In the “Charitamrita” the order of events is different: Rupa came to the Lord before Vallabha invited Him to Adail,

[^5]: In one of the biographies the word *vita* in the translation of this verse is rendered by an expression with a

    of the young cowherd girls.”
[^6]: The sources differ on who composed the verse “Shyamam eva…”. According to the “Charitamrita” (Madhya 19.105), it is

    1911 puts his name under it (there it is no. 83), and one of the biographies also takes it as his composition; but in
    Srila Bhaktisiddhanta's heading to Madhya 19.106 it is “a verse of Madhavendra Puri (‘Padyavali,’ 82).” We do not
    undertake to decide the question.
[^7]: One of the biographies renders the “original rasa” in English by a word that suggests worldly sensuality; this is
    wrong: the shringara of the gopis is transcendental prema. An earlier Russian translation of the “Charitamrita”
    had “the rasa of conjugal love” here, but the love of the gopis is parakiya, not conjugal.
[^8]: The biographies add that all of Vallabha's household were amazed at Raghupati's good fortune as well. In the
    “Charitamrita” (Madhya 19.108) it is Vallabha himself who is amazed — “at all that had happened”; the verse says
    nothing of his household.
[^9]: In the Bengali edition of the “Padyavali” of 1911 (and in one of the biographies) the numbers are one higher:
    “Shrutim apare” is 127, “Kam prati” 99, “Shyamam eva” 83; this is a difference of editions, not an error.
