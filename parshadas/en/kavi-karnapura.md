# Kavi Karnapura
*Кави Карнапура · @kavi-karnapura*

## Shivananda's Youngest Son
Kavi Karnapura — the poet to whom the Gaudiya Vaishnavas owe the drama “Chaitanya-chandrodaya,” the list of the Lord's
associates “Gaura-ganoddesha-dipika” and many other Sanskrit books on Sri Chaitanya — was the youngest son of Shivananda
Sena, the same who year after year led the Bengali devotees to Puri. He has several names, and almost all of them he
received from Mahaprabhu. The name given him at birth at the Lord's word is Paramananda Dasa; so the “Charitamrita” calls him
(Antya 12.45, 49), and so he signs himself: “I, who bear the name Sri Paramananda Dasa” (“Gaura-ganoddesha-dipika” 5),
“the poet Paramananda Dasa, favoured by Srinatha, the son of Shivananda Sena” (prologue of the “Chaitanya-chandrodaya”).
But the Lord jokingly called the boy Puri Dasa (Antya 12.49; 16.65, 73), and the name speaks for itself: “the servant of
Puri,” that is, of Paramananda Puri, the Lord's senior companion. Not for nothing, in Karnapura's drama Mahaprabhu, seeing
Shivananda's youngest son, says to Paramananda Puri: “Svami, this is your servant.” In the list of the branches of the
Chaitanya tree Krishnadasa Kaviraja calls him Karnapura (Adi 10.62), and elsewhere the poet Kavi Karnapura (Madhya
24.342; Antya 6.262). In his great poem on Sri Chaitanya the author speaks of himself simply as the “youngest child” of
Shivananda Sena (20.46). The biographies also call him Srila Kavi Karnapura Gosvami and Paramananda Sena.[^1] <!-- F1 -->

His father, Shivananda Sena, was one of Mahaprabhu's great devotees, and the son recalls him more than once in his books.
The “Gaura-ganoddesha-dipika” opens with a bow to the guru, and next comes a bow to the father:

“With supreme devotion I worship my father, Sri Shivananda, the lamp of the Sena family, foremost among the associates of
Mahaprabhu.” (“Gaura-ganoddesha-dipika” 4)

In the same book he quotes his father's words — a verse on Nityananda that he would recite as he danced (GGD 63) — and his
father's opinion about Saranga Thakura: some consider Saranga to be Prahlada, “but my father does not accept this” (GGD
172). <!-- F2 -->

Shivananda had three sons: Chaitanya Dasa, Ramadasa and the youngest, Paramananda, the future Kavi Karnapura. The
“Charitamrita” says of all three:

“Chaitanya Dasa, Ramadasa and Karnapura, the three sons of Shivananda, were heroic devotees of the Lord.” (CC, Adi 10.62)

“Heroic,” “valiant devotees” — in the Bengali text *bhakta-shura*.[^2] Who his elder brothers are in the eternal
pastimes, Karnapura reveals himself: the two famous parrots of Vrindavana, Daksha and Vichakshana, “have now been born as
my elder brothers, Chaitanya and Ramadasa” (“Gaura-ganoddesha-dipika” 145). <!-- F6 Q1 -->

He was born, according to the “Anubhashya” of Srila Bhaktisiddhanta Sarasvati (on CC, Adi 10.62), in 1448 Shaka, that is,
in 1526/27, in Kanchrapara (Kanchanapalli), near Kumarahatta (Halisahar), where Shivananda's house stood.[^3] The day of
his appearance is not given in the sources, and it is not in the Vaishnava calendar of Sri Chaitanya Saraswat Math. He was
born, as the biographies say, by the blessing of Mahaprabhu Himself. Jagadananda Pandita, whom Shivananda brought up, also
remembered this village: in the “Prema-vivarta” he glorifies Kavi Karnapura, “who lives in my village,” and adds that
Gaura “at seven years of age made him honoured as a great poet” (8.15–16). <!-- F5 F7 -->

## A Name Given Before Birth
Shivananda went to the Lord in Nilachala every year, and on one of these visits Mahaprabhu spoke to him of a son not yet
born. Krishnadasa Kaviraja tells of it when Shivananda, years later, brings his three sons to the Lord (Antya 12.44). The
Lord asked the name of the youngest, and the father answered: “Paramananda Dasa.” And here the earlier conversation is
recalled:

“Earlier, when Shivananda had come to the Lord, Mahaprabhu had said to him, ‘The son who will be born to you this time —
name him Puridasa.’” (CC, Antya 12.46–47)

The boy was then still in his mother's womb, and was born when Shivananda returned home (Antya 12.48).

“By the Lord's order they named him Paramananda Dasa, and the Lord jokingly called him Puridasa.” (CC, Antya 12.49)[^4]
<!-- F8 F9 Q2 -->

When Shivananda first showed the infant to the Lord, Mahaprabhu bestowed on him a mercy that shaped his whole
life:

“When Shivananda presented that child, Mahaprabhu placed His big toe in the boy's mouth.” (CC, Antya 12.50)

This is no jest and no amusement: the “Charitamrita” says “jokingly” only of the nickname, while here it is a blessing.
Later Karnapura himself confesses that his poetic gift came to him “by the mercy shown in His remnants.” Krishnadasa
Kaviraja continues:

“Shivananda's good fortune is an ocean — who can reach its farther shore? The Lord calls his whole family His own.” (CC,
Antya 12.51)

After His meal the Lord ordered that Shivananda's family should receive His prasada every day:

“‘As long as Shivananda's wife and sons are here, they should receive the plate of My remnants.’” (CC, Antya 12.53)

This order was carried out by the Lord's personal servant Govinda.[^5] <!-- F10 Q2 -->

Karnapura's own drama gives the same meeting in its own way. Shrikanta, the boy's cousin, reports to the Lord who has
come: two sons of his uncle — these the Lord has already seen — and the youngest, who has not yet seen His beautiful
feet. And the Lord, turning to Paramananda Puri, says: “Svami, this is your servant” (“Chaitanya-chandrodaya” 10.5).
<!-- F11 -->

## “Say: Krishna!”
Years went by. Once Shivananda came to Puri for the Ratha-yatra with his wife and brought his youngest son, who was then
seven years old:

“Taking his son, he came to the Lord and had the boy bow at His feet. Again and again the Lord said, ‘Say “Krishna”!’ But
the boy stayed silent and would not utter the name of Krishna. Shivananda did all he could to persuade the boy, but still
the boy did not say the name of Krishna.” (CC, Antya 16.66–68)

The Lord wondered:

“The Lord said, ‘I have made the whole world chant the holy name; I have made even trees and stones say the name of
Krishna. Yet I could not make this boy say the name of Krishna!’ Hearing this, Svarupa Gosani said: ‘You have instructed
him in the mantra of Krishna's name. Having received the mantra, he will not disclose it to anyone. He chants it
silently, within, but does not speak it aloud. That, I think, is his intention.’” (CC, Antya 16.69–72) <!-- F12 F13 Q3 -->

So the child's silence turned out to be not obstinacy but faithfulness: the mantra received from the Lord Himself he kept
in his heart. One of the biographies adds that the Lord was pleased to see so small a child know this rule of the
scriptures. Srila Bhaktisiddhanta explains in the “Anubhashya” on this verse: a mantra received from Sri Gurudeva loses
its potency if disclosed to another — as is seen also from the story of Gadadhara Pandita. That is why Puri Dasa did not
utter aloud the mantra Mahaprabhu had given him. <!-- F14 Q4 -->

## The Seven-Year-Old's Verse
The next day the Lord said to him: “Recite, Puri Dasa!” And the boy at once, without any preparation, composed and
recited a verse:

“Glory to Hari! He is all the adornment of the girls of Vrindavana: the blue lotus at their ears, the collyrium of their
eyes, the necklace of great sapphires upon their breast.” (CC, Antya 16.74)

Hari Himself is the whole adornment of the girls of Vraja, “every adornment” (*akhilam mandanam*), as the “Anubhashya”
explains. According to the heading in the edition of the “Charitamrita,” this is the first verse of Karnapura's
“Arya-shataka.”[^6] <!-- F15 Q3 -->

“He was a child of seven, who had not studied, yet he composed such a verse! Everyone was amazed.” (CC, Antya 16.75)

This episode is recalled also when one wishes to show that a great devotee (*mahajana*) can compose verses even as a
child: the proof is the seven-year-old Karnapura and his verse in the *arya* metre. <!-- F16 -->

Krishnadasa Kaviraja concludes the story thus:

“Such is the greatness of Chaitanya Prabhu's mercy; even Brahma and the other gods cannot find its limit.” (CC, Antya
16.76)

How much less an ordinary soul, adds one of the biographies. Not inborn talent nor early learning, but the Lord's mercy,
received in infancy from His foot, made an untaught child a poet. Karnapura himself says so at the end of the drama: “By
the mercy shown in His remnants my ripeness was born in the form of poetry” (“Chaitanya-chandrodaya,” closing verse 1).[^7]
<!-- F17 I1 -->

From that day, by tradition, the name Kavi Karnapura became his: the Lord Himself gave it to the boy on hearing his
verse.[^8] The “Advaita-prakasha” says the same: “By Gaura's mercy all the shastras were revealed to the son of Shivananda
Sena while still a small child. He became famous by the name Kavi Karnapura: the son of Shachi performed a pastime that
amazed the world” (18.126–127). <!-- F18 -->

## The Guru
The mantra of Krishna's name the boy received from Mahaprabhu Himself. And yet, following the established order, he
accepted initiation (*diksha*) from Srinatha Pandita (Srinatha Chakravarti) of Kanchrapara, the family guru of
Shivananda's household. Srinatha was a disciple of Advaita Acharya and a sub-branch of Advaita on the Chaitanya tree; the
“Prema-vilasa” says that he studied the “Bhagavata” with Sri Advaita, installed the Deity of Krishna-raya in Kumarahatta,
wrote a commentary on the “Bhagavata,” the “Chaitanya-mata-manjusha,” and that “his pupil is the poet Karnapura, son of
Shivananda” (vilasa 24); so also the “Anubhashya” on CC, Adi 10.62: “a disciple of Srinatha Pandita.” One of the
biographies reports that Srinatha's Deities, it is said, are still worshipped in Kumarahatta or Kanchrapara. <!-- F19 -->

Initiation “by custom” does not make the guru a mere formality, nor does it in the least diminish the mantra given by
the Lord: so the order of succession is preserved, and Karnapura honours Srinatha as his guru. In the first verse after
the invocation of the “Gaura-ganoddesha-dipika” he bows before him:

“We bow to our guru named Srinatha, a moon in the line of the brahmanas, a jewelled ornament of the earth as it were, dear
to the Lord: having tasted the pure, unalloyed talks of the secret pastimes of Vrindavana that pour from his lips, no one
in the world delights in anything else.” (“Gaura-ganoddesha-dipika” 3)

At the end of the same book he explains why he has not named Srinatha's former name in Krishna's pastimes: the shastra
forbids uttering the name of one's guru (GGD 210) — and he adds that Srinatha expounded the “Bhagavata-samhita” with skill
and that his glory is Krishnadeva, who shines in Kumarahatta (GGD 211). In the prologue of the drama, too, the author is
called a poet “favoured by Srinatha.” In the introduction to the “Ananda-vrindavana-champu” Karnapura likewise bows to his
guru, and in another introductory verse glorifies Mahaprabhu as the family Deity who accepted Shivananda's whole family
as His own — just as the “Charitamrita” says (Antya 12.51). <!-- F20 I3 -->

## In Vraja
Of himself Karnapura says nothing in the “Gaura-ganoddesha-dipika,” but he names who his parents were in Vraja:

“The messenger Vira, who once in Vrindavana led all the gopis to Krishna, is now my father; she who in Vraja was
Bindumati is now my mother.” (“Gaura-ganoddesha-dipika” 176)

Vira is a messenger-sakhi (*duti*) who serves the meetings of Radha and Krishna. These are eternal companions of the Lord
who descended together with Him, not souls that were “reborn.” One of the biographies concludes from this that Karnapura
too is Krishna's companion in Vraja, although he kept silent about himself. <!-- F3 I5 Q5 -->

A late tradition (the “Vaishnava-achara-darpana”) calls him Gunachura-sakhi in Vraja and says that he is a branch of the
Chaitanya tree, lived in Kanchrapara, and received from Mahaprabhu the name Puri Dasa and great spiritual power when the
toe of the Lord's foot came into his mouth.[^9] <!-- F4 Q7 -->

## Books
Almost all that is known of Karnapura after his childhood is his books. The biographies name among them the drama
“Sri Chaitanya-chandrodaya-nataka,” the “Gaura-ganoddesha-dipika,” the “Ananda-vrindavana-champu,” the great poem on
Sri Chaitanya, the treatise on poetics “Alankara-kaustubha,” a commentary on the Tenth Canto of the “Bhagavata,” and the
“Arya-shataka”; one of them adds the “Brihad-ganoddesha-dipika,” the “Ahnika-kaumudi,” the “Chaitanya-sahasra-nama” and
the “Keshavashtaka.” Srila Bhaktisiddhanta in the “Anubhashya” (on Adi 10.62) lists the “Ananda-vrindavana-champu,” the
“Alankara-kaustubha,” the great poem “Sri Chaitanya-charita” (with a question mark), the “Chaitanya-chandrodaya,” the
“Gaura-ganoddesha-dipika” and other books.[^10] <!-- F21 -->

When each was written, the books themselves tell. The great poem was completed in 1464 Shaka (1542), in the month of
Shuchi (Ashadha), on a Monday, on the second tithi of the dark fortnight (20.49). Of the drama Karnapura writes in its last
verse: Gaura Hari appeared in 1407 Shaka, and this book in 1494 (1572). The “Gaura-ganoddesha-dipika” is dated 1498 Shaka
(1576; GGD 215); according to the “Anubhashya,” he wrote until that year. The biographies regard the great poem as his
first book.[^11] <!-- F22 -->

### “Chaitanya-charita-mahakavya”
The great poem in twenty cantos is an account of the Lord written, as the author says, from what was seen and heard:

“Some things I saw myself, some I heard. To me, utterly foolish, those for whom His pastimes are life itself told of Him
again and again. Some things stayed in memory here and there. Of these I, insignificant, tell a little, because His mercy
leads me.” (“Chaitanya-charita-mahakavya” 1.10)

As his chief source he names the records of Murari Gupta: “whatever was written … by one who bears the auspicious name
‘Murari’ — having looked at all that, this child has written it down” (20.42); and again and again he bows to Murari, by
whose mercy “the nectar of the deeds of Chaitanya-chandra was drunk with the eyes” (20.43–44). The signature at the end of
the poem is the “youngest child” of Shivananda Sena, “the embodiment of the rasa of love for the supremely merciful
Gaurachandra” (20.46). <!-- F26 -->

### “Chaitanya-chandrodaya”
The drama on Sri Chaitanya conveys His deeds faithfully. As befits a drama, the order of events is shifted here and there,
but in the presentation of Mahaprabhu's teaching there is not the slightest deviation — and for this the Gaudiya
Vaishnavas revere and love this book. It begins with a prologue: at the festival of Jagannatha's procession to the
Gundicha, the sutradhara, at the command of King Prataparudra, stages a drama composed by “the poet Paramananda Dasa … the
son of Shivananda Sena”; the festival is the same, the same Lord of the Blue Mountain, “but all this, alas, without
Mahaprabhu we hold empty” (Act 1, v. 2).[^12] <!-- F23 I4 -->

### “Gaura-ganoddesha-dipika”
This book — “a lamp showing the associates of Gaura” — names who in Krishna's pastimes were those who appeared as
companions and devotees at the descent of Sri Krishna Chaitanya. From the first verses the author wards off any suspicion
of invention, and says himself where his knowledge comes from:

“The famous associates of Sri Chaitanya Mahaprabhu, and those of the great Nityananda and Advaita too, and some of the
former names of the cowherds which the first sages — Svarupa and others — pointed out each in his own book: having
studied these, and having heard others from the lips of saints living in Mathura, in Odra (Orissa) and in Gauda, having
considered them with my own understanding, and being repeatedly asked by some — I, named Sri Paramananda Dasa, carrying
out the command given to me, write them down.” (“Gaura-ganoddesha-dipika” 5)[^13]

It also gives the guru-parampara of the Gaudiyas — from Brahma through Narada and Vyasa to Madhvacharya and onward, down
to Madhavendra Puri (GGD 22); from here, it seems, Baladeva Vidyabhushana too took his list of acharyas from Madhva to
Chaitanya. This book, Karnapura writes, should be kept hidden from disputants and given to the connoisseurs of rasa who
have taken shelter of Gaura's feet (GGD 213); “this ‘Sri Gaura-ganoddesha-dipika’ has been composed by me” (GGD 214).
<!-- F24 -->

Many later accounts of the Lord's associates rely on it as testimony: Svarupa Damodara is Vishakha, Gadadhara Pandita is
Radha, Prabodhananda is Tungavidya, Kashishvara and Govinda are Bhringara and Bhangura, Saranga is Nandimukhi, and many
more. Scenes from his drama are often cited as well — for example, the Lord's seven days in Madhava's house in Kuliya,
Prataparudra's valour, Rupa — and passages from the great poem. <!-- F27 -->

### “Ananda-vrindavana-champu” and Others
The “Ananda-vrindavana-champu” is a large book in which prose alternates with verse, in twenty-two parts. It describes
Krishna's pastimes from Nanda's festival (Krishna's birth) to the rasa-lila and is so captivating that it gives the
highest joy even to connoisseurs of rasa skilled in poetry. Later Srila Vishvanatha Chakravarti wrote a commentary on it,
the “Sukha-vartini,” and on the “Alankara-kaustubha” the “Subodhini”; on the “Alankara-kaustubha” there is also the
commentary “Didhiti-prakashika” by Vrindavana (Tarkalankara), a disciple of Krishnadeva Sarvabhauma. <!-- F25 F30 -->

Karnapura's verses also entered the “Padyavali” of Srila Rupa Gosvami; from this it is inferred that Rupa compiled the
“Padyavali” after meeting Mahaprabhu. <!-- F29 -->

## His Verses in the “Charitamrita”
Krishnadasa Kaviraja more than once quotes Karnapura's verses. Having told of the Lord's meeting with Rupa in Prayaga, he
writes:

“Kavi-karnapura, the son of Shivananda Sena, has written at length in his book about the Lord's meeting with Rupa.” (CC,
Madhya 19.118)

and quotes three verses from the ninth act of the drama (Madhya 19.119–121). Of the Lord's mercy to Sanatana, too, he
speaks in the words of the “Chaitanya-chandrodaya” (Madhya 24.342–345); and Narahari Chakravarti in the
“Bhakti-ratnakara” repeats: “Kavi Karnapura, dear to the Lord, wrote a book and told of the grace shown to Sanatana,” and
quotes the same verses (1.657–660). And of Raghunatha Dasa Krishnadasa Kaviraja says:

“In this connection Sri Kavi-karnapura wrote of Raghunatha's glory at length in his book.” (CC, Antya 6.262)

In the drama this glorification of Raghunatha is spoken by the poet's father, Shivananda:

“Beloved by the hearts of all, he is a wondrous land of good fortune that yields its harvest without ploughing: the very
moment the seed was sown in it, the incomparable tree of His love bore fruit.” (CC, Antya 6.264) <!-- F28 Q6 -->

## Last Years
Of Karnapura's later years we know only what he himself said in his books. In the closing verses of the drama he writes:

“As far as I could, by my own understanding, as I saw and as I heard, I, a child, by His mercy composed this account of
Sri Chaitanya. Now that the circle of His beloved ones — O Shiva, Shiva! — remains only in memory, who will understand it,
who will listen? So let Krishna Himself be pleased with it.” (“Chaitanya-chandrodaya,” closing verse 2)

And then follows a bitter verse of separation: “We saw the bhagavatas, received their mercy and lived among them”; while
they were with us we lived — but if without them such as we cannot live anyway, why did we not depart at once? (closing
verse 3). So he wrote when most of the Lord's associates had already withdrawn from sight. <!-- F32 -->

Later books have preserved two more testimonies. According to the “Advaita-prakasha,” when Advaita Acharya before His
disappearance called the devotees to Shantipura, there came also “the best of panditas, Kavi Karnapura” (22.106);
according to the “Prema-vilasa,” he was among the branches of Mahaprabhu gathered for the memorial festival in Katwa
(19.142). <!-- F33 -->

When and where Kavi Karnapura departed from this world no source reports; the place of his samadhi is not known either.
The last date we know is 1576, when the “Gaura-ganoddesha-dipika” was completed. His days of appearance and disappearance
are not in the Vaishnava calendar of Sri Chaitanya Saraswat Math. <!-- F34 -->

As late as the eighteenth century Narahari Chakravarti, in the “Namamrita-samudra,” his prayers to the Lord's associates,
after Shivananda, Kashi Mishra and Ramananda Vasu turns to him as well:

“O Karnapura! This is what I ask of you: let me be ceaselessly immersed in the pastimes of Gauranga.”
(“Namamrita-samudra” 36)

He asks for that very thing which Karnapura sang all his life. <!-- F35 -->

## Significance
Kavi Karnapura's books enriched the literature on Sri Chaitanya both in quality and in number. One of the biographies
calls him a saint by nature and a celebrated poet; for his undivided devotion to Sri Chaitanya, his extraordinary holy
life and the wealth of sweet books full of knowledge that he left for those who wish to know Mahaprabhu and His teaching,
his name is remembered with obeisance and reverence.[^14] He is a living witness of what Krishnadasa Kaviraja said: the
mercy of Sri Chaitanya has no limit, and even Brahma cannot find it. The infant into whose mouth the Lord placed the toe
of His foot, the boy who kept the mantra the Lord had given, became the poet who brought down to us His pastimes and the
names of His eternal associates. <!-- F31 I2 -->
