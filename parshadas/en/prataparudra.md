# Maharaja Prataparudra
*Махараджа Пратапарудра · @prataparudra*

## King of Orissa
When Sri Chaitanya, having accepted sannyasa, settled in Nilachala, the throne of Orissa was held by the Gajapati
Prataparudra. Krishnadasa Kaviraja calls him simply “King Prataparudra”; in the biographies he is Sri Prataparudra-deva,
Maharaja Prataparudra, “king of Orissa and Telinga,” “emperor of Utkala.” Kavi Karnapura and Murari Gupta call him
Gajapati, “lord of elephants,” the title of the kings of Orissa. And in the songs of his drama Ramananda Raya, his subject
and minister, extols him as Gajapati Rudra. His capital was Cuttack (CC, Madhya 12.5). <!-- F1 F6 -->

He was an independent sovereign of considerable power, and in his day the lands of Orissa reached south as far as
Rajamahendri, in present-day Andhra Pradesh. His father, Purushottama-deva, ruled until 1497; then Prataparudra ascended
the throne and, according to historical records, held it until about 1540.[^1] Ramananda's drama, Karnapura's
“Chaitanya-chandrodaya” and other books of the time show what kind of man he was: a valiant and formidable commander, yet
humble by nature; a generous, large-hearted Vaishnava and a patron of learning; a guardian of brahminical culture who
especially favoured the Vaishnava faith. <!-- F15 F21 F22 F23 -->

Yet it is not his royal glory that draws the hearts of Vaishnavas to him. The Vaishnava books of that age say that he was
very dear to Mahaprabhu, to Ramananda Raya, to Kashi Mishra and to Sarvabhauma Bhattacharya, and almost every biography
calls him a vessel of Gauranga's boundless mercy. The Lord, a sannyasi, long refused to see him — and yet granted him such
mercy that Gaurasundara came to be called “the Saviour of Prataparudra,” and Vrindavana Dasa, listing the Lord's pastimes
at the start of his book, names among them “the deliverance of Prataparudra” (CB, Adi 1.160). This path, from refusal to
embrace, is the story told here. The sources give neither the year nor the day of his appearance; the day of his departure is not given either, and its
year only tentatively; these days are not in the Vaishnava calendar of the Sri Chaitanya Saraswat Math either. <!-- F24 F25 F84 F85 -->

## The Gajapati Line
The Orissan temple chronicle “Madala-panji” tells how his dynasty began. The last king of the Ganga dynasty,
Kajjalabhanu, had gone on a victorious campaign beyond his kingdom, and meanwhile his minister Sri Kapilendra-deva (also
called Kapileshvara) took the throne; so began the Orissan dynasty of the Gajapatis. Kapilendra's wife was Sri
Parvati-devi, and their son and heir was Sri Purushottama-deva, Prataparudra's father. Prataparudra's mother was Sri
Padmavati-devi, a princess of Kanchi.[^2] <!-- F2 F3 F4 -->

Krishnadasa Kaviraja names Prataparudra as one of the branches of Sri Chaitanya's tree:

“Other branches are King Prataparudra, Krishnananda of Orissa, Paramananda Mahapatra, Shivananda of Orissa, Bhagavan
Acharya, Brahmananda Bharati, Sri Shikhi Mahiti and Murari Mahiti.” (CC, Adi 10.135–136)

The biographies also count him, together with his mother, among the branches of Gadadhara Pandita.[^3] <!-- F5 -->

All his queens and all the princes were devotees of Mahaprabhu. The chief queen (*pattamahishi*) was named Sri Gauri; four
more were Sri Padma, Sri Padmalaya, Sri Ila and Sri Mahila. One of his sons, a son of Gauri, was Prince Purushottama Jana,
“Bada Jana,” as the heir is called in Orissa; the “Bhakti-ratnakara” mentions him as “the son of Maharaja Prataparudra”
who sent the Deities of two Thakuranis to Vrindavana.[^4] The biographies also give lists of the kings who ruled Orissa
after Prataparudra, from Kaluya-Pratapa and Katharuya-Pratapa down to Divyasimha-deva IV.[^5] <!-- F7 F8 F9 F10 -->

## The Father, Kanchi and the Golden Broom
Purushottama-deva had surrendered himself wholly to Jagannatha, and Jagannatha Himself, it is said, came onto the
battlefield to help him. By his strength he regained lost lands and pushed the borders of the kingdom to Rajamahendri.
<!-- F14 F15 -->

A well-known tradition is bound up with his marriage. Purushottama was betrothed to Padmavati, the
daughter of the king of Kanchi. The king of Kanchi travelled to Puri to see the groom for himself — and at the Ratha-yatra saw
him, as the custom was, sweeping the road before Jagannatha's chariot with a golden broom. He himself worshipped Ganesha
and had no such faith in Jagannatha; service to the Lord looked to him like the work of a sweeper, a lowly chandala, and
he refused to give his daughter to such a man. Only the king of Kanchi judged so: for Purushottama and his line the broom
before the chariot was an honour and a service.[^6] <!-- F16 F17 -->

Purushottama marched on Kanchi with a great army, but the first time he did not prevail. Then he surrendered to
Jagannatha, received His promise of help and set out again. According to a tradition given in one of the biographies, in
the village of Anandapur, some twelve miles from Puri, a milkmaid came to the king: two horsemen had bought milk, yoghurt
and buttermilk from her, paid with a ring and told her to collect the money from the king. By the ring the king knew them
to be Jagannatha and Balarama; he rewarded her generously and went on to certain victory. With the help of Jagannatha and
Balarama he conquered, took a jewelled throne from the king of Kanchi and offered it in the service of Jagannatha; from
Kanchi he also brought back the Ganesha who had put obstacles in his way — ever since called Bhanda-Ganesha, “the
deceiver.” Krishnadasa Kaviraja also remembers this campaign:

“The king of Utkala, named Sri Purushottama, made war on that land and conquered it. The victorious king also took that
king's throne, called the Manikya-simhasana, which was set with many jewels. That Purushottama-deva was a great and noble
devotee. <…> He brought the Manikya-simhasana and presented it to Jagannatha, and in Kataka he established the worship of
Gopala.” (CC, Madhya 5.120–124) <!-- F18 F19 -->

Afterwards the king of Kanchi himself brought Padmavati and gave her to Purushottama — at the very moment when, at the
Ratha-yatra, he was again sweeping the road before the chariot. The son of this marriage, Prataparudra, inherited from his
father both the kingdom and the broom: the service that the king of Kanchi had scorned would become for him the door to
Mahaprabhu's mercy. <!-- F20 -->

## Indradyumna
Who he was before, Kavi Karnapura tells us:

“The great king Indradyumna, who once worshipped Jagannatha, has now been born as Prataparudra, equal to Indra.”
(“Gaura-ganoddesha-dipika” 118)[^7] <!-- F11 Q1 -->

The Puranas tell of Indradyumna. In the second half (*parardha*) of Brahma's life, in one of the Satya-yugas, Malava was
ruled by a king of the solar dynasty, Indradyumna, a supreme devotee of Vishnu; his capital was Avantinagara, and Vidyapati, the
priest of his court, was likewise a devotee of Vishnu. Already in the first half of Brahma's life Lord Jagannatha had appeared in
Nilachala as Nilamadhava to deliver fallen souls; He was served by Vishvavasu, chief of the Shabaras. Through Indradyumna,
Vidyapati and Vishvavasu this Nilamadhava revealed Himself to the world as Jagannatha: to bless Indradyumna, the three
*daru-brahmas* — Baladeva, Subhadra and Jagannatha, the Lord Himself in a form of wood — appeared at Banki-mohana.[^8] The
king who once raised a temple for Jagannatha came into the world again to serve Him — and to see Him also in the form of
Sri Chaitanya. <!-- F12 F13 -->

## A Court Full of Vaishnavas
Prataparudra's court was full of Vaishnavas. As head of the court scholars (*sabha-pandita*) he invited Sarvabhauma
Bhattacharya, famed for his learning throughout India. Ramananda Raya was his trusted governor of East and West Godavari,
seated at Vidyanagara, and later a minister. Ramananda's brother Gopinatha Pattanayaka collected revenue for the king in
Malajathya-Dandapata (present-day Medinipur). According to a tradition given in one of the biographies, the king
appointed Madhavi-devi, the sister of Shikhi Mahiti, as scribe (*panjiya*) of the temple chronicle “Madala-panji.”
<!-- F29 F30 F31 -->

The royal priest (*purohita*) was Kashi Mishra — the man in whose house the Lord lived; today the site lies between the
temple of Jagannatha and the sea, and the Lord's room, a small cell on the veranda, is called in Oriya the Gambhira. The
king served Kashi Mishra as his guru.[^9] The “Charitamrita” says:

“Prataparudra kept one rule for as long as he stayed in Sri Purushottama. Every day he would come and massage Mishra's
feet, and hear how the service of Jagannatha was being carried out in all its order and splendour.” (CC, Antya 9.81–82)

He usually came at midday, after his meal. <!-- F32 F33 Q11 -->

Books are also ascribed to the king — the “Sarasvati-vilasa” (a *smriti* digest of which manuscripts survive), the
“Pratapa-martanda,” the “Kautuka-chintamani” and the “Nirnaya-sangraha”; but according to one of the biographies the
first two were written by scholars of his court, Lolla Lakshmidhara and Ramakrishna. Some hold that the
“Bhagavad-bhakti-vilasa” and the “Hari-bhakti-vilasa” were named after the “Sarasvati-vilasa.” The catalogue of Bengali
manuscripts of the Bangiya Sahitya Parishad in Calcutta lists a song signed by Prataparudra, though his authorship is
doubtful: the poet begs Srimati Radharani to make him an ornament of Her body, the bells on Her feet, a chakora bird
before the moons of Her toenails, a bee at Her lotus feet, Her mirror, Her fan — and best of all the dust of Her feet;
and if not, to do with him as She pleases. <!-- F26 F27 -->

At his command Ramananda Raya composed the “Jagannatha-vallabha-nataka,” “the musical drama of Ramananda,” “dear to
Gajapati Prataparudra.” In the prologue the stage-director glorifies the king: at his very name Sekandhara
hides in a cave, the ornament of the land of Kalavarga looks about in tears, the king of Gurjara sees his capital as an
old forest, and the lord of Gauda feels as if aboard a ship in a storm; to the hosts of hostile kings he is the Rudra of
the fire at the world's end. And every song of the drama ends with his name:

“May this noble form of Madhu's Enemy, sung by the poet Ramananda Raya, again and again give birth to joy in the heart of
Gajapati Rudra, lord of men.” (“Jagannatha-vallabha-nataka” 1.22) <!-- F28 Q14 -->

## The Secret Darshan and the Dream
Vrindavana Dasa and Krishnadasa Kaviraja tell differently how the king came to the Lord, and we give both accounts
without merging them.[^10] According to the “Chaitanya-bhagavata,” when the Lord first came to Nilachala the king was not
in Utkala: he had gone to war against Vijayanagara and so did not see the Lord at that time (CB, Antya 3.269–270). Later,
when the Lord had come back to Puri from Gauda, the king, who had been away on a military campaign, returned to Cuttack and
heard: “Sri Gaurasundara has come to Nilachala.” At once he left Cuttack and hurried to Puri to see the Lord.
<!-- F34 F85 -->

But the Lord did not wish to meet him. A sannyasi must not see kings or women or converse with them: whoever seeks a
king's favour wants to meet him for the sake of his own senses. Mahaprabhu, establishing the injunctions of scripture in
the world, did not look upon women and did not seek to meet the king — and none of the devotees ventured to lead the emperor
of Utkala into His presence. This strictness was not disdain for the king but a lesson for all renunciants.[^11] <!-- F35 -->

The king pleaded, “If you are afraid, arrange for me to see Him without His knowing.” Seeing his anguish, the devotees
decided:

“When the Lord dances in kirtana, by divine arrangement He loses external awareness. The king too is a great devotee, and so
he can use this chance to see the Lord without the Lord's knowing.” (CB, Antya 5.145–146)

And so it happened. From a hiding place, without coming near, the king watched Gaurasundara dance — the tears, the
trembling, the perspiration, the falls to the ground, the thunderous cries of “Haribol!” — such as he had never seen
before. When the Lord returned to outer awareness and sat down with the devotees, the king slipped away unnoticed, filled
with bliss. <!-- F36 F37 Q2 -->

Only one thing troubled him. In the Lord's long dance tears flowed from His eyes and saliva from His lips, and His whole
body was covered with dust. Not understanding these manifestations of love for Krishna, the king harboured a doubt, but
told no one. Vrindavana Dasa explains that Jagannatha Himself had appeared in the world in the form of a sannyasi to
relish the pastimes of sankirtana, and the king did not know this secret, for the Lord's *maya* — His internal potency, *yogamaya* — concealed it, and the
Lord Himself revealed it to him.[^12] One of the biographies explains: the king's former offences, born of a lack of devotion
to Krishna, had been washed away at the sight of the Lord's dance; but he leaned too heavily on what the
senses tell, and by his own judgement did not recognise Krishna in Gaurasundara, taking Sri Chaitanya for no more than a great
devotee. Therefore Jagannatha mercifully appeared to him in a dream. This very doubt, says Vrindavana Dasa, became the
cause of mercy. <!-- F38 F41 -->

That night the king saw Jagannatha all covered in dust; tears flowed from His eyes like the Ganga, and nectar ran from His
nose and mouth. The king wanted to touch His feet, but Jagannatha stopped him:

“Your body is anointed with fragrant camphor, musk, perfumed oils, sandal paste and kunkuma. But look at My body: it is
covered with dust and saliva. You should not touch Me. When you went today to see My dancing, you felt disgust at seeing
the dust and saliva on My body.” (CB, Antya 5.172–174)[^13]

“You are a great king and the son of a great king,” Jagannatha went on, “how then can you touch Me?” — and, glancing at His
servant, He smiled. And then the king saw Sri Chaitanya on Jagannatha's throne, covered with dust just as before; He too
smiled and said, “Today you went away feeling aversion to Me — why do you now want to touch Me?” <!-- F39 -->

Waking, the king wept:

“I am a sinner, wicked and an offender. I did not know that Sri Chaitanya is the Supreme Lord. Can an ordinary soul know
Him? Even great personalities like Brahma are bewildered by His potency. O Lord, please forgive my offences. Be merciful to
me and accept me as Your servant.” (CB, Antya 5.182–184)

So the king realised that Sri Chaitanya is Jagannatha Himself and that They are one (CB, Antya 5.185). The Lord cannot be
known by sensory knowledge or by one's own judgement: this is revealed only by His mercy. <!-- F40 I5 -->

Then Vrindavana Dasa describes the meeting. One day the Lord was sitting with His companions in a flower garden; the king
came there alone, without his retinue, fell at His feet and swooned with bliss — in tears, trembling, his hair standing on
end. Seeing on him the marks of devotion to Vishnu, the Lord touched him with His hand: “Rise.” Coming to himself, the king
clasped His feet and prayed at length: “Save me, save me, O ocean of mercy, Sri Krishna Chaitanya, friend of the fallen,
beloved of Lakshmi, Murari who delights in sankirtana, ornament of sannyasa — be merciful to me and never forsake me!”
Pleased, the Lord blessed him: may he have devotion to Krishna; let him do nothing but Krishna's work and ceaselessly
perform Krishna-sankirtana — the Sudarshana disc of Vishnu would protect him. And He said:

“I have come here for the sake of three persons: you, Sarvabhauma and Ramananda Raya.” (CB, Antya 5.202)

The Lord asked the king not to reveal to anyone Who He was — otherwise He would leave this place; He gave him His own
garland and sent him away. Bowing again and again, the king left, accepting His will with all his heart, and from then on
meditated constantly on His feet. “Whoever hears how Prataparudra met the Lord,” Vrindavana Dasa concludes, “obtains the
treasure of prema” (CB, Antya 5.186–208). <!-- F42 Q13 F86 -->

## “Not Even in a Dream”
In Krishnadasa Kaviraja the events run otherwise. The king summoned Sarvabhauma to his palace when the Lord was already
travelling in the south (CC, Madhya 10.3); one of the biographies explains that on returning to Cuttack the king no longer
found the Lord in Puri. The king said:

“I have heard that a great soul has come from Gauda and is staying in your house, and that He is supremely merciful.” (CC,
Madhya 10.5)

Sarvabhauma replied that a meeting was out of the question:

“He is a renounced sannyasi who lives in solitude; not even in a dream would He meet a king.” (CC, Madhya 10.8)

And when asked why he had let the Lord go, he said:

“He is the Lord Himself, fully independent — Sri Krishna in person. He depends on no one.” (CC, Madhya 10.15)[^14]
<!-- F43 Q3 -->

While the Lord was in the south, the king heard much from Sarvabhauma about His glories, and his desire to see Him grew.
Sarvabhauma warned that it would be difficult — a sannyasi of strict vows does not meet kings — but promised to arrange a
meeting by any means once the Lord returned. And the king became firmly convinced that the Lord was Krishna Himself, since
so great a scholar as Sarvabhauma had acknowledged Him. Thus through Sarvabhauma, whom the Lord Himself had converted, the
king gradually came to know Sri Chaitanya and became His great devotee: mercy comes through a devotee. Not for nothing does
one of the biographies call Sarvabhauma an eternal associate of Jagannatha “who won Mahaprabhu's mercy for the king”: his
transformation brought many of the nobility of Orissa to the Lord — and the king himself. <!-- F44 F80 I7 -->

When the Lord returned, Sarvabhauma conveyed the king's request to Him. The Lord covered His ears with His hands and cried,
“Narayana! Narayana!” —

“For Me, a renounced sannyasi, to see a king is as bad as to see a woman: both are like swallowing poison.” (CC, Madhya
11.7)

Sarvabhauma objected that the king was a great devotee and a servant of Jagannatha. But the Lord answered that a king was
still as dangerous to a sannyasi as a venomous snake, and forbade him: “If you speak of this once more, you will not see Me
here any longer” (CC, Madhya 11.9–12). These comparisons concern the royal office and worldly association for a renunciant, not the
Vaishnava king himself: the Lord kept the purity of His sannyasa-lila as a lesson to the world, while inwardly He was
already won by the king's love. <!-- F45 Q4 -->

Learning of the firm refusal, the king said to Sarvabhauma in grief:

“He has descended to deliver the sinful and the fallen; He delivered even Jagai and Madhai. He has descended with this vow:
He will deliver the whole world, leaving out Prataparudra alone. <…> His vow is that He will not let me see Him; my vow is
that without Him I shall give up my life. If I do not obtain the treasure of Mahaprabhu's mercy, what is my kingdom, what is
my body? All of it is useless.” (CC, Madhya 11.45–46, 48–49)

Sarvabhauma found himself between two fires — the Lord's will and the king's anguish. <!-- F46 Q5 -->

## The Intercessors
The first to plead for the king was Ramananda Raya. Prataparudra released him from service with full salary and came
with him from Cuttack to Puri. Ramananda went first of all to the Lord and, telling of the king's Vaishnava qualities and
his love for the Lord, softened His heart. The king, for his part, had told Ramananda that no one is more fortunate than one who worships
Sri Chaitanya's feet; and the Lord said that serving such a mahabhagavata as Ramananda would bring the king good:

“The king has come to love you so much, and for this quality Krishna will accept him as His own. ‘O Partha, those who are
devoted only to Me are not truly My devotees; but those who are devoted to My devotees I consider the best of My
devotees.’” (CC, Madhya 11.27–28) <!-- F47 Q4 -->

Sarvabhauma, for his part, showed the king a way to the Lord's feet. On the day of the Ratha-yatra, after dancing before
the chariot, the Lord would go to rest in a garden and, absorbed in love, would be unaware of the outer world. Then the king
should go there alone, not in royal dress but as a Vaishnava, fall at His feet and recite verses from the five chapters of
the “Bhagavatam” on the rasa dance — and the Lord would embrace him (CC, Madhya 11.50–59).[^15] <!-- F48 -->

Meanwhile the devotees came from Gauda, and the Lord returned from Alalanatha. From the palace roof the king watched the
procession of Vaishnavas — there were nearly two hundred of them — while Gopinatha Acharya, at Sarvabhauma's wish, named
each one for him. The king asked why they went to the Lord before going to Jagannatha; Sarvabhauma answered that such is
the way of love. He ordered lodging and prasada to be provided for them (CC, Madhya 11.79–90). And when the Lord with His
devotees circled the temple with loud kirtana, the king, hearing of it, climbed with his attendants onto the palace roof,
watched the unprecedented kirtana in wonder, and his eagerness to see the Lord grew boundless (CC, Madhya 11.236–237).
<!-- F49 F87 -->

From Cuttack he wrote to Sarvabhauma again and again begging for a meeting, and when the Lord did not consent, asked him to
convey his plea to all the Lord's close companions: if they were kind to him, they would intercede for him at Mahaprabhu's
feet (CC, Madhya 12.4–8). The devotees were moved by such devotion. <!-- F50 -->

And so Nityananda, before all the devotees, spoke to the Lord — not of a meeting, but of the king's feelings:

“Proper or improper, we want to tell You everything: if he cannot meet You, the king wants to become a yogi. He says, ‘I will
put the yogi's rings in my ears and become a beggar. Without Gaurahari, my heart finds no joy in royal pleasures. I shall
gaze my fill on that moonlike face, and I shall lift those lotus feet and hold them to my heart.’” (CC, Madhya 12.19–21)

The Lord's heart softened, but outwardly He answered sternly: it did not befit a sannyasi to meet a king, people would
condemn Him, and Damodara Pandita would shower Him with reproaches. Damodara, however, said:

“You are the independent Lord. You know everything — what should be done and what should not. What tiny soul am I, to give
You rules? You will meet him of Your own accord, and that too I shall see. The king holds You in affection, and You are ruled
by affection. His affection will win him Your touch. Though You are the Lord and supremely independent, by Your very nature
You are subject to love.” (CC, Madhya 12.26–29) <!-- F51 Q6 -->

Then Nityananda proposed: one rejected in love is ready to give up his life — let the Lord give the king at least one of
His outer cloths, and the king would live on that hope. The Lord agreed. Nityananda took the Lord's outer cloth
(*bahirvasa*) from Govinda and sent it to the king through Sarvabhauma. Receiving the Lord's garment, the king was overjoyed
and began to worship it as Mahaprabhu Himself (CC, Madhya 12.30–38); the moment he touched the cloth he felt the symptoms of
prema.[^16] <!-- F52 -->

On another day Ramananda began to plead for the king. The Lord answered that a black spot cannot be hidden on white cloth,
and added:

“It is like a pot full of milk: if a single drop of wine falls into it, no one will touch it. Although Prataparudra has
every good quality, the single name ‘king’ has stained him. Still, if you are so insistent, then bring his son to meet Me.
Scripture says, ‘One's self is born as one's son.’ So when his son meets Me, it will be as if the king himself had met Me.”
(CC, Madhya 12.53–56)

What repelled the Lord was not the king but only the title “king”; and out of friendship for Ramananda He Himself found a
way. <!-- F53 Q6 -->

Ramananda brought the prince — young, dark, with eyes long like lotus petals, in yellow garments and jewels. Whoever saw him
remembered Krishna — and the Lord too remembered Krishna. Overcome with love, He embraced the youth: “Here is a great
devotee, a maha-bhagavata!” At the Lord's touch the prince felt prema — perspiration, trembling, tears, stupor, hair
standing on end — danced and sang through his tears, “Krishna! Krishna!” The Lord told him to come every day. Returning to
the palace, the prince embraced his father, and the king was seized by the same prema, as if he had touched Mahaprabhu
Himself; and from that day the prince was one of the Lord's companions (CC, Madhya 12.57–67). So the Lord showed the king
mercy — for now, through his son. <!-- F54 -->

## The Broom before the Chariot
The Ratha-yatra came. When Jagannatha was being carried from His throne to the chariot, the king stepped forward:

“Then Prataparudra served in person: taking a golden broom, he swept the road. He sprinkled the road with sandalwood water:
he who sat on the royal throne did this menial service. Exalted as he was, the king stooped to this humble service, and
therefore he became a vessel of Jagannatha's mercy. Mahaprabhu was happy to see this service, and through it the king won
His mercy.” (CC, Madhya 13.15–18)

Karnapura too tells of the king's broom in his mahakavya (15.95–96). To the humble the Lord gives more mercy: the high-born,
the learned and the rich are full of pride, but the king, for all his power and virtues, was free of it and gladly took up
the lowliest service. Krishnadasa Kaviraja says:

“Bhagavan shows greater kindness to the lowly; the high-born, the learned and the rich have great pride.” (CC, Antya 4.68)

By this sweeper's service the king became very dear to Jagannatha; and the Lord, seeing his readiness for any menial
service, was inwardly ready to bless him, though outwardly He still kept up His severity. <!-- F55 Q8 Q7 I2 -->

Before the chariot the Lord divided the kirtana into seven groups and danced in all of them at once, so that each thought
He was with it. Only a few saw this secret pastime, and among them was the king. He stood still in amazement, overcome by
prema, and told Kashi Mishra what he had seen; Mishra said, “O king, your fortune is truly boundless.” The king and
Sarvabhauma exchanged silent glances, and no one but they knew this secret (CC, Madhya 13.56–58); Sarvabhauma and Kashi
Mishra both marvelled at the Lord's mercy to the king. Kaviraja adds:

“Seeing the king's menial service, the Lord was pleased at heart, and by that very grace the king was granted a vision of
this secret. He would not meet the king in person, yet He showed him mercy from afar. Who can understand the mysterious
ways of Chaitanyachandra?” (CC, Madhya 13.60–61)

The Lord's mercy is causeless: He alone knows to whom and when to give it, and often He gives it not openly but
indirectly. <!-- F56 I3 -->

Three rings were formed around the dancing Lord. The inner one was led by Nityananda, the second by the devotees headed by
Mukunda and Kashishvara, and the outer one, holding back the press of the crowd, by the king himself with his retinue and
guards (CC, Madhya 13.88–90). With a hand on the shoulder of Harichandana, his minister (*mahapatra*), the king watched
the dance. Srivasa, absorbed in the dance, stood in front of him; Harichandana pushed him aside again and again, until
Srivasa, not understanding who was jostling him, slapped him. When Harichandana flared up in anger, the king stopped him:

“You are fortunate: you have received the touch of his hand! Such fortune was not mine, but your life is now fulfilled.”
(CC, Madhya 13.97)

Even an affront or a blow from a Vaishnava is good fortune: it is not dishonour but true honour. <!-- F57 F58 Q8 -->

Whirling in divine madness (*divyonmada*), the Lord began to fall just where the king stood, and the king reverently caught
Him. Coming to outer awareness and seeing the king, the Lord cried, “Fie, fie! I have been touched by a worldly man!” The
king trembled with fear. But Kaviraja explains:

“Although the Lord had been pleased to see the king serving as a sweeper, and in His heart wished to meet him, still, to
make His own people careful, He outwardly showed a semblance of anger.” (CC, Madhya 13.184–185)

Sarvabhauma reassured the king: the Lord was pleased with him and was only teaching His companions to beware of worldly
association, and the king's desire would be fulfilled in due time (CC, Madhya 13.186–188). The Lord did not regard the king
as worldly: His anger was instruction for His companions. <!-- F59 -->

The Lord Himself, leading the chariot, was in the mood of Radha and the gopis meeting Krishna at Kurukshetra: He longed to
bring Krishna-Jagannatha from the abode of majesty, Nilachala, to the abode of sweet pastimes — Sundarachala, Gundicha. In
His lila supreme love, mercy and instruction to the world are joined, and His deeds are not easy to understand. The Lord's
severity towards the king was only outward: He was teaching renunciants the rules of sannyasa, while inwardly He was won
by the king's pure devotion and lavished causeless mercy on him, teaching the whole world through Prataparudra.
<!-- F60 I4 I1 -->

## In the Garden at Balagandi
Halfway between the temple and Gundicha, at Balagandi (according to one of the biographies, between Shraddha-balu and the
shrine of Ardhasini-devi), the chariot stops at midday, for here food is offered to Jagannatha. To the left of the road lie
a brahmin village and coconut groves, to the right flowering gardens like the groves of Vrindavana. The king too brought
his offerings with his queens and retinue. To get away from the crowd the Lord withdrew into a garden; Vrindavana came to
His mind, and, wearied by the dance, He lay down on a raised platform, unconscious with joy (CC, Madhya 13.193–204).
<!-- F61 -->

Then the king came:

“On Sarvabhauma's advice he had laid aside his royal dress, and he entered alone, dressed as a Vaishnava. With joined palms
he asked permission of all the devotees. Then, mustering his courage, he fell down and clasped the Lord's feet.” (CC,
Madhya 14.5–6)

Massaging the Lord's feet, he began to recite the “Gopi-gita,” the song of the gopis from the “Bhagavatam,” beginning with
the words *jayati te 'dhikam janmana vrajah* (SB 10.31.1). The Lord, inexpressibly happy, kept saying, “Recite more, go
on!” And when the king reached the verse *tava kathamritam…* (SB 10.31.9), the Lord rose and, overcome with prema,
embraced him:

“You have given Me many priceless jewels. I have nothing to give you in return, so I give you My embrace.” (CC, Madhya
14.11)

Again and again the Lord repeated that verse; both of them trembled, and tears streamed from their eyes. “Most munificent!
Most munificent!” He cried, embracing the king: *bhurida*, “most munificent,” is what the verse calls one who brings
people the narrations of Krishna. This was the Lord's prema in the mood of Radha, thirsting for news of Krishna, and there
is nothing worldly in this scene. <!-- F62 F63 F64 Q9 -->

Only then did the Lord, though He knew everything, ask, “Who are you? You have given Me great joy.” The king answered:

“I am a servant of Your servants. Make me a servant of Your servants — that is my one hope.” (CC, Madhya 14.18)

Then Mahaprabhu revealed to him His divine majesty (*aishvarya*) and told him to tell no one; whether He recognised the king,
the Lord never showed. And all the devotees rejoiced that the king had at last received mercy (CC, Madhya 14.19–21).
<!-- F65 -->

That same day, when the chariot moved on towards Gundicha, it suddenly stopped and would not budge. The anxious king hurried
there, ministers and friends with him, and ordered the strongest wrestlers to pull — in vain; he had mighty elephants yoked —
the chariot stood still. Then the Lord came with His companions. He had the elephants unharnessed, gave the ropes to His
own people, and Himself pushed the chariot from behind with His head — and it rolled forward with a roar and in a moment
reached the gates of Gundicha. All marvelled at the Lord's power, and the king, his retinue and ministers were seized with
prema at the sight of Mahaprabhu's greatness (CC, Madhya 14.47–60). <!-- F66 -->

## Nanda-utsava and the Farewell for Vrindavana
On the day of the Nanda-utsava the Lord, dressed as a cowherd boy with pots of milk and yoghurt on His shoulders, enacted
the pastimes of Vraja with the devotees. The king himself was there, together with Sarvabhauma, Kashi Mishra and the
temple superintendent Tulasi Padichha; at his order the superintendent brought cloth that Jagannatha had worn, to tie
around the heads of the celebrants (CC, Madhya 15.17–20, 15.27). One of the biographies says that the king and Sarvabhauma
played the part of cowherd companions in this lila. <!-- F67 -->

For two years the Lord longed to go to Vrindavana, but Ramananda and Sarvabhauma kept Him in Puri (CC, Madhya 16.84–89);
according to one of the biographies, on one pretext or another and at the earnest request of the king, whom the news of
the Lord's departure had saddened (CC, Madhya 16.3). When at last He was firmly resolved, the king himself arranged
everything for His journey. On Vijaya-dashami the Lord came with Ramananda to Cuttack and rested beneath a bakula tree in a
garden. Ramananda informed the king, and he hurried there:

“Delighted at the news, the king came at once, and seeing the Lord, he fell flat on the ground like a rod. Overwhelmed by
love, he rose and fell again and again; he offered prayers, the hair of his body stood on end, and tears flowed from his
eyes. Seeing his devotion, the Lord was pleased at heart. Mahaprabhu rose and embraced him. Again the king offered prayers
and bowed down, and his body was bathed in tears of the Lord's mercy. Ramananda calmed the king and seated him, and the
Lord showed him mercy in body, mind and words. Gauraraya showed him such mercy that He came to be called ‘the Saviour of
Prataparudra.’” (CC, Madhya 16.103–108)

Ever since, one of Gaurasundara's names has been *Prataparudra-santrata*, “the Saviour of Prataparudra.”[^17] <!-- F69 -->

After taking leave of the Lord, the king sent an edict to his governors: in every village on the Lord's route five or seven
new houses were to be built and stocked with all that was needed, and they themselves were to serve Him day and night. He
entrusted its execution to his ministers Harichandana and Mangaraja; at the river he ordered a new boat kept ready and a
memorial pillar set up where the Lord would cross, so that the place would become a tirtha; there, at that crossing, he wished one day to
leave his own body (CC, Madhya 16.110–115). In the evening, as the Lord set out, the king had the queens seated in
covered tents on elephants; lined up along the road, they bowed from afar to the Lord as He bathed in the Chitrotpala and
cried “Krishna! Krishna!” in tears (CC, Madhya 16.117–121). Ramananda, Mangaraja and Harichandana escorted the Lord across
the river Chitrotpala. By the king's order the temple superintendent sent the Lord prasada every day, and in every village
the king's servants gave Him a new house and provisions (CC, Madhya 16.124, 16.152): “on the way Prataparudra served Him in
many ways,” says the “Charitamrita” (Madhya 1.148). And when later the Lord agreed to spend the four months of chaturmasya in
Puri, the king was delighted (CC, Madhya 16.285). <!-- F68 F88 -->

## That the Lord Might Stay
Ramananda's brother Gopinatha Pattanayaka, a son of Bhavananda Raya, owed the treasury two lakhs of *kahanas* of cowries. In
payment he brought the king ten or twelve horses; the king sent the prince, a connoisseur of horses, to value them — he
set a low price, and Gopinatha mocked his habit of throwing back his head. The offended prince — “Bada Jana,” the heir; in
the “Charitamrita,” Prince Purushottama Jana (Antya 9.99) — obtained leave to raise Gopinatha onto the *changa*, the
platform from which the condemned were thrown onto swords.[^18] The devotees came to Mahaprabhu to plead for Gopinatha, but
the Lord, displeased at being troubled with worldly affairs, resolved to go away to Alalanatha. <!-- F70 F71 -->

By the Lord's inner prompting the minister Harichandana Patra told the king everything. The king had not even known of the
execution: “I want the money, not his life” — and he cancelled it at once. And at midday, when as usual he was massaging
Kashi Mishra's feet, Mishra hinted that the Lord meant to leave Puri, because He was being troubled on account of
Bhavananda's family. The king replied:

“I give up all the money, if only the Lord will stay here. If I can have the Lord's darshana for even a moment, not even
millions of *chintamani* gems can equal it. And what are these two lakhs of kahanas? A paltry thing! My life and my
kingdom I offer at the Lord's feet in worship.” (CC, Antya 9.94–96)

He remitted Gopinatha's entire debt, doubled his salary and honoured him with a silk cloth (*neta-dhati*) (CC, Antya
9.80–101, 9.130–139). He forgave him also remembering how close Gopinatha was to Mahaprabhu and to Ramananda. So the
“Charitamrita” says, listing the Lord's pastimes:

“Gopinatha Pattanayaka, Ramananda's brother, was about to be put to death by the king, and the Lord became his saviour.”
(CC, Madhya 1.265)

His readiness to give up everything if only the Lord would stay in Puri is the proof of his love for the Lord's feet.
<!-- F72 Q10 I6 -->

There was another incident. Kamalakanta Vishvasa, a servant and disciple of Advaita Acharya, without his master's
knowledge sent the king a letter (CC, Adi 12.28–29): Advaita, he wrote, was God Himself but was in debt — and he asked for
money. The Lord punished Kamalakanta by not admitting him to His presence for a time. <!-- F73 -->

In other years too, when the Lord with His devotees circled the temple in sankirtana in seven groups, the king and his
retinue watched Him from a distance, and the queens from the upper storey of the palace (CC, Antya 10.58–63). <!-- F87 -->

## Other Accounts
In Murari Gupta the Gajapati, longing to see Mahaprabhu, calls Sarvabhauma and Ramananda to him (Murari
4.16.1).[^19] From there Murari, like Lochana Dasa, tells a different story from Vrindavana Dasa and Krishnadasa Kaviraja.
In Murari the king sees Gaura and Nityananda in the ecstasy of kirtana, and in a dream three times sees Them as Krishna and
Balarama; in Lochana he sees a sannyasi in Jagannatha's place, and later, after a ten-day fast, at the request of
Paramananda Puri and all the devotees, the Lord receives him in Kashi Mishra's house. In both the Lord reveals to the king
His six-armed form; in Lochana He also instructs him in a king's duty — to protect his subjects. The revealed form is the
Lord's revelation to His devotee, not a wonder for its own sake. <!-- F74 F75 -->

The Oriya poet Govinda-deva gives his own account in the “Sri Gaura-krishnodaya.” For him King Prataparudra is an
incarnation of Indra (1.45; in Karnapura he is Indradyumna, “equal to Indra”). The king comes to Puri with his army, and
Sarvabhauma advises him to clasp the Lord's feet at the Gundicha-yatra while singing “Krishna, Krishna” (11.11–14); from the
palace roof the king watches the Bengali devotees arrive, his son is brought to the Lord, and at the Ratha-yatra the king
sweeps the chariots with a broom and sprinkles them with water mixed with camphor and sandal (11.31–51). During the dance
before the chariot he falls at the Lord's feet, and the Lord, in bhava, embraces him, then, coming to outer consciousness,
withdraws as if reproaching Himself (11.58); in the garden the king, in disguise, recites the verses of the gopis in
separation, and the Lord embraces him, saying, “Read on!” (11.61–62). And at Cuttack, as He leaves for Vrindavana, the Lord
reveals to the king His six-armed form composed of Rama and Krishna, and the king falls weeping to the ground until the
Lord, resuming His own form, touches him with His hand (13.3–5). <!-- F89 -->

According to a tradition given in one of the biographies, after the Lord left Puri for Vrindavana the king, unable to bear
the separation, had a Deity of the Lord carved, appointed fifty-four brahmins for His service and granted a large estate
for it. In the palace at Puri, among many Deities, Gaura-Nityananda and Gaura-Gadadhara are worshipped to this day.
<!-- F76 -->

Other Deities too are connected with Prataparudra. His father, Purushottama-deva, brought Deities from Kanchi, among them
Radhakanta; He was installed near the Chatrabhoga of the Jagannatha temple, and later Kashi Mishra, “Prataparudra's guru,”
asked for Him — and in Kashi Mishra's house, where the Lord lived, Sri Sri Radhakanta were installed in the time of Gopala
Guru. And according to the “Sadhana-dipika,” cited in the “Bhakti-ratnakara” (6.92–110), after the passing of Brihadbhanu,
in whose house the Deity of Radharani had lived as his daughter, the king came to Radhanagara and saw Her; in a dream
Radhika told him to take Her to the temple of Jagannatha. She was installed in the Chakrabeda, and people worshipped Her as
Lakshmi. <!-- F77 F78 -->

Baladeva Vidyabhushana ends his “Prameya-ratnavali” with a bow to Sri Chaitanya, “by whose mercy the Gajapati became
faultless and attained peace”; with the same verse he opens the “Siddhanta-darpana” and his treatise on poetics.
<!-- F79 -->

## After the Lord's Disappearance
The “Bhakti-ratnakara” tells what became of the king when the Lord disappeared from sight:

“Then, hearing the news that the Lord had disappeared from sight, the king, flinging himself down, rolled on the ground.
Striking his head with his hands, he fell unconscious; Raya Ramananda alone preserved his life. Unable to bear separation
from the Lord, the king stayed some distance away from Nilachala.” (“Bhakti-ratnakara” 3.217–219) <!-- F81 Q12 -->

When, soon after the Lord's disappearance, Srinivasa came to Nilachala, the king's state of separation was, in Narahari's
words, beyond description, and Srinivasa himself grieved that he had not been able to see the king (“Bhakti-ratnakara”
3.213, 3.220). <!-- F82 -->

Yet even in separation the king went on serving the Lord. Kavi Karnapura opens the “Chaitanya-chandrodaya” with the
stage-director producing the drama at the command of Gajapati Prataparudra during the festival of Jagannatha's procession
to Gundicha: the king had come by right of service, but his heart was wounded by grief at the disappearance of Sri Krishna
Chaitanya, and so even this supreme festival gave him no joy. Karnapura extols his valour and his tranquillity in
*shanta-rasa*. Thus the king became both the commissioner and the patron of the drama that has preserved for us many of the
Lord's pastimes.[^20] <!-- F83 -->

Where Prataparudra left this world the sources do not say, and the primary sources do not give the year; one of the
biographies places his departure in 1540, the year in which his reign ends. The days
of his appearance and departure are not in the calendar of the Sri Chaitanya Saraswat Math either. <!-- F84 F21 -->

Narahari Chakravarti, in the “Namamrita-samudra,” where his prayers call upon the Lord's associates, turns to him as
well:

“O King Prataparudra, this is my plea: destroy the villains — lust, anger and the rest.” (“Namamrita-samudra” 55)
<!-- F90 -->

## Significance
Prataparudra is the one who in a former birth worshipped Jagannatha as King Indradyumna and who became a vessel of
Gauranga's boundless mercy. His story is a lesson about mercy. The Lord, a strict sannyasi, refused to see the king: a
king's office is dangerous for a renunciant, and He taught this to His companions. But His severity was only outward;
inwardly He was won by the king's pure devotion and bestowed on him causeless mercy — first indirectly, through his son,
His cloth and the sight of secret pastimes, and then by His embrace. <!-- F11 F25 I1 I3 -->

The key to this mercy was humility. Birth, learning and wealth breed pride, but the king, who had all three, took up a
sweeper's broom, massaged his guru's feet, called himself a servant of the Lord's servants and counted as good fortune even the slap
a Vaishnava gave his minister. He came to the Lord through devotees — through Sarvabhauma, Ramananda, Nityananda — for mercy
comes through a devotee. His kingship became service: the broom before the chariot, a living fence around the kirtana, the
edict for the Lord's journey, the remission of a debt for the Lord's sake — “my life and my kingdom” he was ready to offer
at His feet. <!-- F65 I2 I7 I6 -->

And the dream of Jagannatha in the dust teaches something else: the Lord cannot be grasped by the senses or by one's own
judgement. Jagannatha and Sri Chaitanya are one, and this is revealed only by Their mercy. That is why in the Lord's lila
love, mercy and instruction to the world are so inseparable — and why Gaurasundara is called the Saviour of Prataparudra.
