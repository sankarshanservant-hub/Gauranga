# Bijli Khan, the Pathan Vaishnava
*Биджли Хан, патхан-вайшнав · @bijli-khan*

## The Pathan Prince
Among those whom Sri Chaitanya blessed on the roads of northern India, a special place belongs to Bijli Khan — a young
Pathan prince whom the “Charitamrita” calls a maha-bhagavata, a great devotee honoured at every holy place. In the text
published by the Gaudiya Math, Krishnadasa Kaviraja calls him Bijali Khan; the biographies call him Sri Bijli Khan, “the
Pathan Bijli Khan,” “the prince Bijli Khan,” “the leader of the Pathans,” and, in the title of the chapter devoted to
him, “the Pathan Vaishnava”[^1]. <!-- F1 -->

By birth he was a Pathan, a Muslim, and he came to be known far and wide as a Vaishnava: Mahaprabhu's mercy does not look at birth. Kaviraja Gosvami writes of him:

“He was young, the son of a king, and Ramadasa and the other Pathans were his servants.” (CC, Madhya 18.208)

So he was young and of royal family[^2]; Pathan horsemen served him, and among them was that grave man in black whom
people called a “pir” and whom the Lord would name “Ramadasa.” One biography remarks that Bijli Khan may with full right
be called a contemporary of Mahaprabhu. Nothing more is known of his worldly life: the sources name neither his
birthplace, nor his parents, nor the year and day of his appearance, nor when and where he departed from this world;
nor are his days found in the Vaishnava calendar of the Sri Chaitanya Saraswat Math. All we know of him is a single
meeting with the Lord, which Kaviraja Gosvami described in the eighteenth chapter of the Madhya-lila, and what he became
after it. <!-- F2 F3 F4 F5 I1 Q2 -->

## On the Way from Vraja
It was the year when Mahaprabhu, after the Ratha-yatra, left Puri for Vrindavana, taking the jungle path across Jharikhanda.
With Him went Balabhadra Bhattacharya and his brahmana servant, whom Ramananda Raya and Svarupa Damodara had given the
Lord as companions. In Vraja the Lord sank ever deeper into love, and once at Akrura-ghata He threw Himself into the
Yamuna. The alarmed Balabhadra took counsel with the Sanodiya brahmana of Mathura, a disciple of Sri Madhavendra Puri,
and they resolved to lead the Lord away from Vraja: the month of Magha had already begun, and, after bathing in the
Ganga at Soro-kshetra — the place on her bank nearest to Mathura — they could follow the Ganga down to Prayaga in time
for the days of Makara-sankranti. The Lord, who always fulfils the desires of His devotees, agreed. With Him went
Balabhadra and his servant, the Sanodiya brahmana and Krishnadasa the Rajput — four companions. The sources do not
name the year of this journey; in our chronology the Lord's journey to Vrindavana and Prayaga falls in 1514–1516.
<!-- F7 F30 -->

On the way the Lord stopped to rest:

“On the way the Lord saw that everyone was tired from walking, and He sat down with them all beneath a tree. Many cows
were grazing near that tree, and the sight of them filled Mahaprabhu's heart with joy. Suddenly a cowherd played on his
flute, and hearing it, Mahaprabhu was overwhelmed with great love. The Lord fell unconscious to the ground; foam came
from His mouth, and the breath in His nostrils stopped.” (CC, Madhya 18.159–162)

The sight of the grazing cows and the sound of the flute reminded the Lord of the pastimes of Vraja, and He sank into
the swoon of love — *prema-murchchha*[^3]. The sources do not name the place: “on the way,” by a pasture; Krishnadasa the
Rajput would later say that his home was in this village. <!-- F8 -->

## The Pathan Horsemen
“Just then ten horsemen arrived there — mleccha Pathans — and dismounted from their horses. Seeing the Lord, the
mlecchas reasoned: ‘This sannyasi had boundless gold…’” (CC, Madhya 18.163–164)

His four companions, they decided, were highwaymen: they had drugged Him with datura, killed Him and taken all His
wealth (CC, Madhya 18.165).

Kaviraja Gosvami does not name the leader of this troop. Srila Bhaktisiddhanta Sarasvati in the “Anubhashya” says it
plainly: “Who Bijali Khan, the leader of the troop of Pathans, was” (heading to CC, Madhya 18.208); so too Srila
Bhaktivinoda Thakura and the biographies understand it: Bijli Khan rode at the head of ten Pathan horsemen[^4]. According
to Bhaktivinoda Thakura and the biographies, it was Bijli Khan himself who voiced this suspicion. The four were Krishnadasa the Rajput, the Sanodiya brahmana,
Balabhadra Bhattacharya and his brahmana companion. <!-- F6 F9 -->

“Then the Pathans bound the four men and wanted to cut them down. The Bengalis all began to tremble. Krsnadasa was a
Rajput and utterly fearless. That brahmana too was fearless, and very bold of speech.” (CC, Madhya 18.166–167) <!-- F10 -->

The brahmana said that he was a brahmana of Mathura and that the unconscious sannyasi was his guru; because of an
illness (so he explained it to strangers) He sometimes faints, but He would soon come to Himself. Let the Pathans sit here a while, keeping them bound, and
when He wakes, ask Him — and if He says they have harmed Him, let them kill him, the brahmana[^5]. The brahmana also
appealed to his connections: “I have a hundred men at the padsah's court” (CC, Madhya 18.169)[^6]. <!-- F11 F12 -->

But the leader judged otherwise:

“The Pathan said, ‘You two are men of the west, from Mathura, and these two Bengali cheats are trembling.’” (CC, Madhya
18.172)

Srila Bhaktisiddhanta explains that in his anger the Pathan calls them all robbers. Then Krishnadasa the Rajput, seeing
the danger, answered sharply:

“Krsnadasa said, ‘My home is in this village. There are two hundred Turks there and a hundred guns. If I give the
signal, they will all come at once, kill you all and carry off your horses and saddles. The Bengalis are not
highwaymen — you are the highwaymen! You rob pilgrims and want to kill them besides.’” (CC, Madhya 18.173–175)

“Turks” (*turuka*), as Srila Bhaktivinoda Thakura explains, are a kind of Muslim soldiery[^7]. Hearing this answer, the
Pathans began to hesitate. <!-- F13 F14 -->

## “Hari! Hari!”
At that very moment the Lord came to Himself.

“He rose with a loud roar, calling ‘Hari! Hari!’, and absorbed in love He danced with His arms raised high. When the
Lord, absorbed in love, cried out, it pierced the mlecchas' hearts like the point of a spear. Frightened, the mlecchas
set the four men free, and the Lord did not see His companions in bonds.” (CC, Madhya 18.177–179)

It was not a mere loud cry that frightened the armed horsemen, but the power of the holy name coming from the lips
of the Lord Himself in the ecstasy of prema: as Srila Bhaktisiddhanta says, the sinful heart cannot bear the sound of
the name of Hari. And that the Lord was not to see His companions in bonds was no accident but His will: He did not
allow enmity to be shown to His devotees before His eyes. <!-- F15 -->

“The Bhattacarya came, took hold of the Lord and seated Him. Seeing the mlecchas, Mahaprabhu returned to outward
consciousness.” (CC, Madhya 18.180)

The Pathans were drawn by His incomparable beauty and His intoxication of love. They bowed at His feet, yet still voiced
their suspicion: these four are robbers; they drugged Him, drove Him out of His mind and robbed Him. The Lord replied:

“They are not cheats; they are My companions. I am a mendicant sannyasi and have no wealth at all. Because of the
falling sickness I sometimes lose consciousness, and these four kindly look after Me.” (CC, Madhya 18.183–184)

So the Lord spoke of Himself before strangers, concealing His prema, as the Sanodiya brahmana too had spoken of His
“illness”; Srila Bhaktisiddhanta notes accordingly that on seeing the Pathans the Lord held His feelings in check. His
unconsciousness is no disease but the highest state of divine love, which He did not reveal to those who
could not understand it. <!-- F16 F17 -->

## The Debate on Scripture
Among the Pathans there was one man whom all held in regard:

“Among those mlecchas was one very grave man. He wore black clothes, and people called him a *pir*.” (CC, Madhya 18.185)

A *pir* is a Muslim holy man, a preceptor; Srila Bhaktisiddhanta calls him a “maulana,” a Muslim divine. This was the
servant of Bijli Khan whom the Lord would soon name Ramadasa[^8]. <!-- F18 -->

“His heart melted when he saw the Lord, and, citing his own scripture, he set out to establish the impersonal
(*nirvisesa*) Brahman.” (CC, Madhya 18.186)

Srila Bhaktisiddhanta explains that the impersonal Brahman is a state in which the two eternal relations, “Khuda” and
“banda,” God and His servant, are absent, and there are no spiritual pastimes. Srila Bhaktivinoda Thakura writes in the
“Amrita-pravaha-bhashya” that the scripture the pir cited is the Koran, and that this impersonal reading is held by the
Sufis, whose saying is “ana-l-haqq” (“I am the Truth”); in his view this teaching arose from the teaching of Shankara.
<!-- F19 -->

“He set forth the doctrine of the non-dual Brahman, and the Lord refuted him with the reasoning of his own scripture.
Whatever he said, the Lord refuted it all. No answer came to his lips, and he was struck utterly dumb.” (CC, Madhya
18.187–188)

The Lord did not reject His interlocutor's scripture or pass judgement on his faith: He took His stand on that very
scripture and showed that its conclusion is love for the personal God and service to Him. He said:

“Your scripture establishes the impersonal, but in the end it refutes that and establishes the personal (*savisesa*). At
the end your scripture speaks of ‘one God alone’, ‘full of all majesty’, whose form is dark.” (CC, Madhya 18.189–190)

This Lord is eternity, knowledge and bliss, the source and shelter of all, the cause of all causes;

“…By devotion to Him the jiva is delivered from samsara. Without serving Him, the jiva is never free of samsara. Love
for His feet is ‘the essence of all human goals’. The joy of liberation and the like is not a particle of that bliss:
to serve His feet is to attain complete bliss.” (CC, Madhya 18.193–195)

The scripture first speaks of duty, knowledge and mystic power — of karma, jnana and yoga — but then rejects these paths
and establishes God and service to Him; and of all its injunctions, the last, concluding one decides. “Look into your
own scripture, consider it carefully, and tell Me plainly what it has written at the end,” the Lord said (CC, Madhya
18.198). Srila Bhaktivinoda Thakura explains: where the scripture describes how Muhammad saw God in the seventh heaven,
it acknowledges the complete form of God; service to Him the Muslims call “ibadat,” and the goal of life this scripture
names is love. In the headings of the “Anubhashya” Srila Bhaktisiddhanta is more definite still: at the very end of the
Koran there is a pointer to Krishna, to Brahman possessed of attributes (*savishesha*); the later injunction of the
scripture — on devotion — is stronger than the earlier ones on karma and jnana. One biography, following Bhaktivinoda,
says that the Lord grounded devotion to Krishna in the Koran's own words. <!-- F20 F31 -->

The pir acknowledged that the Lord was right: yes, so it is written in the scripture, but no one can grasp and accept
it; everyone speaks of the impersonal Lord, and no one knows that it is the Lord with form who is to be served. And he
said:

“You are that very Lord — God Himself. Be merciful to me; I am an unworthy sinner. I have studied much of the mleccha
scriptures, yet I cannot determine what the goal is and what the means. At the sight of You my tongue speaks the name
of Krsna, and my pride — ‘I am a great scholar’ — is gone.” (CC, Madhya 18.201–203)

The Pathans, already delighted by the mere sight of the Lord, on hearing Him set forth the real conclusion on the goal and the
means (*sadhya-sadhana*), became still more attached to Him. And on the tongue of the one who had just been arguing,
Krishna's name appeared of itself, without any effort: as Srila Bhaktisiddhanta says, at the sight of the Lord the name
of Krishna appears of itself on the maulana's tongue, and his pride vanishes. <!-- F21 I2 -->

“The Lord said, ‘Rise. You have taken the name of Krsna. The sins of millions of births are gone; you have become pure.’
He instructed them, ‘Say “Krsna”! Say “Krsna”!’ Everyone said ‘Krsna’, and all were overwhelmed with love.” (CC, Madhya
18.205–206)

Srila Bhaktisiddhanta explains that even a semblance (*abhasa*) of Krishna's name had destroyed the heap of his sins. The
Lord instructed the pir in the name of Krishna and gave him the name “Ramadasa” — an initiated name, as the
“Anubhashya” says[^9]. <!-- F22 F23 -->

## The Prince at the Lord's Feet
Having given the pir the name “Ramadasa,” Kaviraja Gosvami at once names the one for whose sake we tell this story:

“…Another Pathan there was named Bijali Khan. He was young, the son of a king, and
Ramadasa and the other Pathans were his servants. Crying ‘Krsna!’, he fell at Mahaprabhu's feet, and the Lord placed
His holy foot on his head.” (CC, Madhya 18.207–209)

The biographies tell it thus: when the prince Bijli Khan saw what fortune had come to his Pathan servant, he too said
“Krishna, Krishna” and fell at Mahaprabhu's feet, and the Lord showed mercy to him too. That the Lord touched his head
with His foot they do not say; the “Charitamrita” and the “Anubhashya” say it plainly: the Lord placed His holy foot on
his head — the highest blessing the Lord bestows on one who surrenders to Him. Srila Bhaktivinoda Thakura writes: “Bijali
Khan and the horsemen under him took shelter at Mahaprabhu's feet and became ‘devotees of Krishna.’” <!-- F24 -->

No one forced these men: the armed horsemen were holding captives, and the Lord was a defenceless sannyasi without a
coin. They were drawn by His beauty, His love and His word; the name of Krishna appeared of itself on the pir's tongue;
and the young prince, the pir's master, seeing what good fortune his servant had received, fell of his own accord at
the Lord's feet. So the faith of the servant who had received mercy awakened faith in his master as well. <!-- I3 -->

## The Pathan Vaishnavas
“Having shown mercy to them all, the Lord went on His way, and those Pathans all became renunciants. They became famous
as the ‘Pathan Vaisnavas’, and they wandered everywhere singing the glories of Mahaprabhu. That Bijali Khan became a
maha-bhagavata, and his greatness was renowned at every holy place.” (CC, Madhya 18.210–212)

The biographies too cite these verses when they speak of him[^10]. The Pathans accepted renunciation —
*vairagya-dharma*, as Srila Bhaktisiddhanta writes — not because they had become paupers, but so that, leaving everything,
they might wander and sing the Lord's glories everywhere; and people called them “the Pathan Vaishnavas.” Bijli Khan
himself became a maha-bhagavata: “the greatness of Bijali Khan, the maha-bhagavata, spread everywhere,” writes the
“Anubhashya.” A Pathan prince, a Muslim by birth, was honoured at every tirtha, as Haridasa Thakura was honoured: a
Vaishnava is defined not by birth but by devotion to Krishna. <!-- F25 F26 Q1 -->

According to Srila Bhaktivinoda Thakura, “on that spot there stands even now a village known as ‘the village of the
Pathan Vaishnavas’”; one biography says the same. Where this village is, is not stated[^11]. <!-- F27 Q4 -->

“Such are the pastimes of the Lord Sri Krsna Caitanya. Coming to the West, He blessed the yavanas and others.” (CC,
Madhya 18.213)

Srila Bhaktisiddhanta explains: coming to the United Provinces, the Lord delivers the mlecchas. (Mlecchas is the name for
peoples who do not follow the Vedas.) Then, after His bath in the Ganga at Soro-kshetra, the Lord followed her bank to
Prayaga; Krishnadasa the Rajput and the Sanodiya brahmana begged Him to take them along as far as Prayaga: in a land of
Muslims trouble might arise, and Balabhadra Bhattacharya did not know their language. Of Bijli Khan and his Pathans the
sources say no more. <!-- F28 -->

Govinda-deva too remembers this event in the “Gaura-krishnodaya”:

“As the Lord went along the roads as He pleased, even the dull-witted people of the middle country, through their
attraction to true love, became blessed; even a wicked Yavana there became Ramadasa.” (“Gaura-krishnodaya” 14.60)

He names only Ramadasa; his master's name is not in this verse. <!-- F29 -->

## Significance
The whole story of Bijli Khan, told by Kaviraja Gosvami in the eighteenth chapter of the Madhya-lila (verses 159–212)
and retold by the biographies, fits into a single day on the road from Vraja to Prayaga. But that day shows that
Mahaprabhu's mercy does not look at birth: a young Pathan prince, a Muslim by birth, received it and became a celebrated
Vaishnava. Whoever sees the Lord and hears from Him the real conclusion about the goal and the path is himself drawn to
the name of Krishna — even one who had just been arguing with Him. And the good fortune of one devotee awakens faith in
another: seeing what his servant had received, the master himself fell at the Lord's feet and became a maha-bhagavata,
honoured at every holy place. <!-- I1 I2 I3 Q3 -->
