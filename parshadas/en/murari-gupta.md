# Murari Gupta
*Мурари Гупта · @murari-gupta*

## Hanuman in the Pastimes of Gaura
Sri Murari Gupta was a physician of Navadvipa, a fellow student of Nimai Pandita and one of His closest associates —
the one who first set down the Lord's pastimes in writing. Kavi Karnapura's “Gaura-ganoddesha-dipika” reveals who he
was in the Lord's earlier pastimes:

“Murari Gupta is Hanuman; Angada is Sri Purandara; he who was called Sugriva is Govindananda.” (“Gaura-ganoddesha-
dipika” 91)

So all the original sources see him. Lochana Dasa, at the opening of his “Chaitanya-mangala,” glorifies Murari as
Hanuman; the Lord Himself tells him, “I am Ramachandra, and you are Hanuman” (CB, Madhya 10.14) — and many years later,
in Puri, “You are the servant of Sri Ramachandra, Hanuman himself” (CC, Madhya 15.156). And Vrindavana Dasa says that
once Murari received the Lord's mercy “which before only Hanuman had been granted” (CB, Madhya 20.52). <!-- F3 Q1 -->

An eternal servant of Rama, in Gaura's pastimes too he remained the servant of his Ramachandra, and this fidelity, as
we shall see, became one of the most moving lessons Mahaprabhu taught the world. Krishnadasa Kaviraja, enumerating the
branches of the tree of Sri Chaitanya, says of him:

“Sri Murari Gupta is a branch, a storehouse of love; whenever the Lord heard his humble words, His heart would melt.”
(CC, Adi 10.49)

Humility is the ornament of a Vaishnava, and seeing it in Murari, the Lord could not hold back His feelings.
<!-- F6 Q6 I1 -->

Krishnadasa Kaviraja names him more than once among the very closest. Murari is one of those intimate associates who
“are learned men of the highest greatness, and service to Chaitanya makes every one of them mad” (CC, Adi 6.48–49);
together with Svarupa Damodara he is praised as one of the two moons that “have risen and driven all darkness away”
(Adi 13.4); the author places his feet on his own head beside those of Advaita, Nityananda, Shrivasa and Gadadhara
(Adi 4.227–228). And the Lord Himself, speaking in Puri with Vallabha Bhatta, recalls: “Kashishvara, Mukunda, Vasudeva,
Murari and many other Vaishnavas were born in the land of Bengal” (CC, Antya 7.49). <!-- F7 Q24 -->

The original sources call him by various names. The Lord addresses him affectionately simply as “Gupta” (CC, Madhya
15.138, 15.153; CB, Madhya 3.44; Antya 4.317, 4.342), and once calls him “Murari Pandita” (CB, Madhya 1.70). In his own
poem Murari calls himself “Murari the vaidya,” that is, the physician, and “a lion among physicians” (“Kadacha” 2.2.11,
2.4.22, 2.7.18, 2.9.1, 4.17.11); Vrindavana Dasa calls him “the best of physicians” (CB, Madhya 10.10–11). The Lord gave
him one more name — “Ramadasa,” of which we shall speak below — and later generations call him Murari Gupta Thakura.
<!-- F1 -->

## Origin
Murari came from Shrihatta (Sylhet), the land from which many of the Lord's associates came. Vrindavana Dasa, having
named Shrivasa Pandita, Shrirama Pandita and Chandrashekhara, continues:

“All these Vaishnavas appeared in Shrihatta. A native of Shrihatta, too, was Sri Murari Gupta, who could heal the soul
of the disease of worldliness.” (CB, Adi 2.35)

Krishnadasa Kaviraja, however, names him among those born in Radha-desha, on the western side of the Ganga:

“In the land of Radha were born Thakura Nityananda, Gangadasa Pandita, Murari Gupta and Mukunda.” (CC, Adi 13.61)[^1]
<!-- F8 Q3 Q20 -->

Murari came of a family of vaidyas — hereditary physicians of Ayurveda; in his poem he calls himself just that: “Murari,
the son of a physician” (“Kadacha” 1.1.10). By birth he was not a brahmana, and the “Chaitanya-charitamrita” mentions
him among the non-brahmanas (Antya 10.140). The names of his parents no one has preserved. He was married; his wife,
whose name the sources also do not give, was a chaste and devoted woman, and we shall meet her again in the narratives
of Vrindavana Dasa. <!-- F9 F10 F14 -->

Murari was born before the Lord: he was a little older than Nimai[^2]. Vrindavana Dasa says that by His will
Mahaprabhu's eternal associates were born in Nadia before Him:

“By the order of Lord Chaitanya many of His eternal associates — Sri Chandrashekhara, Jagadisha, Gopinatha Acharya,
Shriman Pandita, Murari Gupta, Sri Garuda Pandita and Gangadasa Pandita — took birth secretly in Nadia before His
coming.” (CB, Adi 2.98–99)

Of this same descent of associates who came into the world before the Lord, Kavi Karnapura (“Chaitanya-chandrodaya”
1.21–25) and Murari himself (“Kadacha” 1.4) also tell. <!-- F11 F12 Q4 -->

The biographies add that Murari's family moved from Shrihatta to Navadvipa and settled next to the house of
Jagannatha Mishra, so that Murari was a companion in many of Nimai's childhood games[^3]. Kavi Karnapura testifies that
Murari knew the Lord's pastimes from His very childhood (“Chaitanya-charitamrita-mahakavya” 20.42–49), and Jayananda in
his “Chaitanya-mangala” names a certain Murari among the boys who went with Gaurachandra to the teacher's house.
<!-- F13 -->

## Nimai's Fellow Student
As youths Murari and Nimai studied grammar in the school of Gangadasa Pandita. Vrindavana Dasa names Vishvambhara's
schoolfellows:

“Vishvambhara's school friends were Sri Murari Gupta, Sri Kamalakanta and Sri Krishnananda.” (CB, Adi 8.38)

Murari was the senior among Gangadasa's students. Nimai loved to put tricky questions to His fellows: to Murari,
Krishnananda, Kamalakanta and the others He would explain one and the same sutra now one way, now another, and no one
could get the better of Him. <!-- F19 F11 -->

Murari took no part in these learned skirmishes, and for that very reason Nimai decided to provoke him (CB, Adi 10.11).
He was sixteen then; in learning He surpassed Brihaspati, the teacher of the gods, and gave no peace to those who relied
on themselves alone (10.14–18). Murari silently went about his own business, and the Lord, pleased with His servant,
still went on teasing him (10.19–20):

“He said: ‘O doctor, why study here? Better go and gather leaves and herbs to treat your patients. Grammar books are
hard to understand, and there is nothing in them about phlegm, bile or indigestion. What will you gain by studying on
your own? Better go home and treat the sick.’” (CB, Adi 10.21–23) <!-- F20 -->

Vrindavana Dasa remarks here that Murari is a portion of Rudra and by nature extremely fiery, yet Vishvambhara's words
did not anger him (Adi 10.24). The words “a portion of Rudra” do not reproach him with a worldly flaw but point to his
nature: Hanuman, the eternal servant of Rama, is himself a portion of Rudra. And so his meekness is all the more
precious: fiery by nature, he bears Nimai's mockery without anger and answers Him with dignity, as is fitting<!-- F4 -->:

“Murari replied: ‘Dear sir, why are You so proud? Why do You argue with everyone? Did You ask me a question about the
sutra, the vritti, the panji or the tika, and get no answer? You have asked me nothing, yet You declare that I know
nothing. You are a respectable brahmana — what can I say in reply?’” (CB, Adi 10.25–27)

Then Nimai told him to explain that day's lesson. Murari drew a conclusion from the earlier rules, and the Lord brought
forward another, opposite one and refuted his explanation; then He refuted His own and established it again. “So the
servant and the master could not defeat each other in debate” (Adi 10.28–29). <!-- F21 -->

“By the Lord's mercy Murari Gupta was exceptionally learned. The Lord listened to his explanations with great pleasure.
Pleased with His servant, the Lord touched him with His lotus hand, and Murari's body filled with bliss. Murari Gupta
thought: ‘He cannot be an ordinary man! Could an ordinary man possess such learning? Simply because He touched me, my
whole body has filled with bliss.’” (CB, Adi 10.30–33)

From the touch of that hand a deep attachment to Nimai and love for Him awoke in Murari's heart, and ever new truths
began to open to him. So he understood early that his young companion was no ordinary person. <!-- F22 Q5 -->

“‘Why should I be ashamed to learn from Him? In all Navadvipa there is no one more intelligent.’ Feeling the deepest
satisfaction, that great doctor (Murari Gupta) said: ‘Listen, Vishvambhara: from this day on I will study under You.’”
(CB, Adi 10.34–35) <!-- F23 -->

Lochana Dasa tells another, mischievous story of Nimai's childhood, which is found neither in Vrindavana Dasa nor in
Murari himself. In his youth, Lochana says, Murari was attached to the path of knowledge and to yoga. Once he was walking
along the road with his followers, wagging his head and waving his arms as he expounded a yoga-shastra to them; little
Nimai and the other children ran after him, mimicking his gait and his speech. Murari grew angry and scolded the boy,
and He slyly promised, “You'll find out at mealtime!” When Murari sat down to eat, Nimai came into his house,
approached him and filled his plate with urine. “What is this? Ugh!” — Murari leapt up, while Gaura, clapping His
hands, said, “Abandoning the path of bhakti, you expound yoga! Neglect knowledge and ritual and serve Krishna with all
your heart — He is full of consciousness and bliss, He is the treasure of the gopis' lives” (CM, Adi-khanda, pt. 4,
vv. 383–397). Lochana Dasa explains that the boy's “anger” was mercy: so the Lord broke Murari's attachment to dry
knowledge and turned him to bhakti. A child's prank that made a grown man jump up from his meal was a lesson for life:
food not offered to Krishna and learning not given to His service are equally unfit. The boy vanished, and Murari
understood: “Shachi's son is the Lord Himself.” He hurried to the house of Jagannatha Mishra, fell prostrate before
Nimai in ecstasy, told the parents that their son was the God of gods, bade them keep this secret, and told Advaita of
His pastimes (CM, Adi-khanda, pts. 4–5, vv. 398–403). <!-- F24 -->

## After Gaya
When Nimai Pandita returned from Gaya, where He had received initiation from Ishvara Puri, He could not be recognized:
the proud conqueror of all the scholars of Navadvipa had become a devotee such as the world had never seen. Murari heard
the news from Shriman Pandita. Having been with the Lord, Shriman told the Vaishnavas: “Seeing such extraordinary
devotion to God in Him, I no longer consider Him a mere mortal. When He came to Himself, He said to us: ‘Let us meet
tomorrow early in the morning in the house of Shuklambara. O Sadashiva and Murari Pandita, I want to reveal to you what
My heart is pining for’” (CB, Madhya 1.68–70). In the morning, in the house of Shuklambara Brahmachari, there gathered
“all the Lord's intimate associates, such as Sadashiva, Murari, Shriman and Shuklambara” (1.81), and with wonder and
joy they saw Him weeping for Krishna. The biographies say that the Lord's wondrous transformations in Shuklambara's
house were witnessed by Gadadhara, Sadashiva, Murari, Shrivasa and Shriman. <!-- F32 Q21 -->

From then on Murari was always beside the Lord. He himself recalls that in the house of Shrivasa and Sri Rama the Lord
sang and danced night after night, and with Him were Mukunda “and another vaidya” (“Kadacha” 2.2.5) — so, without
naming himself, the author speaks of himself. <!-- F15 -->

## The Boar in Murari's House
The Lord loved Murari as Ramachandra loved Hanuman (CB, Madhya 3.19). And one day He came to his house Himself:

“One day Vishvambhara heard a verse glorifying Lord Varaha, and with a thunderous roar He made for Murari's house.”
(CB, Madhya 3.18)

Varaha, the Boar, is the third of the Lord's ten principal descents. Murari at once rose and bowed before Him, while
the Lord, approaching the house, kept crying, “Boar! Boar!” — and the astonished Murari looked about him (3.20–21).
<!-- F33 Q8 F34 -->

“Vishvambhara entered the Vishnu shrine and saw right before Him a beautiful pitcher full of water. At once Mahaprabhu
assumed the form of Varaha and, taking the pitcher in His teeth, lifted it above His head. The holy boar roared loudly,
and four tusks jutted from His mouth. The Lord commanded: ‘O Murari, offer Me prayers!’” (CB, Madhya 3.22–24)

Murari himself, in his poem, relates that the Lord, resting on the ground on His knees and hands and filling
everything with His roar, lifted a brass water-vessel on the tips of His tusks and said: “Describe My true form”
(“Kadacha” 2.2.15–17); Lochana Dasa writes that He seized the brass vessel in His teeth, and Kavi Karnapura that He
flung the pitcher aside with His tusks and commanded, “Describe Me!”[^4] Murari was struck dumb: he had never seen
anything like it. “Speak, do not be afraid,” the Lord encouraged him. “All this time you did not suspect that I was
here” (CB, Madhya 3.25–26). <!-- F34 F35 -->

And Murari, trembling, began to speak of his own unworthiness:

“O Lord, You alone know Your glory. Lord Ananta, who holds countless universes on each of His heads, ceaselessly praises
You with His thousand mouths. And yet He admits that He cannot reach the limit of Your glory. Who, then, is able to
glorify You fully? Even the Vedas, whose injunctions all must follow, do not know You as You are. <…> Therefore You
alone know Yourself. All others can know You only when You Yourself wish to reveal Yourself to them.” (CB, Madhya
3.27–33)

Saying this, he burst into tears and bowed before the Lord (3.34); his life, he felt, had found its fulfilment. In
Murari's own poem, in Lochana Dasa and in Karnapura, he answers the Lord in the words of the “Bhagavad-gita”: “You
alone know Yourself by Yourself, O Supreme Person” (10.15). <!-- F36 -->

His prayer satisfied the Lord, and He, angry at the Vedas, said:

“‘The Vedas slander Me, claiming that I have no hands, feet, mouth or eyes. In Kashi lives a rogue named Prakashananda
who hacks My body to pieces. He explains the Vedas, yet rejects My eternal form. Though leprosy has already covered his
whole body, he still cannot understand his mistake.’” (CB, Madhya 3.36–38)

The Lord has in mind such verses as the “Shvetashvatara Upanishad” (3.19): “Without hands and feet, He is swift and
grasps; without eyes He sees, without ears He hears…” — and it is this verse, as Murari and Lochana tell it, that He
recites, laughing: “The Veda does not know Me at all” (“Kadacha” 2.2.20–24). The denial of hands and feet to the Lord
means only that He has no body of matter, but the Mayavadis, missing the true sense of such mantras, teach that the
Lord has no form at all. Prakashananda Sarasvati was then the head of the sannyasis of Shankara's school in Kashi[^5];
his doctrine — that the Lord's body is made of maya — is what the Lord calls “hacking His body to pieces”: “My body is
perfectly pure and is the embodiment of all sacrifices. Therefore even such great personalities as Brahma and Shiva
glorify it” (CB, Madhya 3.39). <!-- F37 Q9 -->

“O Murari Gupta, listen to Me. I will tell you the essence of all teachings. I will reveal to you the secret knowledge
of the Vedas. I am Varaha, the Lord of all holy sacrifices. I am the essence of the Vedas. It was I who delivered the
Earth in ancient times. I have descended to begin the movement of sankirtana. For the sake of My devotees I will destroy
all the wicked.” (CB, Madhya 3.41–43)

Enmity toward His devotees, the Lord said, He would tolerate from no one — not even from His own son. Once, having
raised the Earth from the waters, He begot a son by her, Naraka, and Himself instructed him in dharma; but, falling in
with Bana, Naraka began to persecute the devotees, and the Lord slew him (3.44–50). “You serve Me life after life,” He
concluded. “That is why I have revealed this secret to you” (3.51). The Lord's anger here is no worldly rage: it is the
protection of His servants. Murari listened and could not hold back his tears and loud weeping (3.52), and Vrindavana
Dasa exclaims: “Glory to Sri Gaurachandra and His eternal associate Murari! Glory to Varaha, the Lord of sacrifices
and protector of the devotees!” (3.53) <!-- F38 -->

Krishnadasa Kaviraja recalls this day in a single verse:

“In Murari's house the Lord became absorbed in the mood of Varaha, and climbing onto Murari's shoulders, He danced in
the courtyard.” (CC, Adi 17.19)[^6] <!-- F39 Q7 -->

Lochana Dasa continues the story. When Murari, bowing down, asked, “Be merciful, Lord, grant me prema,” the Lord
replied, “Love Me — that is prema for you,” and told him to worship Krishna, the son of Nanda, sapphire-hued, flute in
hand, together with the daughter of Vrishabhanu, surrounded by the young beauties of Vraja. But Murari, falling at His
feet, asked, “Let me see with my own eyes the form of Raghunatha,” and that very moment he saw Rama, dark as durva
grass, with Lakshmana, Bharata and Shatrughna. He fell unconscious, and the Lord, touching him with His lotus hand,
blessed him: “Be full of prema: you are Hanuman, and I am that same Ramachandra.” The next day, in Shrivasa's house,
the Lord, still in the mood of the Boar, revealed to the devotees the glory of Krishna's holy name (CM, Madhya-khanda,
pt. 1, vv. 84–136). In those same days, according to Lochana, the gods headed by Brahma came to the Lord and begged for
Krishna-prema — and received it. <!-- F40 -->

## Rebuke and Blessing
Murari received another instruction from the Lord soon after this. All three poets who knew these pastimes — Murari
himself, Lochana Dasa and Kavi Karnapura — relate that the Lord rebuked him for a song on the “Self” (adhyatma), that
is, for a composition in the spirit of speculative knowledge, and told him to give it up for songs of Hari. As Murari
tells it, the Lord first placed His foot on Mukunda's head and then said:

“‘How is it, vaidya, that you composed a song on the doctrine of the Self? If you desire life or long for love of Hari,
give up that song and compose a verse about Hari yourself.’” (“Kadacha” 2.4.22–23)

Murari humbly asked for a command — that he might tell of the Lord's present descent — and the Lord, smiling, blessed
him: “What this vaidya says will be the truth of truths” (2.4.24–26). So the rebuke turned into a blessing, to which
we owe his book. <!-- F42 -->

In Lochana the Lord tells Murari to give up his reasonings on the Self and worship Krishna surrounded by the gopis, and
Murari tearfully begs for mercy: without it no one can conquer maya — neither Brahma, nor Shiva, nor Lakshmi, nor
Ananta (CM, Madhya-khanda, pt. 3, vv. 64–80). Karnapura writes that the Lord told him to give up “spiritual”
compositions and describe His feet, and when the physician Narayana asked for mercy on Murari, He blessed him:
whatever Murari said would be flawless (“Chaitanya-charitamrita-mahakavya” 6.42–46). In the drama
“Chaitanya-chandrodaya” it is Advaita who asks for mercy on him, and the Lord says that in Murari's mind there is as
yet no rasa of bhakti, only contemplation of the “supreme Self”: he is still studying the “Vasishtha”; and then the
Lord places His feet on the heads of Murari and Mukunda, and both pray to be made servants of the servants of His
devotees (1.61–64). One of the biographies, retelling Lochana, puts it briefly: the Lord gave Murari His blessing.
<!-- F42 F41 -->

One should not think that Murari had “fallen” and the Lord raised him up. An eternal associate of the Lord takes on
in His pastimes the role of one in need of instruction, so that all may hear the lesson: speculation on the Self, even
the most exalted, does not give love for Krishna, while a word given to Hari becomes “the truth of truths.” Another
time, while glorifying Advaita, the Lord forbade all the devotees to speak of the doctrine of the Self; Murari said, “I
do not know it,” and the Lord, smiling, replied, “You do: you heard it from Kamalaksha,” that is, from Advaita
(“Kadacha” 2.6.23–26). <!-- F42 -->

## The Coming of Nityananda
When Nityananda came to Navadvipa, the Lord knew of it before anyone else: He dreamed of a great personality who had
arrived in the town. Returning to outer consciousness, He Himself, “as the friend of Rama,” explained the meaning of the
dream to the Vaishnavas: “I think that someone truly great has come to Navadvipa” (CB, Madhya 3.157–158)[^7].
According to the “Chaitanya-bhagavata,” the Lord sent Haridasa and Shrivasa to look for the guest (3.160). Murari,
however, relates that those sent were Rama, Murari himself, Narayana and Mukunda: in the daytime they came back
empty-handed, and in the evening the Lord, meeting Murari on the road, said to him: “Let us go where He is: the best of
sages has come to the house of Nandana Acharya,” and together with him and the other devotees He came to Nityananda
(“Kadacha” 2.8.3–9); Kavi Karnapura tells it the same way (“Chaitanya-charitamrita-mahakavya” 6.106–116). <!-- F64 -->

At their first meeting the two Lords, Gaura and Nityananda, began to glorify each other, and the Vaishnavas, listening
to Them, did not know what to say:

“Murari smiled and said: ‘Only You can understand each other. We have understood nothing.’” (CB, Madhya 4.57)
<!-- F65 Q25 -->

Soon the Lord sent Shrivasa, Rama, “Murari the physician” and Narayana to Shantipura to inform Advaita of the
Avadhuta's coming. Advaita, hearing the news, began to sing and dance; the messengers stayed with Him two days and
returned with Him to the Lord (“Kadacha” 2.9.13–16). In Lochana Dasa it is Shrinivasa, Narayana, Rama and Murari who
bring Nityananda to Advaita, and on their return Murari tells the Lord everything. <!-- F66 -->

## The Mahaprakasha: “I Am Ramachandra, and You Are Hanuman”
In Shrivasa's house the Lord manifested the Mahaprakasha, the great revelation: for twenty-one hours — seven
prahars — seated on Vishnu's throne, He revealed to each of the devotees His true nature and their eternal service (CB,
Madhya 9–10). Nityananda held an umbrella over His head, Gadadhara offered Him betel, and before Him stood great souls
headed by Advaita (10.5–6). And then the Lord called Murari:

“The Lord commanded Murari: ‘Behold My form!’ And Murari saw Mahaprabhu in the form of Ramachandra. Before his eyes
was Vishvambhara, seated like a mighty archer in the hero's posture (virasana). His body was dark, the colour of young
durva grass. To the Lord's left and right stood Sita and Lakshmana, and on all sides they were surrounded by the
leaders of the monkeys, offering prayers to the Lord.” (CB, Madhya 10.7–9)

Murari saw his chosen Deity, his Ramachandra — and among the monkeys glorifying Him with joined palms he saw himself as
well. This “best of physicians,” says Vrindavana Dasa, lost consciousness and fell at the Lord's feet (10.10–11).
<!-- F44 Q10 F1 -->

“Vishvambhara cried out: ‘O leader of the monkeys, have you forgotten how Ravana, who stole Sita, burned you? And you
set fire to his whole capital and subdued his whole race. Hear My words: “I am your Lord.” Get up, Murari. You are as
dear to Me as life itself. I am Ramachandra, and you are Hanuman. Look, here stands the son of Sumitra, your life and
soul. To bring Him back to life you carried a whole mountain, Gandhamadana. Bow down at the feet of Sita, whose
sufferings made you weep bitterly.’” (CB, Madhya 10.12–16)

This is no worldly “miracle” and no delusion: the Lord revealed to His servant his eternal form and eternal service, and
Murari recognized in Gaura the One whom he had always served. Coming to himself, he looked at the Lord again and wept
with love so that, in Vrindavana Dasa's words, even stones melted (10.17–18). <!-- F45 -->

The Lord said, “Ask of Me any blessing you wish.” And Murari asked:

“‘O Lord, I need nothing. Only let me always sing Your glory. O Lord, I may be born anywhere, but wherever I am born,
let me always remember You. Let me, life after life, keep company with Your servants. O Lord, do not let me fall into a
state where You cease to be my Master and I cease to be Your servant. Let me always be Your servant, wherever You come
with Your associates.’” (CB, Madhya 10.20–24)

Not the fulfilment of desires, not liberation does he ask for, but one thing alone: always to serve and always to be
born among devotees. “I grant you this blessing,” the Lord replied, and on all sides rose the cry: “Glory! Glory!”
(10.25). <!-- F46 -->

At that same time the Lord explained his name. Murari, “the Enemy of Mura,” is a name of Krishna; gupta means
“hidden”:

“‘Lord Murari dwells unseen (gupta) in his heart; therefore the name “Murari Gupta” suits him perfectly.’” (CB, Madhya
10.31)

And later Vrindavana Dasa, naming Murari among those who came to Puri, repeats: “Lord Gauranga secretly dwelt in his
body” (Antya 8.33). <!-- F2 Q2 F46 -->

Vrindavana Dasa adds: the Vaishnavas doted on Murari, who was kind to all living beings; wherever he appeared, the place
became holy like Vaikuntha; in every descent of the Lord he was His closest associate (CB, Madhya 10.26–28). And the
Lord said to those assembled: “Whoever speaks an ill word about Murari will not be saved even by a million baths in the
Ganga. Neither the holy Ganga nor the holy names of Hari will purify such a sinner” (10.29–30). Whoever hears of the
Lord's mercy to Murari will attain prema-bhakti (10.33). That night Murari and Shridhara stood before the Lord and wept
(10.34), and when the Lord bestowed His mercy on Haridasa, Haridasa, Murari and Shridhara wept (10.112). The mercy that
even the servants of Murari Gupta received, says Vrindavana Dasa, cannot be had by merely shaving one's head — by an
outward sannyasa alone (10.277). <!-- F47 F5 -->

Krishnadasa Kaviraja, in his account of the Mahaprakasha, recalls other words:

“The Lord said to Murari, ‘You have brought Krishna under your control,’ and hearing this, Murari began to recite a
verse: ‘Who am I, a poor and sinful man, and who is Krishna, the abode of the goddess of fortune? Yet because I was born
a brahmana, He embraced me in His arms!’ (Srimad-Bhagavatam, 10.81.16)” (CC, Adi 17.77–78)

These are the words of Sudama, Krishna's poor friend. Murari was not a brahmana by birth, and in repeating them he
speaks not of his social standing but of his unworthiness: so a Vaishnava's humility answers the Lord's praise.
<!-- F48 -->

Murari himself tells of this at greater length. Once the Lord said to the devotees: “When I leave My mother and go away
to distant lands, everyone will say of Me, ‘He did wrong.’” “No, Master, no one will say so,” Murari objected, and the
Lord, delighted, embraced him. Then Murari recited Sudama's verse: “Where am I — a pauper, the most sinful — and where is
Krishna, the abode of Shri! And yet me, a brahmana by birth alone, He embraced in His arms.” Hearing it, the Lord shone
like the thousand-rayed sun and said, “Know that this body is the highest concentrate of being and consciousness”;
Shrivasa bathed Him with the waters of the Ganga, Nityananda held an umbrella over Him, Gadadhara offered betel, and all
sang and danced (“Kadacha” 2.12.5–17). Lochana Dasa and Karnapura (“Chaitanya-charitamrita-mahakavya” 7.98–106) tell it
the same way, and in a song of Krishnadasa about this bathing “Mukunda and Murari sing.” <!-- F48 -->
