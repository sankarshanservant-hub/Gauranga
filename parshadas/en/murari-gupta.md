# Murari Gupta
*Мурари Гупта · @murari-gupta*

## Hanuman in the Pastimes of Gaura
Sri Murari Gupta was a physician of Navadvipa, a fellow student of Nimai Pandita and one of His closest associates —
the one who first set down the Lord's pastimes in writing. Kavi Karnapura's “Gaura-ganoddesha-dipika” reveals who he
was in the Lord's earlier pastimes:

“Murari Gupta is Hanuman; Angada is Sri Purandara; he who was called Sugriva is Govindananda.” (“Gaura-ganoddesha-
dipika” 91)

So all the original sources see him. Lochana Dasa, at the opening of his “Chaitanya-mangala,” glorifies Murari as
Hanuman; the Lord Himself tells him, “I am Ramachandra, and you are Hanuman” (CB, Madhya 10.14) — and many years later,
in Puri, “You are the servant of Sri Ramachandra, Hanuman himself” (CC, Madhya 15.156). And Vrindavana Dasa says that
once Murari received the Lord's mercy “which before only Hanuman had been granted” (CB, Madhya 20.52). <!-- F3 Q1 -->

An eternal servant of Rama, in Gaura's pastimes too he remained the servant of his Ramachandra, and this fidelity, as
we shall see, became one of the most moving lessons Mahaprabhu taught the world. Krishnadasa Kaviraja, enumerating the
branches of the tree of Sri Chaitanya, says of him:

“Sri Murari Gupta is a branch, a storehouse of love; whenever the Lord heard his humble words, His heart would melt.”
(CC, Adi 10.49)

Humility is the ornament of a Vaishnava, and seeing it in Murari, the Lord could not hold back His feelings.
<!-- F6 Q6 I1 -->

Krishnadasa Kaviraja names him more than once among the very closest. Murari is one of those intimate associates who
“are learned men of the highest greatness, and service to Chaitanya makes every one of them mad” (CC, Adi 6.48–49);
together with Svarupa Damodara he is praised as one of the two moons that “have risen and driven all darkness away”
(Adi 13.4); the author places his feet on his own head beside those of Advaita, Nityananda, Shrivasa and Gadadhara
(Adi 4.227–228). And the Lord Himself, speaking in Puri with Vallabha Bhatta, recalls: “Kashishvara, Mukunda, Vasudeva,
Murari and many other Vaishnavas were born in the land of Bengal” (CC, Antya 7.49). <!-- F7 Q24 -->

The original sources call him by various names. The Lord addresses him affectionately simply as “Gupta” (CC, Madhya
15.138, 15.153; CB, Madhya 3.44; Antya 4.317, 4.342), and once calls him “Murari Pandita” (CB, Madhya 1.70). In his own
poem Murari calls himself “Murari the vaidya,” that is, the physician, and “a lion among physicians” (“Kadacha” 2.2.11,
2.4.22, 2.7.18, 2.9.1, 4.17.11); Vrindavana Dasa calls him “the best of physicians” (CB, Madhya 10.10–11). The Lord gave
him one more name — “Ramadasa,” of which we shall speak below — and later generations call him Murari Gupta Thakura.
<!-- F1 -->

## Origin
Murari came from Shrihatta (Sylhet), the land from which many of the Lord's associates came. Vrindavana Dasa, having
named Shrivasa Pandita, Shrirama Pandita and Chandrashekhara, continues:

“All these Vaishnavas appeared in Shrihatta. A native of Shrihatta, too, was Sri Murari Gupta, who could heal the soul
of the disease of worldliness.” (CB, Adi 2.35)

Krishnadasa Kaviraja, however, names him among those born in Radha-desha, on the western side of the Ganga:

“In the land of Radha were born Thakura Nityananda, Gangadasa Pandita, Murari Gupta and Mukunda.” (CC, Adi 13.61)[^1]
<!-- F8 Q3 Q20 -->

Murari came of a family of vaidyas — hereditary physicians of Ayurveda; in his poem he calls himself just that: “Murari,
the son of a physician” (“Kadacha” 1.1.10). By birth he was not a brahmana, and the “Chaitanya-charitamrita” mentions
him among the non-brahmanas (Antya 10.140). The names of his parents no one has preserved. He was married; his wife,
whose name the sources also do not give, was a chaste and devoted woman, and we shall meet her again in the narratives
of Vrindavana Dasa. <!-- F9 F10 F14 -->

Murari was born before the Lord: he was a little older than Nimai[^2]. Vrindavana Dasa says that by His will
Mahaprabhu's eternal associates were born in Nadia before Him:

“By the order of Lord Chaitanya many of His eternal associates — Sri Chandrashekhara, Jagadisha, Gopinatha Acharya,
Shriman Pandita, Murari Gupta, Sri Garuda Pandita and Gangadasa Pandita — took birth secretly in Nadia before His
coming.” (CB, Adi 2.98–99)

Of this same descent of associates who came into the world before the Lord, Kavi Karnapura (“Chaitanya-chandrodaya”
1.21–25) and Murari himself (“Kadacha” 1.4) also tell. <!-- F11 F12 Q4 -->

The biographies add that Murari's family moved from Shrihatta to Navadvipa and settled next to the house of
Jagannatha Mishra, so that Murari was a companion in many of Nimai's childhood games[^3]. Kavi Karnapura testifies that
Murari knew the Lord's pastimes from His very childhood (“Chaitanya-charitamrita-mahakavya” 20.42–49), and Jayananda in
his “Chaitanya-mangala” names a certain Murari among the boys who went with Gaurachandra to the teacher's house.
<!-- F13 -->

## Nimai's Fellow Student
As youths Murari and Nimai studied grammar in the school of Gangadasa Pandita. Vrindavana Dasa names Vishvambhara's
schoolfellows:

“Vishvambhara's school friends were Sri Murari Gupta, Sri Kamalakanta and Sri Krishnananda.” (CB, Adi 8.38)

Murari was the senior among Gangadasa's students. Nimai loved to put tricky questions to His fellows: to Murari,
Krishnananda, Kamalakanta and the others He would explain one and the same sutra now one way, now another, and no one
could get the better of Him. <!-- F19 F11 -->

Murari took no part in these learned skirmishes, and for that very reason Nimai decided to provoke him (CB, Adi 10.11).
He was sixteen then; in learning He surpassed Brihaspati, the teacher of the gods, and gave no peace to those who relied
on themselves alone (10.14–18). Murari silently went about his own business, and the Lord, pleased with His servant,
still went on teasing him (10.19–20):

“He said: ‘O doctor, why study here? Better go and gather leaves and herbs to treat your patients. Grammar books are
hard to understand, and there is nothing in them about phlegm, bile or indigestion. What will you gain by studying on
your own? Better go home and treat the sick.’” (CB, Adi 10.21–23) <!-- F20 -->

Vrindavana Dasa remarks here that Murari is a portion of Rudra and by nature extremely fiery, yet Vishvambhara's words
did not anger him (Adi 10.24). The words “a portion of Rudra” do not reproach him with a worldly flaw but point to his
nature: Hanuman, the eternal servant of Rama, is himself a portion of Rudra. And so his meekness is all the more
precious: fiery by nature, he bears Nimai's mockery without anger and answers Him with dignity, as is fitting<!-- F4 -->:

“Murari replied: ‘Dear sir, why are You so proud? Why do You argue with everyone? Did You ask me a question about the
sutra, the vritti, the panji or the tika, and get no answer? You have asked me nothing, yet You declare that I know
nothing. You are a respectable brahmana — what can I say in reply?’” (CB, Adi 10.25–27)

Then Nimai told him to explain that day's lesson. Murari drew a conclusion from the earlier rules, and the Lord brought
forward another, opposite one and refuted his explanation; then He refuted His own and established it again. “So the
servant and the master could not defeat each other in debate” (Adi 10.28–29). <!-- F21 -->

“By the Lord's mercy Murari Gupta was exceptionally learned. The Lord listened to his explanations with great pleasure.
Pleased with His servant, the Lord touched him with His lotus hand, and Murari's body filled with bliss. Murari Gupta
thought: ‘He cannot be an ordinary man! Could an ordinary man possess such learning? Simply because He touched me, my
whole body has filled with bliss.’” (CB, Adi 10.30–33)

From the touch of that hand a deep attachment to Nimai and love for Him awoke in Murari's heart, and ever new truths
began to open to him. So he understood early that his young companion was no ordinary person. <!-- F22 Q5 -->

“‘Why should I be ashamed to learn from Him? In all Navadvipa there is no one more intelligent.’ Feeling the deepest
satisfaction, that great doctor (Murari Gupta) said: ‘Listen, Vishvambhara: from this day on I will study under You.’”
(CB, Adi 10.34–35) <!-- F23 -->

Lochana Dasa tells another, mischievous story of Nimai's childhood, which is found neither in Vrindavana Dasa nor in
Murari himself. In his youth, Lochana says, Murari was attached to the path of knowledge and to yoga. Once he was walking
along the road with his followers, wagging his head and waving his arms as he expounded a yoga-shastra to them; little
Nimai and the other children ran after him, mimicking his gait and his speech. Murari grew angry and scolded the boy,
and He slyly promised, “You'll find out at mealtime!” When Murari sat down to eat, Nimai came into his house,
approached him and filled his plate with urine. “What is this? Ugh!” — Murari leapt up, while Gaura, clapping His
hands, said, “Abandoning the path of bhakti, you expound yoga! Neglect knowledge and ritual and serve Krishna with all
your heart — He is full of consciousness and bliss, He is the treasure of the gopis' lives” (CM, Adi-khanda, pt. 4,
vv. 383–397). Lochana Dasa explains that the boy's “anger” was mercy: so the Lord broke Murari's attachment to dry
knowledge and turned him to bhakti. A child's prank that made a grown man jump up from his meal was a lesson for life:
food not offered to Krishna and learning not given to His service are equally unfit. The boy vanished, and Murari
understood: “Shachi's son is the Lord Himself.” He hurried to the house of Jagannatha Mishra, fell prostrate before
Nimai in ecstasy, told the parents that their son was the God of gods, bade them keep this secret, and told Advaita of
His pastimes (CM, Adi-khanda, pts. 4–5, vv. 398–403). <!-- F24 -->

## After Gaya
When Nimai Pandita returned from Gaya, where He had received initiation from Ishvara Puri, He could not be recognized:
the proud conqueror of all the scholars of Navadvipa had become a devotee such as the world had never seen. Murari heard
the news from Shriman Pandita. Having been with the Lord, Shriman told the Vaishnavas: “Seeing such extraordinary
devotion to God in Him, I no longer consider Him a mere mortal. When He came to Himself, He said to us: ‘Let us meet
tomorrow early in the morning in the house of Shuklambara. O Sadashiva and Murari Pandita, I want to reveal to you what
My heart is pining for’” (CB, Madhya 1.68–70). In the morning, in the house of Shuklambara Brahmachari, there gathered
“all the Lord's intimate associates, such as Sadashiva, Murari, Shriman and Shuklambara” (1.81), and with wonder and
joy they saw Him weeping for Krishna. The biographies say that the Lord's wondrous transformations in Shuklambara's
house were witnessed by Gadadhara, Sadashiva, Murari, Shrivasa and Shriman. <!-- F32 Q21 -->

From then on Murari was always beside the Lord. He himself recalls that in the house of Shrivasa and Sri Rama the Lord
sang and danced night after night, and with Him were Mukunda “and another vaidya” (“Kadacha” 2.2.5) — so, without
naming himself, the author speaks of himself. <!-- F15 -->

## The Boar in Murari's House
The Lord loved Murari as Ramachandra loved Hanuman (CB, Madhya 3.19). And one day He came to his house Himself:

“One day Vishvambhara heard a verse glorifying Lord Varaha, and with a thunderous roar He made for Murari's house.”
(CB, Madhya 3.18)

Varaha, the Boar, is the third of the Lord's ten principal descents. Murari at once rose and bowed before Him, while
the Lord, approaching the house, kept crying, “Boar! Boar!” — and the astonished Murari looked about him (3.20–21).
<!-- F33 Q8 F34 -->

“Vishvambhara entered the Vishnu shrine and saw right before Him a beautiful pitcher full of water. At once Mahaprabhu
assumed the form of Varaha and, taking the pitcher in His teeth, lifted it above His head. The holy boar roared loudly,
and four tusks jutted from His mouth. The Lord commanded: ‘O Murari, offer Me prayers!’” (CB, Madhya 3.22–24)

Murari himself, in his poem, relates that the Lord, resting on the ground on His knees and hands and filling
everything with His roar, lifted a brass water-vessel on the tips of His tusks and said: “Describe My true form”
(“Kadacha” 2.2.15–17); Lochana Dasa writes that He seized the brass vessel in His teeth, and Kavi Karnapura that He
flung the pitcher aside with His tusks and commanded, “Describe Me!”[^4] Murari was struck dumb: he had never seen
anything like it. “Speak, do not be afraid,” the Lord encouraged him. “All this time you did not suspect that I was
here” (CB, Madhya 3.25–26). <!-- F34 F35 -->

And Murari, trembling, began to speak of his own unworthiness:

“O Lord, You alone know Your glory. Lord Ananta, who holds countless universes on each of His heads, ceaselessly praises
You with His thousand mouths. And yet He admits that He cannot reach the limit of Your glory. Who, then, is able to
glorify You fully? Even the Vedas, whose injunctions all must follow, do not know You as You are. <…> Therefore You
alone know Yourself. All others can know You only when You Yourself wish to reveal Yourself to them.” (CB, Madhya
3.27–33)

Saying this, he burst into tears and bowed before the Lord (3.34); his life, he felt, had found its fulfilment. In
Murari's own poem, in Lochana Dasa and in Karnapura, he answers the Lord in the words of the “Bhagavad-gita”: “You
alone know Yourself by Yourself, O Supreme Person” (10.15). <!-- F36 -->

His prayer satisfied the Lord, and He, angry at the Vedas, said:

“‘The Vedas slander Me, claiming that I have no hands, feet, mouth or eyes. In Kashi lives a rogue named Prakashananda
who hacks My body to pieces. He explains the Vedas, yet rejects My eternal form. Though leprosy has already covered his
whole body, he still cannot understand his mistake.’” (CB, Madhya 3.36–38)

The Lord has in mind such verses as the “Shvetashvatara Upanishad” (3.19): “Without hands and feet, He is swift and
grasps; without eyes He sees, without ears He hears…” — and it is this verse, as Murari and Lochana tell it, that He
recites, laughing: “The Veda does not know Me at all” (“Kadacha” 2.2.20–24). The denial of hands and feet to the Lord
means only that He has no body of matter, but the Mayavadis, missing the true sense of such mantras, teach that the
Lord has no form at all. Prakashananda Sarasvati was then the head of the sannyasis of Shankara's school in Kashi[^5];
his doctrine — that the Lord's body is made of maya — is what the Lord calls “hacking His body to pieces”: “My body is
perfectly pure and is the embodiment of all sacrifices. Therefore even such great personalities as Brahma and Shiva
glorify it” (CB, Madhya 3.39). <!-- F37 Q9 -->

“O Murari Gupta, listen to Me. I will tell you the essence of all teachings. I will reveal to you the secret knowledge
of the Vedas. I am Varaha, the Lord of all holy sacrifices. I am the essence of the Vedas. It was I who delivered the
Earth in ancient times. I have descended to begin the movement of sankirtana. For the sake of My devotees I will destroy
all the wicked.” (CB, Madhya 3.41–43)

Enmity toward His devotees, the Lord said, He would tolerate from no one — not even from His own son. Once, having
raised the Earth from the waters, He begot a son by her, Naraka, and Himself instructed him in dharma; but, falling in
with Bana, Naraka began to persecute the devotees, and the Lord slew him (3.44–50). “You serve Me life after life,” He
concluded. “That is why I have revealed this secret to you” (3.51). The Lord's anger here is no worldly rage: it is the
protection of His servants. Murari listened and could not hold back his tears and loud weeping (3.52), and Vrindavana
Dasa exclaims: “Glory to Sri Gaurachandra and His eternal associate Murari! Glory to Varaha, the Lord of sacrifices
and protector of the devotees!” (3.53) <!-- F38 -->

Krishnadasa Kaviraja recalls this day in a single verse:

“In Murari's house the Lord became absorbed in the mood of Varaha, and climbing onto Murari's shoulders, He danced in
the courtyard.” (CC, Adi 17.19)[^6] <!-- F39 Q7 -->

Lochana Dasa continues the story. When Murari, bowing down, asked, “Be merciful, Lord, grant me prema,” the Lord
replied, “Love Me — that is prema for you,” and told him to worship Krishna, the son of Nanda, sapphire-hued, flute in
hand, together with the daughter of Vrishabhanu, surrounded by the young beauties of Vraja. But Murari, falling at His
feet, asked, “Let me see with my own eyes the form of Raghunatha,” and that very moment he saw Rama, dark as durva
grass, with Lakshmana, Bharata and Shatrughna. He fell unconscious, and the Lord, touching him with His lotus hand,
blessed him: “Be full of prema: you are Hanuman, and I am that same Ramachandra.” The next day, in Shrivasa's house,
the Lord, still in the mood of the Boar, revealed to the devotees the glory of Krishna's holy name (CM, Madhya-khanda,
pt. 1, vv. 84–136). In those same days, according to Lochana, the gods headed by Brahma came to the Lord and begged for
Krishna-prema — and received it. <!-- F40 -->

## Rebuke and Blessing
Murari received another instruction from the Lord soon after this. All three poets who knew these pastimes — Murari
himself, Lochana Dasa and Kavi Karnapura — relate that the Lord rebuked him for a song on the “Self” (adhyatma), that
is, for a composition in the spirit of speculative knowledge, and told him to give it up for songs of Hari. As Murari
tells it, the Lord first placed His foot on Mukunda's head and then said:

“‘How is it, vaidya, that you composed a song on the doctrine of the Self? If you desire life or long for love of Hari,
give up that song and compose a verse about Hari yourself.’” (“Kadacha” 2.4.22–23)

Murari humbly asked for a command — that he might tell of the Lord's present descent — and the Lord, smiling, blessed
him: “What this vaidya says will be the truth of truths” (2.4.24–26). So the rebuke turned into a blessing, to which
we owe his book. <!-- F42 -->

In Lochana the Lord tells Murari to give up his reasonings on the Self and worship Krishna surrounded by the gopis, and
Murari tearfully begs for mercy: without it no one can conquer maya — neither Brahma, nor Shiva, nor Lakshmi, nor
Ananta (CM, Madhya-khanda, pt. 3, vv. 64–80). Karnapura writes that the Lord told him to give up “spiritual”
compositions and describe His feet, and when the physician Narayana asked for mercy on Murari, He blessed him:
whatever Murari said would be flawless (“Chaitanya-charitamrita-mahakavya” 6.42–46). In the drama
“Chaitanya-chandrodaya” it is Advaita who asks for mercy on him, and the Lord says that in Murari's mind there is as
yet no rasa of bhakti, only contemplation of the “supreme Self”: he is still studying the “Vasishtha”; and then the
Lord places His feet on the heads of Murari and Mukunda, and both pray to be made servants of the servants of His
devotees (1.61–64). One of the biographies, retelling Lochana, puts it briefly: the Lord gave Murari His blessing.
<!-- F42 F41 -->

One should not think that Murari had “fallen” and the Lord raised him up. An eternal associate of the Lord takes on
in His pastimes the role of one in need of instruction, so that all may hear the lesson: speculation on the Self, even
the most exalted, does not give love for Krishna, while a word given to Hari becomes “the truth of truths.” Another
time, while glorifying Advaita, the Lord forbade all the devotees to speak of the doctrine of the Self; Murari said, “I
do not know it,” and the Lord, smiling, replied, “You do: you heard it from Kamalaksha,” that is, from Advaita
(“Kadacha” 2.6.23–26). <!-- F42 -->

## The Coming of Nityananda
When Nityananda came to Navadvipa, the Lord knew of it before anyone else: He dreamed of a great personality who had
arrived in the town. Returning to outer consciousness, He Himself, “as the friend of Rama,” explained the meaning of the
dream to the Vaishnavas: “I think that someone truly great has come to Navadvipa” (CB, Madhya 3.157–158)[^7].
According to the “Chaitanya-bhagavata,” the Lord sent Haridasa and Shrivasa to look for the guest (3.160). Murari,
however, relates that those sent were Rama, Murari himself, Narayana and Mukunda: in the daytime they came back
empty-handed, and in the evening the Lord, meeting Murari on the road, said to him: “Let us go where He is: the best of
sages has come to the house of Nandana Acharya,” and together with him and the other devotees He came to Nityananda
(“Kadacha” 2.8.3–9); Kavi Karnapura tells it the same way (“Chaitanya-charitamrita-mahakavya” 6.106–116). <!-- F64 -->

At their first meeting the two Lords, Gaura and Nityananda, began to glorify each other, and the Vaishnavas, listening
to Them, did not know what to say:

“Murari smiled and said: ‘Only You can understand each other. We have understood nothing.’” (CB, Madhya 4.57)
<!-- F65 Q25 -->

Soon the Lord sent Shrivasa, Rama, “Murari the physician” and Narayana to Shantipura to inform Advaita of the
Avadhuta's coming. Advaita, hearing the news, began to sing and dance; the messengers stayed with Him two days and
returned with Him to the Lord (“Kadacha” 2.9.13–16). In Lochana Dasa it is Shrinivasa, Narayana, Rama and Murari who
bring Nityananda to Advaita, and on their return Murari tells the Lord everything. <!-- F66 -->

## The Mahaprakasha: “I Am Ramachandra, and You Are Hanuman”
In Shrivasa's house the Lord manifested the Mahaprakasha, the great revelation: for twenty-one hours — seven
prahars — seated on Vishnu's throne, He revealed to each of the devotees His true nature and their eternal service (CB,
Madhya 9–10). Nityananda held an umbrella over His head, Gadadhara offered Him betel, and before Him stood great souls
headed by Advaita (10.5–6). And then the Lord called Murari:

“The Lord commanded Murari: ‘Behold My form!’ And Murari saw Mahaprabhu in the form of Ramachandra. Before his eyes
was Vishvambhara, seated like a mighty archer in the hero's posture (virasana). His body was dark, the colour of young
durva grass. To the Lord's left and right stood Sita and Lakshmana, and on all sides they were surrounded by the
leaders of the monkeys, offering prayers to the Lord.” (CB, Madhya 10.7–9)

Murari saw his chosen Deity, his Ramachandra — and among the monkeys glorifying Him with joined palms he saw himself as
well. This “best of physicians,” says Vrindavana Dasa, lost consciousness and fell at the Lord's feet (10.10–11).
<!-- F44 Q10 F1 -->

“Vishvambhara cried out: ‘O leader of the monkeys, have you forgotten how Ravana, who stole Sita, burned you? And you
set fire to his whole capital and subdued his whole race. Hear My words: “I am your Lord.” Get up, Murari. You are as
dear to Me as life itself. I am Ramachandra, and you are Hanuman. Look, here stands the son of Sumitra, your life and
soul. To bring Him back to life you carried a whole mountain, Gandhamadana. Bow down at the feet of Sita, whose
sufferings made you weep bitterly.’” (CB, Madhya 10.12–16)

This is no worldly “miracle” and no delusion: the Lord revealed to His servant his eternal form and eternal service, and
Murari recognized in Gaura the One whom he had always served. Coming to himself, he looked at the Lord again and wept
with love so that, in Vrindavana Dasa's words, even stones melted (10.17–18). <!-- F45 -->

The Lord said, “Ask of Me any blessing you wish.” And Murari asked:

“‘O Lord, I need nothing. Only let me always sing Your glory. O Lord, I may be born anywhere, but wherever I am born,
let me always remember You. Let me, life after life, keep company with Your servants. O Lord, do not let me fall into a
state where You cease to be my Master and I cease to be Your servant. Let me always be Your servant, wherever You come
with Your associates.’” (CB, Madhya 10.20–24)

Not the fulfilment of desires, not liberation does he ask for, but one thing alone: always to serve and always to be
born among devotees. “I grant you this blessing,” the Lord replied, and on all sides rose the cry: “Glory! Glory!”
(10.25). <!-- F46 -->

At that same time the Lord explained his name. Murari, “the Enemy of Mura,” is a name of Krishna; gupta means
“hidden”:

“‘Lord Murari dwells unseen (gupta) in his heart; therefore the name “Murari Gupta” suits him perfectly.’” (CB, Madhya
10.31)

And later Vrindavana Dasa, naming Murari among those who came to Puri, repeats: “Lord Gauranga secretly dwelt in his
body” (Antya 8.33). <!-- F2 Q2 F46 -->

Vrindavana Dasa adds: the Vaishnavas doted on Murari, who was kind to all living beings; wherever he appeared, the place
became holy like Vaikuntha; in every descent of the Lord he was His closest associate (CB, Madhya 10.26–28). And the
Lord said to those assembled: “Whoever speaks an ill word about Murari will not be saved even by a million baths in the
Ganga. Neither the holy Ganga nor the holy names of Hari will purify such a sinner” (10.29–30). Whoever hears of the
Lord's mercy to Murari will attain prema-bhakti (10.33). That night Murari and Shridhara stood before the Lord and wept
(10.34), and when the Lord bestowed His mercy on Haridasa, Haridasa, Murari and Shridhara wept (10.112). The mercy that
even the servants of Murari Gupta received, says Vrindavana Dasa, cannot be had by merely shaving one's head — by an
outward sannyasa alone (10.277). <!-- F47 F5 -->

Krishnadasa Kaviraja, in his account of the Mahaprakasha, recalls other words:

“The Lord said to Murari, ‘You have brought Krishna under your control,’ and hearing this, Murari began to recite a
verse: ‘Who am I, a poor and sinful man, and who is Krishna, the abode of the goddess of fortune? Yet because I was born
a brahmana, He embraced me in His arms!’ (Srimad-Bhagavatam, 10.81.16)” (CC, Adi 17.77–78)

These are the words of Sudama, Krishna's poor friend. Murari was not a brahmana by birth, and in repeating them he
speaks not of his social standing but of his unworthiness: so a Vaishnava's humility answers the Lord's praise.
<!-- F48 -->

Murari himself tells of this at greater length. Once the Lord said to the devotees: “When I leave My mother and go away
to distant lands, everyone will say of Me, ‘He did wrong.’” “No, Master, no one will say so,” Murari objected, and the
Lord, delighted, embraced him. Then Murari recited Sudama's verse: “Where am I — a pauper, the most sinful — and where is
Krishna, the abode of Shri! And yet me, a brahmana by birth alone, He embraced in His arms.” Hearing it, the Lord shone
like the thousand-rayed sun and said, “Know that this body is the highest concentrate of being and consciousness”;
Shrivasa bathed Him with the waters of the Ganga, Nityananda held an umbrella over Him, Gadadhara offered betel, and all
sang and danced (“Kadacha” 2.12.5–17). Lochana Dasa and Karnapura (“Chaitanya-charitamrita-mahakavya” 7.98–106) tell it
the same way, and in a song of Krishnadasa about this bathing “Mukunda and Murari sing.” <!-- F48 -->

## The Glory of Murari Gupta
Vrindavana Dasa devotes a whole chapter of the “Chaitanya-bhagavata” to “the glory of Murari Gupta” (Madhya 20).
Through Murari, the biographies say, the Lord taught the truth about Himself and about Nityananda, and how a devotee
ought to conduct himself. <!-- F49 I2 -->

### The Dream of the Elder Brother
One day, when Mahaprabhu was relishing bliss with Nityananda in Shrivasa's house, Murari came and bowed first to the
Lord and then to Nityananda. The Lord, very pleased with him, said plainly: “Murari, you have acted wrongly: you have
broken the proper order. You teach people right conduct — why do you break it yourself?” “Lord, how was I to know?”
Murari replied. “You Yourself moved me to act so.” “Very well,” said the Lord. “Go home. Tomorrow I will explain it to
you, and you will understand everything” (CB, Madhya 20.5–12). <!-- F49 -->

With joy and anxiety Murari returned home and lay down to sleep. And in a dream he saw:

“Nityananda held a plough and a club in His hands, and over His head swayed the hoods of a gigantic serpent. Nityananda
was exactly like Haladhara. Behind Him stood Vishvambhara, fanning Him with a whisk. The Lord smiled and said to Murari
in the dream: ‘Do you understand now, Murari? You should regard Me as the younger.’” (CB, Madhya 20.15–17)[^8]

Nityananda appeared to him as Balarama-Haladhara, “the bearer of the plough,” in the guise of a wrestler, while
Vishvambhara, the younger brother, served Him with a whisk. Murari woke in tears and began to call, “Nityananda!
Nityananda!” while his pious wife, frightened, cried out, “Krishna! Krishna! Krishna!” (20.19–20). <!-- F50 F14 -->

In the morning, having understood that Nityananda is the elder brother, Murari went to the Lord in bliss:

“Lotus-eyed Mahaprabhu sat on His seat, and to His right sat Nityananda, laughing. Murari first offered obeisances at
the lotus feet of Nityananda, and then bowed at the lotus feet of Vishvambhara.” (CB, Madhya 20.22–23)

“Why did you do that, Murari?” Vishvambhara asked with a smile. “Lord, I follow Your instructions,” Murari answered. “As
a blade of grass is moved by the breath of the wind, so all living beings obey Your power.” And the Lord, pleased that
Murari had learned the truth about Nityananda, said: “You are very dear to Me, Murari. That is why I have revealed this
secret truth to you” (20.24–26). From then on Murari always bowed first to Nityananda. <!-- F51 Q12 -->

Later, embracing Murari as a brother, the Lord said to him with great tenderness: “Murari, you are truly My faithful
servant, for you have understood the greatness and glory of Nityananda. As long as even a particle of envy toward
Nityananda remains in My servant, he cannot become dear to Me. <…> You have conquered Me, for you have realized the glory
of Nityananda” (CB, Madhya 20.48–51). “So Murari received the Lord's mercy which before only Hanuman had been granted,”
says Vrindavana Dasa (20.52). <!-- F51 -->

Srila Bhaktisiddhanta explains this lesson in the “Gaudiya-bhashya” (on CB, Madhya 20.9): the Lord tells Murari that he
is a devotee of Sri Balarama, and to worship the Supreme Lord without first bowing to the guru and jagad-guru is a
breach of order. Nityananda-Balarama is the original guru, and without His mercy service to Gaura cannot be attained.
<!-- F51 I2 -->

### Betel and the “Lost Caste”
That same day the Lord told Murari of His glory. Gadadhara, seated on His left, was offering Him betel.

“The Lord said, ‘Murari, you are the best of My servants.’ Saying this, the Lord gave Murari the remnants of His betel.
With deep reverence Murari accepted the betel with both hands. Tasting the Lord's remnants, Murari became intoxicated
with bliss. The Lord said, ‘Murari, wash your hands at once.’ But Murari simply wiped them on his head. The Lord said,
‘You have lost your caste, for you have defiled yourself by touching the remnants of My food.’” (CB, Madhya 20.28–31)

So the Lord joked about the greatness of His remnants: for the world they are an “impurity” that costs one his caste,
for the devotee a sacred thing to be placed on the head. The biographies say that the Lord, loving Murari dearly, more
than once gave him His betel. <!-- F52 Q13 -->

And at once the Lord entered the mood of the Supreme Master and, grinding His teeth, spoke again of the Mayavadis:

“‘In Kashi lives a sannyasi named Prakashananda. He takes pleasure in cutting Me to pieces. He teaches the “Vedanta,”
but denies My divine form. I have struck him with leprosy, yet he still has understood nothing. Countless universes are
manifest in My body. How then does this fool dare to claim that My body is illusory? Murari, I reveal the truth to you,
for you are My servant. Whoever denies My spiritual form is doomed.’” (CB, Madhya 20.33–36)

“I am eternal, My servants are eternal, and the servants of My servants are eternal too,” the Lord went on. “My
pastimes and deeds are eternal. My abode is eternal. One who says they are illusory cuts Me to pieces” (20.39–40). In
instructing Murari, the Lord was instructing everyone (20.45). <!-- F53 Q13 -->

### “Eat, Krishna!”
Returning home, Murari could not contain his merriment: he laughed at whatever he did. “I'll eat now,” he said, and his
chaste wife served him rice with ghee. But Murari, intoxicated with love for the Lord, began to throw the rice on the
floor by handfuls, crying, “Eat, Krishna! Eat!” His wife watched him and laughed — she knew her husband was a
maha-bhagavata — and kept serving him more, repeating the names of Krishna (CB, Madhya 20.54–60). <!-- F54 F14 -->

“The Lord ate everything Murari offered Him.” (CB, Madhya 20.61)

It is wondrous that the Lord, though not bodily present there, accepted this offering: however a devotee's offering is
made, He accepts it with joy. <!-- F54 I3 -->

Early in the morning the Lord came to Murari — as if for treatment. “What brings You here?” Murari asked. “I have come
to be treated.” “Where did You get indigestion? What did You eat yesterday?”

“The Lord said: ‘Dear friend, how can you not know? You threw rice on the floor and said, “Eat! Eat!” You have
forgotten, but your wife remembers it all. You offered Me that rice — how could I not eat it? How do you treat yourself,
and what medicines do you take? I ate your rice, and now I have indigestion. To get rid of indigestion one should drink
water. My stomach is upset because of your rice, and so your water will be the medicine.’” (CB, Madhya 20.66–69)

Having prescribed His own medicine, the Lord took Murari's water-pot and drank until He had drunk it all, filled with
bliss (20.70). Seeing such mercy, Murari fainted, and the whole of Gupta's family wept with love of God (20.71)[^9]. The
Lord's “indigestion” is no bodily ailment: He in whom the universes rest knows no disease. It is a loving pastime in
which the Lord shows that He ate all His devotee offered Him — ate “beyond measure,” because it was offered with all the
heart — and that the only medicine for Him is the water of His own physician. <!-- F55 -->

“The mercy the servants of Murari Gupta received was not seen even by the bhattacharyas of Navadvipa.” (CB, Madhya
20.73)

So, says Vrindavana Dasa, Mahaprabhu bestowed His mercy on Murari every day, and whoever hears these narratives
attains the treasure of loving service (20.76–77). <!-- F56 -->

### Garuda
One day in Shrivasa's house Mahaprabhu, with a loud cry, manifested His four-armed form — with conch, disc, club and
lotus — and began to call, “Garuda! Garuda!” At that moment Murari came in and, feeling a surge of bliss, cried out
loudly. He took on the mood of Garuda, the king of birds, the son of Vinata, and said, “I am Garuda, the great devotee
of God.” To Vishvambhara's call Murari answered, “O Lord, Your servant is here” (CB, Madhya 20.78–82).

“The Lord said, ‘My dear friend, you will carry Me.’ Murari Gupta replied, ‘Yes. Yes.’ Murari Gupta went on: ‘Perhaps
You have forgotten how I carried You and the parijata tree from heaven. Perhaps You have forgotten how I brought You to
Banapura, where I tore to pieces the peacock of Karttikeya. Lord, climb onto my shoulders and tell me to which universe
I should carry You.’” (CB, Madhya 20.83–86)

Vishvambhara climbed onto his shoulders, and Murari began to run about Shrivasa's courtyard carrying the Lord of
Lakshmi. The whole house filled with cries of “Jaya! Jaya!”, the women made auspicious sounds, and the devotees wept
with love; someone cried, “All glory to Vishvambhara, whom Murari carries!” Returning to outer consciousness, Gauranga
jumped down from his shoulders, and Murari ceased to feel himself Garuda (20.87–100). <!-- F57 -->

This is not possession and not a “miracle,” but the awakening of eternal service. Vrindavana Dasa says: “Murari Gupta
is the leader of the Lord's servants in all His descents” (20.98); his service bore fruit, for he carried Vishvambhara
in His pastimes (20.103). Hanuman, the servant of Rama, carried the Lord on his shoulders; in the mood of Garuda Murari
recalls how he carried Him with the parijata from heaven and how in Bana's city he tore apart the peacock of
Karttikeya. <!-- F57 F5 -->

### The Knife
The last story of this chapter is the most moving. Once Murari began to reflect on the Lord's pastimes: no one knows
what He will do the next moment. Rama for Sita's sake destroyed Ravana with all his race — and then abandoned Sita;
Krishna let the Yadavas, dear to Him as life, perish before His eyes. “While the Lord is here in this world with His
associates, I must leave my body,” Murari decided (CB, Madhya 20.105–112). In all His descents, he reasoned, the Lord
arranges that His associates depart before Him; and since Mahaprabhu must one day depart, it is better to go first: to
see that would be unbearable. He secretly obtained a sharp knife and hid it in the house, resolving: “Tonight I will
gladly leave this body” (20.112–113). <!-- F58 F59 -->

Vrindavana Dasa calls this state *nirveda* — “so Gupta, in nirveda, reflected.” It is not worldly despair or weariness
of life: so feels a servant who cannot conceive of life without his Lord and fears only one thing — to outlive
separation from Him. The strength of his love is seen in this, that for its sake he was ready to leave his body.
<!-- F58 I5 -->

The all-knowing Lord knew of his intention and came to him that very day. Murari prostrated himself before Him. The Lord
sat down and began to speak of Krishna, and then asked:

“‘Murari, will you do whatever I ask?’ Murari answered, ‘Lord, this body belongs to You.’ The Lord asked, ‘Is that
true?’ Murari answered, ‘Yes.’ Then the Lord whispered in his ear: ‘Then give Me the knife. You have hidden a knife in
the house with which you planned to kill yourself. Give it to Me.’” (CB, Madhya 20.116–119)

“Alas! Someone has told You an untruth,” Murari exclaimed in sorrow. “Murari, you are too simple,” the Lord replied.
“Do you think I know something only because someone told Me? I know who made this knife and where you hid it.” And the
Lord Himself took the knife from its hiding place (20.120–123). <!-- F60 -->

“The Lord asked: ‘Murari, what are you doing! For what fault of Mine do you want to leave Me? If you go, how will I
enjoy My pastimes? Who will give Me new ideas? Murari, give Me your word that you will never think of this again.’ <…>
‘If you ever again plan to leave your body, you will eat My head, Gupta. You will eat My head.’” (CB, Madhya
20.124–128)

“You will eat My head” is a Bengali oath: “if you do this, it will be as if you killed Me.” The Lord did not simply
forbid: He revealed that He Himself needs His servant, that without Murari His pastimes would be poorer. Murari fell to
the ground and bathed the Lord's feet with tears, and Shachinandana raised him, embraced him and Himself could not hold
back His tears (20.129–130). Both these stories — of Garuda and of the knife — are recalled also in the
“Bhakti-ratnakara” (the twelfth wave). <!-- F61 Q29 -->

“The mercy the Lord poured on Murari Gupta is desired even by Lakshmi, Brahma, Ananta and Shankara.” (CB, Madhya
20.131)

“Such is the glory of Murari Gupta,” Vrindavana Dasa concludes. “What more can I say? His greatness is known to all”
(20.155). <!-- F62 I5 -->

## In the Kirtans of Navadvipa
All the years the Lord spent in Navadvipa after Gaya, Murari was among those who night after night sang kirtan with Him
in Shrivasa's house (CB, Madhya 8.112–116). Nityananda, wandering through Navadvipa in a childlike mood, would drop in
on Gangadasa and on Murari (8.25), and the Lord would go into the houses of Gangadasa, Murari and Acharyaratna and
manifest there the forms of His avataras (8.84). <!-- F67 Q22 -->

In Murari's house the Lord also manifested the mood of Balarama. Murari himself relates that the Lord demanded honey
and drank water as honey, and flung a brahmana aside with one finger, saying, “This one is a wrestler!” (“Kadacha”
2.14.1–9); Kavi Karnapura (“Chaitanya-charitamrita-mahakavya” 8.17–36) and Lochana Dasa write of it too, and in
Karnapura's drama “Chaitanya-chandrodaya” the devotees in Murari's courtyard on a full-moon night smell the fragrance of
intoxicating kadambari and see the plough and the club (the prelude to the second act). Krishnadasa Kaviraja and
Vrindavana Dasa also tell of the mood of Balarama, but do not say that it was in Murari's house. <!-- F70 -->

When Jagai and Madhai had been delivered, Murari, together with other associates, sported with the Lord in the waters of
the Ganga (CB, Madhya 13.336–339); in Lochana Dasa it is Murari who, at the Lord's command, brings the repentant
brothers to Him. And when the Lord in a surge of emotion threw Himself into the Ganga and the devotees brought Him back,
all went to Murari's house, and from there to the abode of Vijaya (“Kadacha” 2.12.1; so too in Lochana). <!-- F69 -->

In the play the Lord staged in Chandrashekhara's house, Haridasa played the guard of Vaikuntha and, twirling his
moustache, “joined Murari Gupta. These two dear servants of Krishna were filled with love, for Gaurasundara was
enjoying His pastimes in their bodies” (CB, Madhya 18.48–49). When the Lord came out in the form of the Goddess, Murari,
by his own account, was commended to Her: “Goddess, look upon this wretched Murari!” (“Kadacha” 2.16.19), and, according
to Lochana, She looked on him with mercy. There is also a tradition that once Gaura seated Gadadhara at the foot of a
tree, Advaita performed arati, and Murari Gupta sang the Lord's glory. <!-- F71 -->

On the night of the great city kirtan, when the Lord went to subdue Chand Kazi, Murari, as the biographies remember, was
in the front ranks of the procession. The Kazi — then the Muslim ruler of Navadvipa — had broken a devotee's mridanga
and forbidden kirtan, and the Lord led the whole town to his house; He was accompanied by Gadadhara, Vakreshvara,
Murari, Shrivasa, Gopinatha and countless servants (CB, Madhya 23.150–152). In that procession there danced “such great
devotees as Murari, Mukunda Datta, Ramai, Govinda, Vakreshvara and Vasudeva” (23.209), and when that night the Lord
drank water from Shridhara's old pot, Murari wept with the others (23.450). <!-- F68 Q26 -->

Once, when Mahaprabhu sat surrounded by Shrivasa, Murari, Mukunda, Haridasa and others, young Lokanatha came to
Navadvipa; the Lord went out to him and embraced him: “Why have you forgotten Me so long?” (so too in the
“Prema-vilasa,” ch. 7). <!-- F72 -->

The poets remember Murari in other pastimes of Navadvipa too. Lochana Dasa tells how once the Lord vanished together
with Shrivasa, and Murari, Mukunda and Haridasa ascribed His return to Shrivasa's mercy. Jayananda places Murari among
the Lord's companions on the way to Gaya, which is not in Vrindavana Dasa. In songs ascribed to Narahari, Shrivasa and
Murari have a feast served on the bank of the Ganga; Murari dances with a carrying-pole at the Nanda-utsava, cries
“Jaya!” at Gauranga's arati, dances at Holi and sings in a kirtan party, and the elders of Navadvipa say that Shrivasa
and Murari cannot live without the Lord's darshan. <!-- F74 -->

## On the Eve of Sannyasa
Murari was one of the first to sense that the Lord was about to leave Navadvipa. Once, he relates, the Lord, bathed in
tears, said, “I can stay here no longer. I am going to the city of Mathura,” and in the anguish of separation from
Krishna tore His sacred thread. And the humble physician Murari answered Him:

“‘Bhagavan, You can do anything, You know all truths; You are free to go and free to stay. And yet, by the custom of the
noble, You must by no means go. If You, the Master, begin to act by Your own will, then all people too will act by
theirs — and fall into impurity. <…> Should You go today, it is the end of all embodied beings: they will be left
without Chaitanya, without consciousness.’” (“Kadacha” 2.17.14–17)

Hearing this, the Lord fell silent, overwhelmed with love (2.17.19). This sarga is called “The Admonition of Sri Murari
Gupta.” In Lochana Dasa the Lord, in the mood of Radha, pines: “Where is Vrindavana? Where is Lalita?” — and Murari
consoles Him and then says the same as in the “Kadacha”: if the Lord leaves, the Vaishnavas will become wilful; and all
sing kirtan (CM, Madhya-khanda, pt. 12, vv. 25–40). <!-- F75 -->

A few days later the Lord related that in a dream a certain brahmana had spoken the mantra of sannyasa into His ear,
and since then He had been weeping day and night. By its sound the sannyasa mantra speaks of identity with Brahman, and
Murari proposed to understand the “great saying” differently: “Understand it in your mind as a compound in the genitive
case — and be happy” (“Kadacha” 2.18.3–4), that is, not “I am He” but “I am His,” “you are His.” So too in Lochana Dasa
and Karnapura (“Chaitanya-charitamrita-mahakavya” 11.40–42). <!-- F76 -->

When it became clear that the sannyasa was near, Shrivasa and Murari came to the Lord, and He tried to console them. In
Lochana, Murari said with tears: “You planted a wondrous tree, raised it day and night, watered and guarded it — and
when the time of flowers and fruit has come, You cut it down” (CM, Madhya-khanda, pt. 12, vv. 110–113) — and yet he
submitted: “You are independent in everything; to teach the people, to show mercy, You take on the mood of the
torment of separation” (pt. 13, v. 151). <!-- F77 -->

On the evening before His departure the Lord came with Haridasa to Murari's house and gave him a secret instruction:

“‘The excellent Advaita Acharya is truly great: He is the abode of virtues, a part of the Lord. Serve Him diligently and
with reverence. So I have revealed this secret instruction to you for your happiness.’ Having said this, the Lord, who
loves His devotees, went home.” (“Kadacha” 2.18.29–30)

According to Lochana Dasa, this was in the Deity room of Murari's house, and Murari understood: the sannyasa was
inevitable (CM, Madhya-khanda, pt. 15, vv. 52–68); Karnapura too speaks of this instruction (11.47). At dawn the Lord
left His home (“Kadacha” 3.1.6). Jayananda adds that He left without telling either Shrivasa or Murari. <!-- F77 F18 -->

## Separation
When Mahaprabhu accepted sannyasa, Murari wept bitterly with all the rest:

“Falling to the ground in the Lord's courtyard, Mukunda, Murari, Shridhara, Gadadhara, Gangadasa, Shrivasa and his
family, Chandrashekhara and Haridasa wept without ceasing.” (CB, Madhya 28.85)

In Karnapura's drama it is Murari who guesses that since neither Nityananda nor Acharyaratna is there, they must be with
the Lord, and Mukunda goes to console Shachi with this (“Chaitanya-chandrodaya” 4.15–34). <!-- F78 -->

Soon the news came that the Lord was in Shantipura, in Advaita's house, and the devotees of Navadvipa hurried there.
The Lord met each one — Shrivasa, Ramai, Vidyanidhi, Gadadhara, Gangadasa, Vakreshvara, Murari, Shuklambara (CC, Madhya
3.137–167). In Lochana Dasa, Haridasa, Murari, Mukunda and Shrinivasa fall prostrate before Him and He embraces each,
and at parting Shrinivasa, Haridasa, Murari and Mukunda say to Him reproachfully: “You are the independent Lord, and we
are all dependent… You have taken sannyasa and now You are going away, abandoning all Your servants” (CM,
Madhya-khanda, pt. 19, vv. 19–21). A song signed “Murari” tells how the people of Nadia go to Shantipura, and “behind
them all walks Murari.” Lochana Dasa also names Murari among the Lord's companions on the way to Nilachala and writes
that he danced with the Lord in Jagannatha's temple (pt. 20, vv. 82–83 and after)[^10]. <!-- F79 -->

## The Servant of Rama
Through Murari the Lord taught one more thing — fidelity to one's chosen Deity, the ishta-deva. Murari, Hanuman in the
pastimes of Gaura, saw in Sri Chaitanya his own Ramachandra, and the Lord not only did not take this fidelity from him
but glorified it. <!-- F87 -->

Once, still in Navadvipa, the Lord asked Murari to recite “that poem of yours” — eight verses on Rama. Murari recited:

“His eyes are like lotuses awakened by the rays of the rising sun; His lips are like ripe bimba fruit, His nose is
lovely; His charming smile outshines the moonbeams. Rama, the teacher of the three worlds, I worship ever.” (“Kadacha”
2.7.11)

Having heard these eight verses, the Lord placed His foot on the head of Murari the physician and wrote on his
forehead: “You are Ramadasa,” that is, “servant of Rama,” and added: “Be so by My grace!” (“Kadacha” 2.7.9–18). Next to
this Murari tells how the Lord at that same time also explained the name of Shrivasa. Krishnadasa Kaviraja recalls it in
one verse:

“Hearing from Murari Gupta the glories of Rama, the Lord wrote the name ‘Ramadasa’ on his forehead.” (CC, Adi 17.69)

Karnapura tells it the same way (“Chaitanya-charitamrita-mahakavya” 6.98–101), while in Lochana Dasa Murari recites the
“Raghuvira-ashtaka,” and the Lord, revealing to him the form of Rama with Sita, tells him to relish also the rasa of
Radhanatha[^11]. <!-- F43 Q11 -->

Many years later, in Puri, the Lord, tenderly embracing Murari, told the devotees how He had tested his fidelity to Rama
(CC, Madhya 15.137). In the past, He said, He had again and again urged Murari to worship Krishna: “Gupta, the highest
sweetness is found in the son of Maharaja Nanda. Sri Krishna is the original source of all incarnations, the abode of
pure love and the reservoir of all rasas; nothing can so captivate and charm the mind as the worship of Krishna”
(15.138–142). Murari, hearing this, gradually yielded to His influence and promised: “I am Your servant, dependent on
You in all things. I will do whatever You tell me” (15.143–144). <!-- F88 -->

“He went home and thought about it all night. The very thought that he would have to give up the worship of
Ramachandra threw him into great turmoil. ‘If I ever resolve to give up the service of Ramachandra's lotus feet, then,
Lord, I pray, take my life that very night!’ So he wept all night, unable to sleep for the anxiety that seized him.”
(CC, Madhya 15.145–147)

In the morning Murari came to the Lord, fell at His feet and, sobbing, implored Him:

“‘I have sold my whole self to the feet of Raghunatha. The thought of leaving Him gives me unbearable pain, as if I were
trying to cut off my own head. I can neither give up the service of Sri Ramachandra's lotus feet nor disobey You. What
am I to do now? O merciful Master! Be merciful to me and let me die right now, and so put an end to all my doubts.’”
(CC, Madhya 15.149–151) <!-- F88 F89 Q15 -->

These words, the Lord said, filled His heart with great joy; He raised Murari and tenderly embraced him (15.152):

“I said to him: ‘O Gupta! How great you are! You are so deeply absorbed in the worship of your Lord that even My words
could not move you. Such is a servant's true love for the feet of the Lord who dwells eternally in his heart. Even if
the Lord should wish to abandon His servant, the servant will never give up the service of His feet. And to test your
faith in your Lord I again and again tried to change your worship of Him. You are the servant of Sri Ramachandra,
Hanuman himself. How could you give up the worship of His lotus feet?’” (CC, Madhya 15.153–156)

“Murari Gupta is as dear to Me as My own life,” the Lord concluded (15.157). <!-- F90 -->

This is neither stubbornness nor disobedience. The test was arranged by the Lord Himself to reveal to the world the
eternal nishtha of Hanuman, the eternal servant of Rama, and He Himself called it “a servant's true love.” Murari does
not dispute that Krishna is the source of all avataras: he promises to worship Krishna and only cannot tear himself
from Rama's feet. Rama and Krishna are not rivals: in Gaura Murari sees his own Ramachandra, and Advaita, having heard
his book, will say to him: “This Sri Rama has appeared in the world as Gaura” (“Kadacha” 4.26.26). Without special
devotion to one's ishta-deva one cannot advance in bhakti, and the Lord, who Himself had tested Murari, was pleased with
his steadfastness. <!-- F90 I4 -->

There is a verse by which, as tradition holds, Hanuman himself became famous:

“The Husband of Shri and the Husband of Janaki are one Supreme Soul, without difference; and yet my all is the
lotus-eyed Rama” (*shri-nathe janaki-nathe chabhede paramatmani / tathapi mama sarvasvo ramah kamala-lochanah*).
<!-- F91 Q16 -->

With the same test the Lord later tried Anupama, the younger brother of Rupa and Sanatana and father of Srila Jiva:
Anupama was likewise devoted to Rama and, however his brothers urged him, could not give up His worship. When Sanatana
told of this, the Lord remembered Murari:

“Sri Chaitanya replied: ‘Before this I tested Murari Gupta in the same way. Glorious is the devotee who under no
circumstances abandons the shelter of the Lord's lotus feet, and glorious is the Lord who never abandons a servant
surrendered to Him.’” (CC, Antya 4.45–46) <!-- F92 Q17 I4 -->

## Physician of Body and Soul
Murari was a physician by birth and by calling — and his healing too was service. Krishnadasa Kaviraja says:

“He accepted no gifts and took no one's money, but supported his family by his own calling. All whom he treated with
kindness were freed from both diseases — the disease of the body and the disease of worldly existence.” (CC, Adi
10.50–51)

He treated everyone who came to him — both body and soul. That is why Vrindavana Dasa calls him one who “could heal the
soul of the disease of worldliness” (CB, Adi 2.35) and “a mighty doctor who cured the disease of material life”
(Antya 8.33). A physician who takes neither fee nor gifts does not heal for gain: his medicine is mercy, and the true
healing he gives is release from the disease of worldly existence, forgetfulness of Krishna. It is no accident that the
Lord too, when He wished to show His love for him, came to him as a patient comes to a physician. <!-- F63 Q14 I6 F9 -->

    Shrihatta is named by Vrindavana Dasa (CB, Adi 2.35) and, following him, by the biographies.
