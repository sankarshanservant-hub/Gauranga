# Srila Bhaktisiddhanta Sarasvati Gosvami Thakura Prabhupada
*Srila Bhaktisiddhanta Sarasvati Gosvami Thakura Prabhupada · @bhaktisiddhanta*

## Srila Prabhupada
Srila Bhaktisiddhanta Sarasvati Gosvami Thakura was the son of Srila Bhaktivinoda Thakura, the only disciple of Srila
Gaurakishora Dasa Babaji, and the founder of the Sri Chaitanya Math in Mayapur and of the Gaudiya Maths throughout
India and beyond. His disciples called him Srila Prabhupada; his contemporaries called him Sarasvati Thakura and Srila
Siddhanta Sarasvati, and on solemn occasions “Om Vishnupada Srila Bhaktisiddhanta Sarasvati Gosvami Thakura” and “the
acharya of the Gaudiya Math.” When the Gaudiya Math announced his departure, it named him “Paramahamsa
Parivrajakacharya Om Vishnupada Srila Bhaktisiddhanta Sarasvati Gosvami Prabhupada.” As a child he was called
Bimalaprasad (or Vimalaprasad). At initiation his guru gave him the name Sri Varshabhanavi-dayita Dasa — “the servant
of one dear to the Daughter of Vrishabhanu”; after taking sannyasa in 1918 he was known as Parivrajakacharya Sri
Bhaktisiddhanta Sarasvati, yet even then he sometimes signed himself by his earlier name, Varshabhanavi-dayita Dasa.
<!-- F1 F22 -->

## Appearance in Puri
He appeared in Puri, Nilachala-dhama, on Friday, 6 February 1874, at half past three in the afternoon. By the Bengali
reckoning it was the 25th of Magha 1280 (year 1795 of the Shaka era), the krishna-panchami of the month of Magha; by
our calendar (Sri Chaitanya Saraswat Math) it is the krishna-panchami of Govinda-masa, the day on which his appearance
is celebrated. The house where he appeared was called “Narayana-chhata”: Bhaktivinoda Thakura lived there, and it
stood on the Grand Road (Bada-danda) not far from the temple of Jagannatha. The Holy Name resounded in it ceaselessly.
The child’s mother was Sri Bhagavati Devi; he was the fourth child of the family. <!-- F2 F3 -->

The newborn shone with a golden complexion, and his body bore all thirty-two marks of a great personality
(*mahapurusha*). Across his shoulder lay a natural sign like a sacred thread: the umbilical cord was wound over the
shoulder just as a brahmana’s thread lies, and those who saw it marvelled. <!-- F4 F5 -->

One of the biographies sees the meaning of his coming in the very place of his appearance. In Purushottama-kshetra
Mahaprabhu had left the treasure of prema; appearing there, Srila Bhaktisiddhanta was to carry that treasure to the
place where the Lord Himself appeared — Sri Mayapur — to establish it in the principal math, the Sri Chaitanya Math,
and in its branches, and to distribute it to all without distinction of birth or caste. So an eternal associate of the
Lord descends into the world not by the law of karma but for the work of his Master. <!-- F6 I1 -->

The name “Bimalaprasad” means “the grace of Vimala.” Vimala Devi is the internal energy (*para-shakti*) of Lord
Jagannatha, and the child’s first solid food (*anna-prashana*) was prasad[^1]. <!-- F7 -->

Six months later, during the Ratha-yatra, Jagannatha’s chariot stopped on the Grand Road — in front of Bhaktivinoda’s
house. At the Thakura’s word Bhagavati Devi carried the infant to the chariot; he stretched out his hands towards
Jagannatha’s feet, and the garland from the Lord’s neck fell upon his head. The panda priests foretold that the boy
would become a great personality[^2]. <!-- F8 -->

He spent the first ten months of his life in Purushottama-dhama and then travelled overland with his mother, in a
palanquin, to Ranaghat in Bengal. His whole childhood passed amid a ceaseless festival of harinama-sankirtana. <!-- F9 -->

## Childhood in Bhaktivinoda’s Home
From his earliest years he listened to the “Bhagavata” from his father’s lips, attentively and with deep absorption.
Bhaktivinoda also read to him the “Chaitanya-shikshamrita,” which he was then writing. And when the family lived in
Shrirampur (Serampore), the Thakura, returning from Puri, gave his son — then in the seventh class — a tulasi mala
that had been worn by Jagannatha and initiated him into chanting the Holy Name and into the mantra of Nrisimha.
<!-- F10 F13 F11 -->

In 1881 Bhaktivinoda began building his house “Bhakti-bhavan” at Rambagan in Calcutta. While the foundations were being
dug, a small Deity of Kurma was found in the earth. The Thakura taught his seven-year-old son the rules of worship and
the mantra of Kurma, and from then on Bimalaprasad always wore tilaka and performed puja. In 1885 his father founded
the “Vaishnava Depository” press in Bhakti-bhavan, and in the same year his monthly “Sajjana-toshani” began to appear
again. The boy learned the printer’s craft, read proofs, and helped in every other way. <!-- F14 F15 -->

With his father he visited the shripatas of Mahaprabhu’s associates — Kulinagrama, Saptagrama and others — and there
heard the Thakura speak on the doctrine of the Holy Name. In that same year 1885, the 399th year since Mahaprabhu’s
appearance, Bhaktivinoda founded the “Vishva-vaishnava-sabha,” the World Vaishnava Association, in the house of
Ramagopal Basu on Krishna Singh Lane (now Bethune Row); the following year it arranged the celebration of the four
hundredth anniversary of the Lord’s appearance. Its members included Madangopal Gosvami, Nilakantha Gosvami, Bipin
Bihari Gosvami, Radhika Nath Gosvami and Shishir Kumar Ghosh. The boy went with his father to its weekly Sunday
meetings, carried his “Bhakti-rasamrita-sindhu” for him, and listened attentively. <!-- F16 F23 -->

He studied at the Shrirampur school, and while still in the fifth class he invented a simple shorthand, which he called
“Bicanto.” At home he did not go over his lessons but read Narottama Thakura’s “Prarthana” and “Prema-bhakti-chandrika,”
his father’s books and various philosophical shastras; yet he always answered his teachers’ questions at once and
correctly. In the upper classes he gave more time to astronomy (*jyotisha*) and the Vaishnava scriptures than to the
syllabus. His knowledge astonished his teachers of mathematics, astronomy and philosophy, and whoever argued with him
was defeated. He kept away from ill-behaved boys: the wish to avoid unholy company (*asat-sanga*) and to seek only the
company of saints is visible in him from his earliest years. <!-- F12 F17 F18 F19 -->

His gift for mathematics and jyotisha showed itself in the fifth class. He studied under Pandita Maheshachandra
Chudamani of Siyakhala (on the Tarakeshvar line), quickly mastered the casting of horoscopes, and later studied with
Sundar Lal Pandita of Aloya. Chudamani never ceased to wonder at his fifteen-year-old pupil. Thus the youth came to bear
the title “Siddhanta Sarasvati”[^3]. “Our Teachers” adds that by the age of seven he knew the whole “Bhagavad-gita” by
heart. <!-- F20 F21 F268 -->

## College and Tripura
While still a schoolboy he published the “Surya-siddhanta,” the “Bhakti-bhavana-panjika” and other books on astronomy.
In the afternoons he would go to Beadon Square in Calcutta to debate with students on religion, politics and much else;
in 1891 their circle took the name “The August Assembly,” and all its members vowed not to marry. People of every age
attended, including educated and respected men. <!-- F24 F25 -->

Having passed the entrance examination, in 1892 he entered the Sanskrit College in Calcutta. Instead of the syllabus he
systematically read every book in the library, and he studied the Vedas under Pandita Prithvidhara Sharma. At the
college he openly differed with the famous scholar of Kashi, Bapudeva Shastri, and with Professor Panchanana
Sahityacharya. He soon left the college, and later explained why in his autobiographical notes: had he gone on to
university, his relatives would certainly have married him off; but if he showed the world a “useless fool,” no one
would push him into such a life. He looked for an honest living that would demand the least labour, so that he could
live in devotion to the Lord. One of the biographies compares this with Mahaprabhu’s life: first the joys of learning
and debate, then only the chanting and preaching of the Name. <!-- F26 F28 F29 I2 -->

In 1895 he entered the service of the royal house of independent Tripura as assistant editor of the dynastic chronicle
“Raja-ratnakara,” and studied the books of the royal library. Already in July 1896 Bhaktivinoda had taken him to
Agartala to the Raja of Tripura, Birchandra Devavarma Manikya, and in the same year to Kurseong in the Himalayan
foothills. After Maharaja Virachandra’s death (11 December 1896) the new maharaja, Radha Kishor Manikya Bahadur,
entrusted him with teaching Sanskrit and Bengali to the heir, Vrajendra Kishor, and later sent him to Calcutta on
various commissions. When in 1905 he wished to leave service altogether, the maharaja granted him a pension equal to his
salary; he received it until 1908. <!-- F30 F31 F244 -->

## The Sarasvata Chatushpathi
In 1897 a Sanskrit school, the Sarasvata Chatushpathi, opened in Bhakti-bhavan. Many educated men came there to study
astronomy: Lala Hara Gauri Shankar, Dr. Ekendranath Ghosh, Satkori Chattopadhyaya Siddhanta-bhushana, Shyamlal
Gosvami — a scholar of Nityananda’s family — Sharat Chandra Jyotir-vinoda and others. From it appeared the monthlies
“Jyotirvid” and “Brihaspati” and ancient works on astronomy. In 1898, while teaching Sanskrit in this school, he went
through the whole “Siddhanta-kaumudi” with the same Prithvidhara Sharma — quickly, from beginning to end. He was
advised to devote his life to grammar; he replied that human life is meant for the worship of the Supreme, not for
grammars with their roots and conjugations, nor for mundane poetry. <!-- F32 F27 -->

In Bhakti-bhavan Srila Jagannatha Dasa Babaji saw him and showered boundless love upon him. Learning of his knowledge
of astronomy, the Babaji told him to compile a Vaishnava calendar — and so the “Navadvipa-panjika” began[^4]. <!-- F247 -->

From 1897 to the end of his life he kept chaturmasya according to the Vaishnava shastras: he cooked his own *havishyanna*
— food without salt or spices — ate straight from the floor without a plate, and slept on the ground without a pillow.
<!-- F35 -->

## Pilgrimages and the Schools of the Acharyas
In October 1898 he followed his father on pilgrimage to Kashi and Prayaga, and on the way back to Gaya: one of the
biographies remarks that pilgrimage without following a Vaishnava brings only fatigue. In Kashi he discussed the
Ramanuja-sampradaya with Rama Mishra Shastri; at that time his spirit of renunciation clearly showed itself. What he
gathered in these places later went into his “Anubhashya” on the “Sri Chaitanya-charitamrita.” <!-- F33 F34 -->

He had begun studying Ramanuja’s school while still in Bengal. Earlier Sundareshvar Shrauti had brought him books in four
South Indian languages, and from them he learned the teachings of Ramanuja and Madhva; he published the fruits of his
study as books and articles, and in 1898 printed in the “Sajjana-toshani” a series of articles on Shrinatha Muni,
Yamunacharya and other acharyas of the Shri-sampradaya. In 1899 he wrote for the weekly “Nivedana,” and in 1900 he
published the book “Bange Samajikata” (“Social Customs in Bengal”), the fruit of extensive research. <!-- F36 F37 -->

## Gaurakishora’s Only Disciple
In 1897 Bhaktivinoda made himself a place for bhajana in Godrumadvipa of Navadvipa, on the bank of the Sarasvati river
— the Svananda-sukhada-kunja. Here in 1898 Sarasvati Thakura first saw Srila Gaurakishora Dasa Babaji Maharaja — a
paramahamsa indifferent to all worldly conventions — and was drawn to him with all his heart. He was captivated by the
Babaji’s heartfelt kirtana; one of his songs — on Raghunatha Dasa’s separation from Radha, “*kothay go premamayi radhe
radhe*” — he wrote down and later taught to his disciples. “Our Teachers” relates that at their very first meeting the
Babaji gave the young man his beads — knotted cords — a wooden tilaka stamp, and a cap and basket for worship which he
himself had received from his guru, Bhagavata Dasa Babaji. <!-- F38 F240 F269 -->

Gaurakishora had vowed not to accept disciples. Sarasvati Thakura asked for initiation more than once; the first time
the Babaji answered, “If Mahaprabhu tells me to, I will give it.” But seeing the young man’s determination and devotion,
he changed his mind. In the month of Magha 1900, with Bhaktivinoda’s permission — according to one of the biographies,
at his instruction — Sarasvati Thakura received *bhagavati-diksha* from Srila Gaurakishora Dasa Babaji and the name Sri
Varshabhanavi-dayita Dasa. He became Babaji Maharaja’s only disciple. <!-- F239 F39 -->

## Puri: Bhakti-kuti and the “Vaishnava-manjusha”
In March 1900 he accompanied his father to Baleshvar and Remuna, where they had darshana of Kshirachora Gopinatha, then
to Bhubaneshvar, Sakshi-Gopala and Puri; at that time his attachment to Puri grew strong. He wished to found a math by
the samadhi of Haridasa Thakura. The sub-registrar Jagabandhu Pattanayak and other leading citizens asked him to take
charge of the service of one of the seven thrones of the Satasan Math — the Deity Giridhari. And in 1902 Bhaktivinoda
himself began building a house near Haridasa’s samadhi and named it “Bhakti-kuti.” <!-- F40 F41 -->

Here Sarasvati Thakura constantly read and explained the “Chaitanya-charitamrita” in his father’s presence — especially
in 1903. Nearby, in a tent on the seashore, lived the Maharaja of Cossimbazar, Manindra Chandra Nandi Bahadur, grieving
the loss of one dear to him, and he often came to hear Bhaktivinoda and his son. The famous Radharaman Charan Dasa Babaji
also visited the Thakura; Sarasvati Thakura, on the basis of the shastras, showed that this babaji’s conduct and teaching
departed from the genuine tradition. <!-- F42 F244 -->

In Puri he gathered material for the “Vaishnava-manjusha.” He conversed with the abbot of the Govardhana Math,
Madhusudana Tirtha, who held him in high esteem, and discussed the shastras with Vasudeva Ramanuja Dasa and Damodara
Ramanuja Dasa (Samadhi Math), Raghunandana Ramanuja Dasa (Emar Math), Jagannatha Dasa (Papudiya Math, of the Jamayat
Ramanandi sampradaya), Omkara Japi Vriddha Tapas (Svargadvara-chhata), the scholar Sadashiva Mishra, the advocate
Harishchandra Basu, Bihari Dasa Pujari (the math of Gangamata), the abbot of the Radhakanta Math Narottama Dasa,
Ananta Charan Mahanti and many others. <!-- F43 -->

The “Vaishnava-manjusha” was conceived as a Vaishnava encyclopaedia. Bhaktivinoda and Shishir Kumar Ghosh asked him to
undertake it, and for its sake he travelled all over India. It was to contain explanations of Vaishnava terms, short
lives of the mahajanas, descriptions of the acharyas’ works and of Vaishnava tirthas, the doctrines of the various
sampradayas, and the technical, philosophical and difficult words of Vaishnava books — with sources, sense and
significance. The late Sri Haridasa Dasa Babaji of the “Haribol Kutir” in Navadvipa warmly praised the preparation of
this material, and afterwards himself compiled and published the “Gaudiya-vaishnava-abhidhana.” <!-- F44 -->

Preaching from house to house among the leading people of Puri, Sarasvati Thakura met with many difficulties and even
dangers; nor was his service of Giridhari at the Satasan Math easy. Like Prahlada he stood firm and paid no heed to
abuse. Then Bhaktivinoda advised him to go to Mayapur and perform bhajana there in seclusion — as Ramanuja once withdrew
to Tirunarayanapuram. <!-- F45 -->

## Mayapur and the Vow of the Name
In Navadvipa Bhaktivinoda introduced him to Vamshidasa Babaji. Sarasvati Thakura was drawn by this babaji’s Vaishnava
conduct and his indifference to worldly rules; he himself would bow to him from a distance, but he did not allow his
disciples to associate with him: for a beginner, imitating a paramahamsa is dangerous. Soon Charan Dasa Babaji, with
Vishnu Dasa of Kalna and many others, went with Sarasvati Thakura in kirtana to Mayapur, the place of Mahaprabhu’s
appearance, for the festival of Dola-purnima. The following year Charan Dasa Babaji announced to Bhaktivinoda that he
wished to lead a kirtana party around Navadvipa-dhama every year, but in 1906 he left this world before he could do so[^5].
<!-- F46 F249 -->

His learning had not left him. On 2 January 1902 he debated astronomy with a gifted pupil of Bapudeva Shastri, under the
chairmanship of Rai Bahadur Rajendra Chandra Shastri, president of the “Royal Society.” Sarasvati Thakura so routed his
opponent that the opponent’s teacher, overcome by shock, lost control of himself in the very assembly. <!-- F47 -->

In January 1904 he visited Sita-kunda and Chandranatha, in December Puri, and on 23 February 1905 he set out on pilgrimage
to the South: Simhachalam, Rajahmundry, Madras, Perumbudur, Tirupati, Kanchivaram, Kumbakonam, Shrirangam, Madurai and
other places; then he returned to Calcutta and Mayapur. In Perumbudur a tridandi of the Ramanuja-sampradaya explained to
him the rules of Vaishnava tridandi sannyasa. <!-- F48 -->

Having settled in Sri Mayapur and begun to preach, in 1905, following in the footsteps of Haridasa Thakura, he vowed to
chant a hundred crores of Holy Names — no fewer than three lakhs a day, that is, a hundred and ninety-two rounds on his
beads — and he fulfilled the vow[^6]. <!-- F49 F270 -->

In 1906 his first disciple came to him: Rohini Kumar Ghosh, a nephew of Judge Chandra Madhava Ghosh, had a wondrous dream
telling him to go to Sarasvati Thakura for initiation. <!-- F50 -->

In 1909, on the site of Chandrashekhara Acharya’s house, he built a hut called “Vraja-pattana” and continued his vow
there, contemplating himself on the bank of Radha-kunda. <!-- F51 -->

## Vaishnava and Brahmana
Three years before Bhaktivinoda’s departure, Sarasvati Thakura made a vow to his father: unworthy servant though he was,
he would take up the highest good — the overthrow of every teaching opposed to pure bhakti. The Thakura was delighted.
The occasion soon came. Descendants of Bengali acharyas, holding to smarta caste views, were humiliating and oppressing
Vaishnavas; the smarta brahmanas were jointly attacking the Vaishnava faith and its acharyas, and some of those who
called themselves descendants of Nityananda and other associates of Mahaprabhu had taken their side for gain. In 1911,
on 8–11 September, a great assembly of hundreds of scholars was convened at Balighai (Medinipur district); it was
chaired by Pandita Vishvambharananda Deva Gosvami of Gopivallabhapura, and Sri Madhusudana Gosvami Sarvabhauma of the
Radharamana enclosure in Vrindavana was also present. Bhaktivinoda was invited, but he was confined to bed and sent his
son in his place, together with Suresh Chandra Mukhopadhyaya. <!-- F245 F52 -->

First the smarta pandits spoke, each proving the superiority of his own view. Then Madhusudana Gosvami gave the floor to
Sarasvati Thakura. He read a paper and, in a long address that was the fruit of great research, set out the nature of
the brahmana and the Vaishnava and the difference between them, demolishing the arguments of the smarta karmis. The
assembly was enchanted and struck dumb; the smarta descendants of acharyas and the brahmanas were amazed, and all
acknowledged that the glory of the Vaishnava stands above that of the brahmana. The address later appeared as a book,
“Brahmana o Vaishnava.” A year later Madhusudana Gosvami, visiting Bhaktivinoda at Bhakti-bhavan, enthusiastically
declared his son a worthy successor to his father in defending the Gaudiya sampradaya. Sarasvati Thakura was defending
not one caste against another but the truth of the shastras: a devotee of the Lord, whatever his birth, stands above one
who is a brahmana by birth alone. <!-- F52 F245 -->

In that same year, 1911, a dispute arose at Bara-akhra in Navadvipa over the Gaura-mantra. On the basis of the
“Chaitanyopanishad” of the Atharva-veda and other shastras, Sarasvati Thakura proved the eternal validity of a distinct
mantra for the worship of Gauranga. <!-- F53 -->

## Against Imitation Devotion
He stood no less firmly against the *prakrita-sahajiyas* — those who pass off mundane feelings as the highest love of
God. “Our Teachers” relates that once a well-known sahajiya babaji came to Bhaktivinoda; the son came in, saw the guest,
bowed to his father from a distance and left, and the Thakura explained to the puzzled guest that the young man had
vowed never to come within a hundred paces of a sahajiya. Against their errors he wrote the poem
“Prakrita-rasa-shata-dushini.” He expressed his creed in a couplet that is now inscribed over the entrance to the
nat-mandira of the Sri Chaitanya Saraswat Math in Navadvipa:

*matala hari-jana kirttana-range*
*pujala raga-patha gaurava-bhange*

“We shall worship the path of raga-marga, of exclusive and selfless devotion to God, from afar, with the deepest
reverence, always performing kirtana in the company of true Vaishnavas”[^7]. <!-- F273 -->

On 23 March 1912, at a religious conference in Cossimbazar, in the palace of Maharaja Manindra Chandra Nandi, he spoke
for four days on shuddha-bhakti — on the marks of pure devotion and against the worldly pretensions and sycophancy of
the “leaders” of Gaudiya Vaishnavism of the day. Seeing around him so-called prakrita-sahajiyas with their show of
feeling, he ate nothing for all four days, and only on returning to Mayapur did he again take prasad. The maharaja,
learning of this, came to Mayapur, expressed his sorrow and long begged forgiveness. Sarasvati Thakura said: “Having
judged that there was no bhakti there, one should not eat in a common place”[^8]. <!-- F54 -->

On 4 November 1912, with a small party of devotees, he set out for the places of Mahaprabhu’s associates — Shrikhanda,
Yajigrama, Katwa, Jhamatpur, Akai-hat, Chakhandi, Daihat and others — preaching pure devotion everywhere. <!-- F55 -->

## The “Anubhashya”
In 1913 he began writing a commentary on the “Chaitanya-charitamrita,” the “Anubhashya,” conceived as a companion to
Bhaktivinoda’s “Amrita-pravaha-bhashya,” and read passages of it to his father. In April 1913 he founded the
“Bhagavata Yantralaya” press; its first book was the “Chaitanya-charitamrita” with the “Anubhashya,” followed by the
“Bhagavad-gita” with Vishvanatha Chakravarti’s commentary and the mahakavya “Gaura-krishnodaya” of the Oriya poet
Govinda Deva of Vakreshvara’s line[^9]. <!-- F246 F56 F257 -->

Bhaktivinoda Thakura left this world on 23 June 1914, and his son became editor of the monthly “Sajjana-toshani” that
he had founded. In January 1915 the press was moved to Vraja-pattana in Mayapur; in March the journal appeared again;
on 14 June the “Anubhashya” was completed there; and in July the press moved to Krishnanagar, from where the journal and
Bhaktivinoda’s books were issued. <!-- F57 -->

He wrote at that time in the “Sajjana-toshani” that the journal’s aim was unchanged: the Thakura had entered the
eternal pastimes (*nitya-lila*), but the journal would serve as before. Some, he wrote, set aside pure devotion for the
sake of a supposed bhakti; others take as their standard the easy path of mundane “devotees.” And he quoted
Bhaktivinoda’s lines from the “Kalyana-kalpataru” beginning *bhakti-badha jaha haite*: on the head of learning that
obstructs bhakti — a kick without guile; Sarasvati, dear to Krishna, is full of devotion to Krishna — and that is
Vinoda’s wealth. The verse rejects not learning as such but only the knowledge that eclipses bhakti. “Service to Gaura’s
teaching in following the guru is the same as service to Radha-Govinda… following Sri Rupa Manjari,” he wrote; “be a
servant of the followers of Svarupa and Rupa and always sing the Name.” <!-- F238 Q6 -->

## The Guru’s Departure
On 17 November 1915, on utthana-ekadashi, Srila Gaurakishora Dasa Babaji entered the eternal pastimes at Kulia, in the
Rani Dharmashala. Overwhelmed by separation, Sarasvati Thakura hurried there. The mahantas of various akharas were
disputing where and how to give the body samadhi: each wanted the future temple, and the income that would come with
it. He alone stood against them. The police chief of Navadvipa, Jatindranath Singh Rai Bahadur, arrived. The babajis
argued that he was not yet a sannyasi and had no right to perform the rite. Sarasvati Thakura replied that he was
Babaji Maharaja’s only disciple, and said: let him touch the body who among them had been pure with regard to women for
even the last six months, three months, a month or three days — for one who was impure that touch would be ruinous. On
the 1st of Agrahayana he performed the rite according to Vaishnava smriti, the “Samskara-dipika” of Gopala Bhatta
Gosvami, and established his guru’s samadhi at Nutan-chora in old Kulia (now the town of Navadvipa). His sternness
was the protection of his guru’s sanctity, not enmity towards people. <!-- F58 F241 -->

After the departure of his father and his guru, a deep despondency came over him: how could he fulfil their command to
preach when spiritual life around him had fallen so low? “Our Teachers” relates that one night Sri Gaurasundara
appeared to him in a sankirtana procession from the Yogapitha, with the six Gosvamis, Jagannatha Dasa Babaji,
Bhaktivinoda and Gaurakishora; they told him to abandon his doubts and preach, promising their constant support. This
despondency, “Our Teachers” says, was not weakness but part of an inconceivable lila. <!-- F271 -->

## Sannyasa
In 1918, on the day of Mahaprabhu’s appearance, Gaura-purnima, he took tridandi sannyasa at Vraja-pattana in Sri
Mayapur[^10]. “Our Teachers” describes the day: having bathed in the Ganga and put on saffron cloth, he bowed before
the pictures of his two gurus — Bhaktivinoda and Gaurakishora — and took the vows of the tridanda: to dedicate body,
mind and speech to the Lord’s service. He took the principle of tridandi sannyasa from the “Bhagavatam” (11.23.57) and
from the example of the Ramanuja-sampradaya, whose rules he had once learned in Perumbudur. From that day he was known
as Srila Bhaktisiddhanta Sarasvati. <!-- F59 F272 -->

To one eternally perfect (*nitya-siddha*) renunciation was not needed: its goal he already possessed. But he accepted
sannyasa in order to preach the Lord’s teaching everywhere without hindrance, to establish *daiva-varnashrama* — an
order of life whose centre is service to the Lord — and to show the transcendental nature of the paramahamsa-babaji dress
worn by his preceptors: Jagannatha Dasa, Sachchidananda Bhaktivinoda Thakura and Gaurakishora Dasa Babaji. Having
accepted sannyasa himself, he then introduced it among his disciples, and was the first in the Gaudiya Vaishnava
sampradaya to do so[^11]; and from that day the mission went swiftly forward. <!-- F60 I3 -->

On the same day he installed the Deities of Guru-Gauranga and Radha-Govinda in the house of Chandrashekhara Acharya[^12]
and founded the Sri Chaitanya Math — the principal math of all the Gaudiya Maths in the world and the head office of the
mission; the Sri Chaitanya Math still stands on the site of Chandrashekhara’s house. <!-- F61 F256 -->

## A Sannyasi’s First Journeys
At the end of March 1918 he gave a full lecture on Vaishnava philosophy in the Krishnanagar town hall, and in May he
spoke hari-katha at Daulatpur and other places. On 2 June he set out for Puri with devotees from Calcutta, preaching on
the way — at Sauri, Kuamara and elsewhere. Walking Mahaprabhu’s road, he was overcome by separation from the Lord. At
Remuna he bowed to Gopinatha; at Baleshvar he lectured on the “Shikshashtaka” at the “Hari-bhakti-pradayini Sabha,”
where the sub-divisional magistrate Rai Saheb Gaura Shyama Mahanti and others received him; in Cuttack he was the guest
of Dewan Bahadur Sri Krishna Mahapatra and expounded the “Bhagavata.” In Puri they stayed at Bhakti-kuti and walked
around Sri Kshetra-mandala, and all the while he remained in the mood of separation. <!-- F62 F63 -->

In Puri the former collector and deputy magistrate Atal Bihari Maitra heard his explanations of the
“Chaitanya-charitamrita” and the “Bhagavata.” At a large gathering in the courtyard of the house of Hariballabha Basu Rai
Bahadur (“Shashi-bhavana”) he lectured on the difference between the personal and impersonal understanding of the
Absolute Truth. At that time he also composed several Sanskrit verses on the memorial of Mahaprabhu’s footprints in the
temple of Jagannatha. <!-- F64 -->

In August–September 1918 the spokesman of a circle of atheists sent him twenty-nine questions against the Vaishnava
faith and its acharyas. He answered with reason and shastra; the questions and answers appeared as a booklet,
“Pratipera Prashnera Pratyuttara” (“Reply to an Opponent’s Questions”). <!-- F65 -->
