# Lakshmipriya Devi
*Лакшмиприя-деви · @lakshmipriya*

## The Lord's First Consort
Lakshmipriya Devi is the first consort of Sri Chaitanya Mahaprabhu, the Lakshmi of His youthful pastimes in Navadvipa.
The original sources most often call her simply Lakshmi: so does the “Chaitanya-bhagavata”; in Krishnadasa Kaviraja she is
also Lakshmidevi (CC, Adi 14.62; 15.29); in the “Gaura-ganoddesha-dipika” she is “the daughter named Lakshmi,” and in
Lochana Dasa, Lakshmi Thakurani. The name “Lakshmipriya Devi” appears in the “Anubhashya” of Srila Bhaktisiddhanta
Sarasvati (on CC, Adi 14.62–68). The biographies also honour her as Sri Lakshmi, Lakshmidevi, Mahalakshmi, Maheshvari and
Ishvari, “the Mistress,” and call her *sati-sadhvi*, a faithful and chaste wife.[^1] <!-- F1 -->

Her pastimes in this world are brief: the meeting with the Lord by the Ganga, the matchmaking, the wedding, a short time
of service in Shachi's house — and her departure while the Lord was travelling in East Bengal. Her departure and the
Lord's second marriage Vrindavana Dasa names already in the list of pastimes with which the “Chaitanya-bhagavata” opens:

“In the Adi-khanda the Lord's consort (Lakshmipriya) leaves the world, and the Lord marries a second time — the daughter
of the Raja Pandita (Vishnupriya).” (CB, Adi 1.110) <!-- F47 Q3 -->

Of her life in this world little is known. The sources name neither her mother nor the year and day of her appearance, nor the
year and day of her departure; nor are these days in the Vaishnava calendar of the Sri Chaitanya Saraswat Math.
<!-- F10 -->

## Vallabhacharya's Daughter
Her father, the brahmana Vallabhacharya, lived in Navadvipa. Srila Bhaktisiddhanta calls him a royal pandita of
Navadvipa, and Vrindavana Dasa says he was “in every way equal to Maharaja Janaka” (CB, Adi 10.47). Kavi Karnapura reveals
who he is in the eternal pastimes:

“He who was once the great king Janaka, lord of Mithila, is now Vallabhacharya; he is also accepted as Bhishmaka.”
(“Gaura-ganoddesha-dipika” 44)

Thus the father of Sita and the father of Rukmini were united in Lakshmi's one father. <!-- F8 -->

His daughter, says Vrindavana Dasa, seemed to be Lakshmi herself in visible form, and Vallabhacharya thought constantly of
where to find her a worthy husband, for she had reached the age of marriage (CB, Adi 10.48). Who she is, the next verse of
the “Gaura-ganoddesha-dipika” tells:

“Sri Janaki and Rukmini are his daughter named Lakshmi.” (“Gaura-ganoddesha-dipika” 45)

Karnapura supports this with a verse from Murari Gupta's “Chaitanya-charita”: Vallabhacharya's daughter Lakshmi, who had
descended for the Lord's sake, was going with her friends to bathe in the Ganga and came into His sight
(“Gaura-ganoddesha-dipika” 46). Srila Bhaktisiddhanta also cites both verses in the “Anubhashya.”[^2] <!-- F2 F3 F9 Q2 -->

Vallabhacharya and his daughter are praised in the “Vaishnava-vandana” too:

“I praise Vallabha Acharya, whose daughter Lakshmi captivates the heart: rich in virtues, he gave her to the son of
Shachi, the bridegroom.” (“Vaishnava-vandana” 53) <!-- F50 -->

## The Meeting by the Ganga
Vrindavana Dasa relates that one day Lakshmi came to the Ganga to bathe, and by providence Gaurasundara came there to bathe
at the same time. The Lord recognised in her His own Lakshmi and smiled, and she bowed in her heart to His lotus feet.
Having thus recognised each other, they went home:

“Who can understand the pastimes of Lord Gaurasundara?” (CB, Adi 10.51) <!-- F11 -->

Krishnadasa Kaviraja places their first meeting still earlier, among Nimai's childhood pastimes. One day the girl Lakshmi,
having bathed in the Ganga, came to worship the gods; seeing her, the Lord rejoiced at heart, and seeing the Lord, Lakshmi
too felt bliss:

“A natural love awoke in them both, though the mood of childhood still veiled its true form. Seeing each other, both
rejoiced at heart, and on the pretext of worshipping the gods they let their feelings show.” (CC, Adi 14.64–65)

Nimai told her to worship Him Himself, for He is the Supreme Lord; Lakshmi offered Him flowers and sandal paste, garlanded
Him and bowed before Him, and the Lord, smiling, accepted her worship with a verse of the “Bhagavatam”: “O chaste ones, I
know your resolve to worship Me. I accept it, and it deserves to come true” (SB 10.22.25).

“After this pastime the two of them went home. Who can fathom the deep pastimes of Chaitanya?” (CC, Adi 14.70)

Srila Bhaktivinoda Thakura explains in the “Amrita-pravaha-bhashya”: Lakshmi is the Lord's eternal consort and the Lord
her eternal husband, and so their love is *sahaja*, natural and inborn; here it is only veiled by the mood of childhood.
There is nothing worldly in this meeting: it is the eternal love of Lakshmi and Narayana awakening again in their
pastimes. Later, when Nimai was already a student, they met once more:

“One day, by providence, as the Lord was coming home from His studies, He saw the daughter of Vallabhacharya on the way to
the Ganga. The love that had been theirs from of old awoke in them both.” (CC, Adi 15.28–29)[^3]

## The Matchmaking
On the very day the Lord met Lakshmi by the Ganga, Vanamali Acharya, the best of the matchmakers of Navadvipa, came to
Shachi's house — in Rama's pastimes, according to the “Gaura-ganoddesha-dipika” (49), Vishvamitra, and in Krishna's the
brahmana whom Rukmini sent to Krishna. Bowing to Shachi, he spoke of her son's marriage: in Navadvipa there lives a
brahmana, Vallabhacharya, faultless in lineage, character and conduct, and he has a daughter who in beauty, virtues and
temperament would not yield to Lakshmi herself; if Shachi wished, he would arrange the match (CB, Adi 10.52–56). But the
mother replied:

“My son is growing up without a father. Let Him first complete His studies. When the time comes, I will think of His
marriage.” (CB, Adi 10.57)

The answer did not please the matchmaker, and he went away downcast. <!-- F12 F13 -->

By providence, on his way back he met Gaurachandra Himself. The Lord embraced him joyfully and asked where he had been;
Vanamali replied that he had gone to His mother to arrange His marriage, but for some reason she had refused. Nimai was
silent a moment, smiled and went home, and there asked His mother why she had declined the brahmana's proposal.[^4] Shachi
understood her son's hint, called the matchmaker the next morning and told him to arrange at once all that he had spoken
of the day before (CB, Adi 10.59–65). The brahmana took the dust of her feet and went straight to Vallabhacharya: “Fix an
auspicious day for your daughter's wedding. The son of Jagannatha Mishra, Sri Vishvambhara, is a great scholar and an
ocean of every virtue; He is a worthy husband for your daughter” (CB, Adi 10.66–70). <!-- F14 F15 -->

Vallabhacharya rejoiced: such a husband comes to a daughter only by great fortune; if Krishna is kind to him, and Lakshmi
and Parvati to his daughter, such a son-in-law will be his. One thing only troubled the poor brahmana: he could give
nothing with his daughter but five haritaki fruits — would they accept that? (CB, Adi 10.71–75). Murari Gupta puts his
words more simply still: all he can give is the daughter herself. Vanamali returned gladly to Shachi and asked her to begin
the preparations, so that all might be done at an auspicious hour (CB, Adi 10.76–77). Thus, in Krishnadasa Kaviraja's
words, “at a sign from Shachi he arranged the match, and her son married Lakshmi” (CC, Adi 15.30). Mahaprabhu was at that
time absorbed in His pastimes as a scholar, and it was His mother who arranged everything. <!-- F16 F17 F18 -->

## The Wedding
Nimai's relatives, hearing the happy news, set about the preparations with enthusiasm. On an auspicious day the
*adhivasa*, the pre-wedding rite, was held: the house rang with songs and music, the brahmanas chanted Vedic hymns, and
Nimai shone among them like the moon among the stars; the relatives honoured the brahmanas according to custom, and
Vallabhacharya performed the auspicious rites for his future son-in-law (CB, Adi 10.78–83). On the morning of the wedding
day the Lord bathed, gave gifts and honoured the ancestors, and at dusk, at the auspicious hour, He came to
Vallabhacharya's house; Vallabhacharya received his son-in-law according to the Vedic rite and seated Him with respect
(CB, Adi 10.84–92). <!-- F19 F20 -->

Then Lakshmi, finely dressed and adorned with every ornament, was led to the Lord. As the rite requires, the bride was
lifted up and carried seven times around Nimai,[^5] then set down before Him, and with joined palms she bowed to the Lord.
Bride and bridegroom exchanged garlands, and He who is served and His maidservant (*sevya* and *sevika*) rejoiced to see
each other. Lakshmi offered a garland at the Lord's feet and gave herself wholly to Him (*atma-nivedana*) (CB, Adi
10.93–97). After the “auspicious glance” (*shubha-drishti*), the first look of bride and groom, the Mistress was seated at
the left of the Master, who had entered His first youth (CB, Adi 10.99). <!-- F21 -->

And Vallabhacharya, “non-different from Bhishmaka” (CB, Adi 10.102), worshipped the feet that Shiva and Brahma worship,
anointed the Lord with sandal paste, offered Him cloth and garlands and gave Him his daughter — so Bhishmaka, king of
Vidarbha, once again placed in Krishna's hand Mahalakshmi, non-different from Rukmini (CB, Adi 10.102–105). In describing
this wedding neither Vrindavana Dasa nor the others speak of anything worldly: it is the union of Lakshmi and Narayana, the
eternal maidservant and her eternal Lord. <!-- F7 F22 -->

The next morning Nimai set out for home with Lakshmi. They were carried in a palanquin, and the women of Navadvipa,
watching them, compared them now to Shiva and Parvati, now to Lakshmi and Narayana, now to Sita and Rama; in the evening
Shachi Devi with the wives of the brahmanas met her daughter-in-law at the threshold and led her into the house (CB, Adi
10.107–117). One of the biographies, retelling Lochana Dasa, adds the Bengali wedding customs: on the eve, bride and
groom were anointed with turmeric paste and bathed, and the place of the rite was sanctified with water; Vallabhacharya
conducted the Vedic ceremony in person, honoured his son-in-law with water and *arghya*, brought Lakshmidevi up onto the
dais and placed her before Gaurahari; the fire-sacrifice of the wedding followed, the brahmanas were fed, and at last the
bride was taken to her new home. <!-- F23 -->

Vrindavana Dasa closes the account of the wedding with a promise: whoever hears of the Lord's marriage will not sink into
worldly cares (CB, Adi 10.119). The biographies explain: for an ordinary man marriage is a bond, but when the Lord marries,
imitating human beings, it is a transcendental event; hearing of the Lord's union with His energy frees one from the power
of the external energy and, in those who take shelter of the divine couple, extinguishes the desire to please one's own
senses, awakening instead, according to each one's nature, service to Gaura and an understanding of that service.

## Lakshmi in Shachi's House
When Lakshmi took her place at the Lord's side, light filled Shachi's house.[^6] Vrindavana Dasa says that the house where
Lakshmi always stayed beside the Lord became like Vaikuntha, the abode of Narayana and Mahalakshmi. Shachi Devi saw a
wondrous radiance in it; at times she saw tongues of flame beside her son, again and again the fragrance of the lotus
filled the house, and she guessed the cause:

“Surely my daughter-in-law is Lakshmi Devi herself. That is why I see this radiance and smell the fragrance of the lotus.
Now our poverty will come to an end. How did I come to have such a daughter-in-law? Without doubt she is Lakshmi
herself!” (CB, Adi 10.124–126)

And so it was: from the time the daughter-in-law set foot in the house, the old want was gone, and everything needed
appeared from no one knew where. Murari Gupta and Kavi Karnapura tell the same. <!-- F24 F25 -->

Yet the true nature of the Lord and His consort remained hidden — such was the Lord's unrestricted will. Vrindavana Dasa
says:

“Who can understand the supreme will of the Lord? Who knows when and how He performs His pastimes? Even Lakshmi cannot
know the Lord unless the Lord Himself allows her to know Him.” (CB, Adi 10.128–129)

The Vedas, the Puranas and all the scriptures, Vrindavana Dasa goes on, declare that only one whom the Lord has blessed
with His mercy can understand Him (CB, Adi 10.130). The biographies explain: the Lord's pastimes cannot be grasped by any
worldly effort; without the mercy of the independent Lord, full in six opulences and the master of maya, His
confidential pastimes are unknown not only to the conditioned soul but even to Lakshmi herself. This “not knowing” on Lakshmi's part is not the power of maya over her but part of the lila itself: by the
Lord's will yogamaya covers His majesty, so that the pastimes may flow as human ones. <!-- F26 I2 -->

## Service
Each day, after teaching His students, Nimai bathed in the Ganga, worshipped Krishna, watered Tulasi and sat down to eat.

“Lakshmi served the rice, and the Lord of Vaikuntha ate it. Pious mother Shachi watched with great joy. After the meal the
Lord chewed betel and lay down to rest, and Lakshmi massaged His lotus feet.” (CB, Adi 12.102–103) <!-- F27 -->

Nimai Pandita lived as an exemplary householder: He gave generously to the needy and honoured guests and people of all
four ashramas. At times He invited ten or twenty sannyasis and sent word to His mother to cook at once for twenty. If
there was nothing in the house Shachi grew anxious — but no sooner had she thought of it than someone brought all that
was needed, and Lakshmi Devi cooked with great joy. The Lord Himself watched His guests being served, and, in Vrindavana
Dasa's words, no one was more fortunate than those guests, “for they had the good fortune to receive food from the hands
of Lakshmi-Narayana Themselves” (CB, Adi 14.11–28). <!-- F28 -->

The biographies call her the embodiment of service to the Lord, and therefore the model of service to one's husband. She
alone ran the whole household in the Lord's home, and ran it with joy:

“Rising at dawn, Lakshmi did all the household work with her own hands. She decorated the floor of the shrine room with
svastikas, conches and discs. For the worship of Lord Vishnu she prepared sandal paste, flowers, incense, lamps and
scented water.” (CB, Adi 14.40–42)

Murari Gupta and Lochana Dasa describe her day in almost the same words. Mahalakshmi in human form, she performed her
household duties for the pleasure of the Lord, and so showed how such duties ought to be done. <!-- F29 I3 -->

Shachi Devi could not rejoice enough in her daughter-in-law's gentleness and good nature. Lakshmi served Tulasi tirelessly,
“but far more gladly still she served mother Shachi” (CB, Adi 14.43). Gaura-Narayana saw her service and, though He said
nothing aloud, was very pleased at heart with His faithful consort (CB, Adi 14.44). Often Lakshmi held the Lord's feet in
her hands for hours, and Shachi saw around her son's lotus feet a radiance like tongues of flame, and on other days felt
the fragrance of the lotus throughout the house (CB, Adi 14.45–47).[^7]

“Thus Lakshmi and Narayana lived in Navadvipa, recognised by no one.” (CB, Adi 14.48) <!-- F33 -->

## Separation
After some time the independent Lord decided to go to East Bengal. To His mother He said, “Mother, I am setting out on a
journey,” and to Lakshmi, “You must serve Mother constantly” (CB, Adi 14.50–51; so also Lochana Dasa). <!-- F34 -->

The sources speak differently of the purpose of this journey. Murari Gupta, and the biographies following Lochana Dasa,
name the outward occasion — the Lord went to teach in order to support His mother and young wife. Krishnadasa Kaviraja
names its essence: the Lord went to bestow His mercy on East Bengal.

“In this way the Lord did good to all the people of East Bengal: giving them the holy name He made them devotees, and
teaching them He made them panditas.” (CC, Adi 16.19)

The Lord needs nothing: earning was only the outward pretext of His pastime, and its aim was mercy to the people on the
banks of the Padmavati. <!-- F35 -->

While Gaura-Narayana in East Bengal was absorbed in His pastimes of learning, in Navadvipa Lakshmi pined in separation from
Him, yet kept silent and showed her grief to no one. Krishnadasa Kaviraja says:

“While the Lord enjoyed these many pastimes in East Bengal, here in Navadvipa Lakshmi grieved in separation from Him.”
(CC, Adi 16.20) <!-- F36 -->

Vrindavana Dasa describes her separation more fully, and it is these verses that the biographies cite:

“Meanwhile in Navadvipa Lakshmi suffered deeply in separation from the Lord, yet she showed it to no one. Almost ceasing to
eat in the Lord's absence, she served mother Shachi constantly. Grieving deeply in separation from the Lord, she took a
little rice only for appearance's sake. All night long she wept alone in her room; not for a moment did her heart find
peace.” (CB, Adi 14.99–102) <!-- F37 Q1 -->

## Departure from This World
At last Lakshmi could bear the separation no longer and wished to go to the Lord (CB, Adi 14.103). How she went,
Vrindavana Dasa tells thus: leaving on earth a likeness of her body (*pratikriti-deha*), she went quite unseen to the Lord; on
the bank of the Ganga, holding His lotus feet in her heart and absorbed in meditation, the Devi “made her departure”
(*karila vijaya*) (CB, Adi 14.104–105).[^8] The biographies say the same: Maheshvari Lakshmi Devi, ever devoted to the
service of the Lord's feet, unable to bear the separation and longing to be at His feet, absorbed in meditation on
Gaura's feet, went to her own abode — she withdrew from sight. One of them adds that she left “prematurely.”[^9]
<!-- F38 F39 -->

Krishnadasa Kaviraja conveys the same departure through an image:

“The snake of separation from the Lord bit Lakshmi, and by its poison she passed from this world.” (CC, Adi 16.21)

The snake here is separation itself. Srila Bhaktivinoda Thakura explains in the “Amrita-pravaha-bhashya”: the pain of
separation from the Lord took the form of a snake and bit Lakshmi, and she went to “the other world” — her own abode,
Vaikuntha, the highest of all. Srila Bhaktisiddhanta writes in the “Anubhashya”: “Bitten by the black snake of
separation, Lakshmi leaves the manifest pastimes.” So too says Lochana Dasa: “Separation took the form of a serpent”
(“Chaitanya-mangala,” Adi-khanda, part 13, v. 111) — and, retelling him, one of the biographies: the snake of separation
bit Lakshmidevi, and she passed into the hidden lila of the Lord, the spiritual realm. This departure is not a
worldly death: the Lord's eternal consort, whose body is not material, only withdrew from His manifest lila and entered
the eternal pastimes. Murari Gupta names this chapter of his poem accordingly — “The Festival of Sri Lakshmi's Departure”
(*Shri-lakshmi-vijaya-utsava*).[^10] <!-- F40 F41 -->

Srila Bhaktisiddhanta explains this at length in his “Gaudiya-bhashya” on the “Chaitanya-bhagavata.” Lakshmipriya Devi is
Mahalakshmi, the svarupa-shakti, the internal energy of Gaura-Narayana; unable to bear separation from Him, she left on
the bank of the Ganga only a likeness of her body and, in her eternal form, passed from people's sight, meditating on the
Lord's feet. And should anyone doubt whether the Lord's svarupa-shakti could, like a conditioned soul, be bitten by a
snake and give up her body, that doubt is removed by the “Bhagavatam” in its account of Krishna's own departure: the Lord
only gives the appearance of leaving a body, like a magician in a performance, while He Himself goes to His abode in His
original form. The bodies of the Lord and of His svarupa-shakti, Srila Bhaktisiddhanta adds, quoting the “Varaha Purana,”
are not made of flesh, blood and bone. And the words *ati alakshite*, “quite unseen” (CB, Adi 14.104), he explains from the “Bhagavatam”: even the gods
could not see Krishna going to His abode (SB 11.31.8–9). <!-- F52 -->

The lament of Shachi, left alone, says Vrindavana Dasa, would have melted even wood; it cannot be described, and he
mentions it only briefly, for it pains him to speak of it. The devotees and kind neighbours, learning of Lakshmi's
departure, helped as they could and reverently performed the rites — in the words of the biographies, the “festival of
withdrawal” (*aprakata-mahotsava*) (CB, Adi 14.106–108). <!-- F42 -->

## The Lord's Return
After spending some time in East Bengal, the Lord returned home with His students and rich gifts. Krishnadasa Kaviraja
says that He knew everything beforehand:

“Dwelling in every heart, the Lord knew of it; and, aware of Shachi's grief, He returned home. He came home with much
wealth and many followers, and by speaking of the truth He freed Shachi from her grief.” (CC, Adi 16.22–23)

Vrindavana Dasa describes the outward course of the lila. At first the relatives, not wishing to sadden the Lord, said
nothing. Seeing His mother downcast, He asked the cause; Shachi lowered her head and wept, unable to speak. “Mother, I know
everything,” said the Lord. “Has something happened to your daughter-in-law?” And then the relatives said, “Pandita, Your
wife has attained the Ganga” — that is, she has left this world (CB, Adi 14.168–178).[^11] The one speaks of the Lord's
omniscience, the other of how He conducted His human pastime; there is no contradiction here. <!-- F43 -->

Hearing of His wife's departure, the Lord bowed His head and for a while was silent.

“For a while He grieved like an ordinary man, and then, with perfect composure, He spoke.” (CB, Adi 14.181)

His sorrow is not the power of grief over Him: it is *nara-lila*, the Lord's pastime in human form, in which He expresses
feelings as a human being would. Srila Bhaktisiddhanta explains: as an ordinary man grieves in separation from his wife,
so for a while the Lord imitated him, and then showed resignation to the inevitable; He Himself is Narayana, untouched by
ignorance. <!-- F53 --> Then He spoke of the highest truth: who in this world is anyone's husband, anyone's son? No one here belongs to anyone,
and to think otherwise is delusion (CB, Adi 14.182). “Mother, why do you grieve? What is ordained cannot
be averted; the course of time is irresistible, and every tie here may break at any moment — that is why the Vedas call
this world impermanent. All the universes are in the power of the Supreme Lord — who but He can join and separate? Why
then grieve over what has come to pass by His will? Who is more fortunate than a pious wife who attains the Ganga before
her husband?” Having thus consoled His mother, He returned with His friends to His affairs (CB, Adi 14.183–188).
<!-- F44 -->

The words “no one here belongs to anyone” were spoken of worldly ties, to console His mother, not of the Lord's
relationship with His eternal consort: Karnapura in his drama recalls the words of the “Vishnu Purana” about Lakshmi:
“When He is a god, she is a goddess; when He is a man, she is a woman” (“Chaitanya-chandrodaya” 1.28). And the words
about the fortunate wife who departs before her husband are consolation in the language of worldly dharma, not evidence
that Lakshmi was an ordinary woman. In Murari Gupta and Lochana Dasa the Lord consoles His mother differently — with the
story of an apsara cursed by Indra to be born on earth; but Murari himself says that with these words the Lord was hiding
the truth, and at once reveals it: “it was the Mistress of the world herself, in the form of Lakshmi.”[^12] <!-- F44 -->

Shachi's heart ached for a long time, but in time she began to look for a new bride for her son — and found Vishnupriya,
the daughter of the raja-pandita Sanatana Mishra (CB, Adi 15). <!-- F45 -->

## Sri and Bhu
Who Lakshmipriya was, Kavi Karnapura says most clearly. In his drama “Chaitanya-chandrodaya” even Kali — the
personification of the age of quarrel, the Lord's adversary — has to admit it:

“Ah, when the Lord descends into the world, His special energy descends with Him — Sri herself in bodily form. Having
accepted her, who imitated the ways of human beings, and having spent a few days with her, God made her disappear.”
(“Chaitanya-chandrodaya” 1.28)

And then, Kali goes on, He took as His wife another beloved, Vishnupriya, the embodiment of a portion of Bhu — so that,
leaving her too, He might give the lesson of renunciation (1.29). <!-- F4 -->

Vishnu has three energies — Sri, Bhu and Nila (Lila). In the pastimes of Gaura-Narayana the sri-shakti is Lakshmipriya,
the bhu-shakti Vishnupriya, and the lila-shakti Sri Navadvipa-dhama itself; and all of them are expansions and maidservants
of the supreme shakti, Sri Radhika. So too Srila Bhaktisiddhanta sings: “Lakshmi Devi is Lakshmipriya, Nila Devi is the
Dhama; the three shaktis serve Radha and Krishna.” <!-- F51 --> Krishnadasa Kaviraja says of the goddesses of fortune that they are partial
manifestations of Radha, “as it were, the splendour of Her body” (CC, Adi 4.77–78). Lakshmipriya is therefore not Radha:
Radha in Gaura's pastimes is Gadadhara Pandita. Srila Bhaktisiddhanta explains in the “Anubhashya” (on CC, Adi 1.37–45):
when Gaura shows His oneness with Narayana, His shaktis are His consorts Lakshmipriya and Vishnupriya; when with Krishna,
Gadadhara Pandita. Lakshmipriya, Vishnupriya and others are the shaktis of Gaura-Vasudeva, and all of them assist
Gaurasundara in His vipralambha, the mood of separation. <!-- F5 F6 Q5 -->

The change of consorts is explained in the same way. Before Gaurasundara revealed Himself as the incarnation of
prema-bhakti, Lakshmi withdrew: while Vishnupriya — prema-bhakti embodied — was growing up, Lakshmipriya served
Gaura-Narayana; and when Vishnupriya had grown and become worthy of that service, Lakshmipriya departed.[^13] <!-- F46 Q4 -->

## Remembrance
In 1892, as Srila Bhaktivinoda Thakura's “Sajjana-toshani” reports, Srila Jagannatha Dasa Babaji proposed that at the
Lord's birthplace in Mayapur one temple building be given to Jagannatha Mishra and Shachi Devi, and a second to Mahaprabhu, flanked by
Lakshmi Devi and Vishnupriya. <!-- F48 -->

Among the prayers of the “Namamrita-samudra,” in which Narahari Chakravarti calls upon the Lord's associates, is one
addressed to both of Gaura's consorts:

“Lakshmi and Vishnupriya Devi, with your companions! Be merciful: may the pastimes in Nadia abide in my mind.”
(“Namamrita-samudra” 6) <!-- F49 -->

And in another prayer of the same poem Narahari asks Govinda Datta: “Show me that pastime — how the Lord was wed to
Lakshmipriya” (“Namamrita-samudra” 115). <!-- F54 -->

## Significance
Lakshmipriya Devi is the Lakshmi of Gaura-Narayana, Janaki and Rukmini in one form, the Lord's sri-shakti who came into
His pastimes in Navadvipa. Her life beside Him is short, yet it holds the whole image of service: at the first meeting she
bowed in her heart to His feet; at the wedding she gave herself to Him; in Shachi's house she served the Lord, Tulasi and
her mother-in-law with her own hands from dawn; and in separation — silently, almost without food, faithful to the Lord's
word — she went on serving His mother until separation itself carried her away to His feet. Hearing of her union with
