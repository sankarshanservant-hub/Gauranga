# Shridhara Thakura (Kholavecha)
*Шридхара Тхакур (Кхолавеча) · @shridhara*

## The Seller of Khola
Sri Shridhara Thakura was a brahmana of Navadvipa and one of the servants dearest to Mahaprabhu’s heart. Hardly anyone
in Navadvipa remembered his name: everyone knew him simply as Kholavecha, “the seller of khola.” In Bengal khola means
whatever the banana tree gives — the leaves and bark from which cups and plates are made, the tender pith of the stem
(thor), the fruit and the flowers (mocha). The “Chaitanya-bhagavata” relates that Shridhara would buy a banana tree,
cut it up and sell it piece by piece (Madhya 9.139–140); at his stall one could buy bananas, stems, banana flowers,
roots (mula) and leaf-plates, and the biographies add that he sold the fruit of his own banana grove. <!-- F2 -->

By this nickname the original sources too remember him. Vrindavana dasa Thakura calls him “Kholavecha Shridhara” (CB,
Madhya 23.93) and “the penniless servant of Krishna” (Antya 8.24); the “Gaura-ganoddesha-dipika” calls him “the
brahmana Shridhara Pandita, known as Kholavecha” (133); Murari Gupta, “the blissful devotee Shridhara, called
Kholavecha” (4.17.8); Lochana Dasa, “Shridhara Pandita, whose home is in Navadvipa” (CM, Madhya-khanda, pt. 1, v. 57);
and Narahari, in a song of prayer, “Shridhara Thakura, the seller of banana leaves.” Hence his two names in the
tradition: Shridhara Pandita and Shridhara Thakura. <!-- F1 F57 F61 -->

Krishnadasa Kaviraja, enumerating the branches of the tree of Sri Chaitanya, speaks of him thus:

“Shridhara, who sold cups of banana bark, was a dear servant of the Lord, and the Lord was always joking with him.
Every day the Lord took banana pith, banana flowers and fruit from him, and once He drank water from his broken iron
pot.” (CC, Adi 10.67–68)

These two verses hold the whole story of Shridhara and the Lord: the jokes and the haggling, the bananas “taken away,”
and the water from the old pot. And none of it is a scene from the bustle of the bazaar: these are the loving pastimes
of the Lord with His devotee, in which He shows whose gifts He accepts, and why. <!-- F5 -->

## A Poverty the World Did Not Understand
Shridhara was poor. Apart from a small banana grove, as Srila Bhaktisiddhanta writes, he had nothing, and the
biographies say that in this lila he played the role of a poor brahmana who lived on the fruit of his grove. But the
way he used his meagre earnings shows who he really was:

“Half of his daily earnings he spent on the worship of the sacred Ganga. The other half he spent on his own needs. This
is how a true devotee of Lord Vishnu is tested.” (CB, Madhya 9.141–142) <!-- F6 F7 -->

He was as truthful as Maharaja Yudhishthira: he named an honest price and never changed it, and those who knew him
bought from him without bargaining (CB, Madhya 9.143–144). Yet no one saw in him a devotee of Vishnu: to all
Navadvipa he was simply a seller of banana leaves (Madhya 9.145). <!-- F8 F9 -->

At night Shridhara did not sleep. Until dawn he loudly, at the top of his voice, chanted the names of Krishna and Hari
(CB, Madhya 9.146, 9.150), and the devotees who heard him rejoiced. But the godless were angry. Vrindavana dasa records
their abuse:

“Because of Shridhara’s shouting we cannot sleep all night. Our ears already ache. He is just an unlucky pauper who
never gets enough to eat — that is why he bawls all night.” (CB, Madhya 9.147–148)

In the Bengali text these people call him something cruder still — a boorish ploughman. So the worldly eye sees a
Vaishnava: to it the loud name of Hari is the cry of a hungry man. And Shridhara, hearing their jeers, only rejoiced and
went on singing (Madhya 9.149). <!-- F10 Q2 -->

His poverty was not worldly destitution, nor “bad luck,” as the scoffers thought, but the selfless devotion of an
akinchana — one who has nothing but Krishna. He asks nothing of the Lord for himself, gives half his earnings to the
Ganga, and later, as we shall see, will refuse both the mystic perfections and a kingdom. Srila Bhaktisiddhanta
explains that the eternal associates of the Lord of Vaikuntha sometimes play the part of paupers to show the world the
ideal of a Vaishnava, but they do not suffer from so-called poverty: suffering in the service of the Lord is for them
the highest happiness. Vrindavana dasa says the same:

“Know for certain: whatever worldly suffering may fall to a Vaishnava’s lot, he experiences it as spiritual
happiness.” (CB, Madhya 9.240) <!-- F6 F10 I6 -->

The Lord had known him, it seems, since childhood. Jayananda in his “Chaitanya-mangala,” listing the companions with
whom Nimai went to His teacher’s house, names Srigarbha Pandita, Murari, Govinda and Shridhara, and among the games of
Nimai’s childhood and youth he recalls how He mischievously made off with Shridhara’s jute.[^1] <!-- F55 -->

## A Conversation on Poverty and Wealth
When Nimai Pandita was already teaching in Navadvipa, He once called on an astrologer and from there, smiling, went on
to the house of His dear Shridhara (CB, Adi 12.178). He loved him deeply and looked for any pretext to see him, and
their joking talks went on for an hour or two (Adi 12.179–180). Long afterwards, when Srinivasa Acharya was shown the
holy places of Navadvipa, he was shown this house too, where the Lord had come from the astrologer’s
(“Bhakti-ratnakara” 12.2233). Seeing Nimai at the door, Shridhara bowed respectfully and gave Him a seat. He himself was
perfectly calm, while the Lord played the part of a restless youth (Adi 12.181–182). <!-- F14 F59 -->

“The Lord asked, ‘Shridhara, you are always chanting the name of Hari — why then are you so poor? Explain to Me how one
who serves the beloved of Lakshmi, the goddess of fortune, can be in want of food and clothing?’” (CB, Adi 12.183–184)

Shridhara answered that he did not go hungry and had some clothing or other on him. But the Lord went on: your cloth
is all knotted, and the thatch has blown off your roof; yet those who worship Chandi or Vishahari lack neither food nor
clothes in their homes (Adi 12.185–187).[^2] Srila Bhaktisiddhanta explains that the Lord only outwardly took the side
of the shaktas, the worshippers of the goddess: by question and answer He was revealing the true philosophy of the
Vaishnavas. And Shridhara replied: <!-- F15 -->

“Shridhara answered, ‘O brahmana, what You say is true. But time passes alike for all. In a king’s house there are many
jewels and he eats sumptuous food, while birds live in the trees. Yet time passes alike for all: by the will of the
Lord each receives the fruits of his karma.’” (CB, Adi 12.188–190)

A bird that feeds on whatever it finds passes its day no worse than a king in his palace: in the measure of happiness
there is no difference between them. So the biographies explain these words, and in them is the calm of a man who has
nothing to lose and nothing to ask. <!-- F16 -->

Then, as if picking a quarrel for no reason at all, the Lord said: “You are hiding a great treasure which you secretly
enjoy. Soon I shall tell everyone about it. Then you will no longer be able to deceive us” (CB, Adi 12.191–192). He
was telling the truth: Shridhara was rich indeed — not in gold, but in devotion. Srila Bhaktisiddhanta explains:
outwardly Shridhara is poor, but in truth he is the richest of men, and soon the Lord will show the foolish world that
the devotees are the owners of all wealth.[^3] Shridhara pleaded: “O Pandita, please go home. We should not quarrel”
(Adi 12.193). But Nimai would not give way: what will you give Me, then? “I live by selling khola,” Shridhara
replied; “what can I offer You?” <!-- F17 -->

“The Lord said, ‘Very well. Let the secret treasure stay with you for now; I shall take it later. But now give Me,
without payment, some bananas, banana flowers and stems. Then I shall stop quarrelling with you.’” (CB, Adi
12.196–197)

Srila Bhaktisiddhanta discloses the meaning of these words: “Today I do not ask you for your spiritual wealth — give Me
only a part of your outer wealth; later I shall accept your spiritual service too. For now, satisfy My needs as befits
a sadhaka: for those who follow the path of sadhana-bhakti, I am the One who is worshipped.” The Lord needs nothing,
but He wishes the devotee to serve Him with the little he has. <!-- F19 I3 -->

Shridhara pondered. Nimai was playing the part of a quarrelsome youth, and Shridhara thought that this unruly brahmana
might well give him a beating one day; and to give away his goods every day for nothing was beyond his means. But love
had its way:

“But by my great good fortune He comes to me and by trickery or deceit takes whatever He needs. I think I shall go on
giving Him whatever He asks.” (CB, Adi 12.200)

So in this game “fear” and “grievance” turn into happiness: whatever the Lord takes from him, Shridhara counts as his
good fortune. And the fear of a “beating” is itself part of the play, in which Nimai deliberately acts the bully — not
dread of worldly force. <!-- F18 -->

“I shall gladly give You bananas, banana flowers, cups of banana leaf and banana stems. Only one thing I ask of You —
leave me in peace!” said Shridhara. And the Lord replied: “Very well, I shall not quarrel with you. But see to it that
I get the very best bananas and banana stems.” (CB, Adi 12.202–203) <!-- F18 -->

From then on the Lord ate every day from Shridhara’s cups and relished dishes made of his banana leaves, flowers and
stems:

“Whenever a gourd grew on the roof of Shridhara’s house, the Lord would take it and eat it, cooked with milk and black
pepper.” (CB, Adi 12.205) <!-- F20 -->

That day the Lord put one more question to Shridhara: what do you think of Me?

“Shridhara replied, ‘You are a brahmana, a part of Lord Vishnu.’ The Lord said, ‘You do not know. In truth I belong to
the caste of cowherds. Though you take Me for a brahmana’s son, I am a cowherd’s son.’” (CB, Adi 12.207–208)

This is no joke but the truth about Himself, covered by a joke. Srila Bhaktisiddhanta gives the sense of these words
thus: “I am not a part of Vishnu; I am svayam-rupa, the original Lord Himself; I am Krishna, the son of Nanda.”
Shridhara smiled, but did not recognize his Lord. So the Lord Himself willed it, guarding His secret: His internal
potency, yogamaya, veiled Him from His eternal associate, the best of devotees (Adi 12.209).[^4] <!-- F21 F22 -->

“Then the Lord said, ‘Shridhara, I shall tell you the truth: the Ganga has her glory only because of Me.’” (CB, Adi
12.210)

And this is true: the Ganga flows from the feet of Vishnu, and Nimai is Krishna Himself, the source of Vishnu. But
Shridhara, by the Lord’s will not seeing this, took His words for impudence: “O Nimai Pandita, how is it You are not
afraid to insult the Ganga? As a man grows older he should become steadier, but You, I see, grow ever more restless”
(Adi 12.211–212). And so, having enjoyed His jokes with Shridhara, Lord Gauranga returned home (Adi 12.213). <!-- F21 -->

The biographies draw a lesson from this conversation. Worldly prosperity is no sign of the Lord’s mercy: worshippers of
the gods often live in plenty, while truly blessed is he who is rich in devotion. Whoever loves Krishna is rich, and
whoever does not is poor, however well provided for: so poor Vidura was richer than Duryodhana. Through Kholavecha
Shridhara the Lord showed the world that love alone conquers Him and that He accepts what is offered with love. Krishna
Himself says in the “Bhagavatam”: “By devotion alone, full of faith, am I attained — I, the Self dear to the saints”
(Bhag. 11.14.21) — and in the “Gita”: “If one offers Me with love a leaf, a flower, a fruit or water, I accept that
offering made with love by one whose heart is pure” (Bhagavad-gita 9.26).[^5] <!-- I1 Q14 -->

## Bargaining at the Stall
Even in the years when the Lord in His pastimes was a scholar — a proud one, as it seemed to people — He would come
every day, concealing His nature, to Shridhara’s stall. On the pretext of buying banana leaves He enjoyed the company of
His devotee (CB, Madhya 9.160–162).[^6] The haggling went on for an hour or two, and each time the Lord offered half of
an already modest price:

“Then Shridhara would get up and snatch his goods back from Nimai, and a quarrel would break out between them.
Shridhara and Gaurasundara would start pushing each other.” (CB, Madhya 9.165) <!-- F23 Q3 -->

Each of them pulled the bunch of bananas his own way. And the Lord, scolding him, hinted at His divinity: “O brother
Shridhara, why do you live like a beggar ascetic when you have so much wealth? Why do you snatch My purchases from Me?
Don’t you know who I am?” (Madhya 9.166–167). But Shridhara could not be angry with Him:

“Shridhara, who possessed all the virtues of a brahmana, was not angry with Mahaprabhu. Looking into Gaurasundara’s
face, he would give Him all his goods.” (CB, Madhya 9.168)

The Lord, as the biographies say, took the goods only when He saw that Shridhara was not angry. And Shridhara, gazing
on the Lord’s beauty, sank into an ocean of joy and smiled (Madhya 9.177) — how could he be angry? <!-- F24 -->

Once, while bargaining, the Lord said to him:

“I am the father of the Ganga whom you worship every day. This is the very truth.” (CB, Madhya 9.179)

“Every day you buy gifts and offer them to her — so give Me a better price too,” He said (Madhya 9.178). Shridhara
would cover his ears with his palms and cry, “Vishnu! Vishnu!” — and, seeing such unheard-of impudence, give way
(Madhya 9.180). Here too the Lord was telling the truth about Himself: the Ganga flows from His feet; but Shridhara, as
before, by His will heard in these words only blasphemy. <!-- F25 Q4 -->

At last they came to terms. “All right, You win,” said Shridhara, “only leave me in peace. I shall give You something
for free: one stem, a few bananas and a few leaves.” — “Good, good,” the Lord replied, “I have nothing more to reproach
you with” (CB, Madhya 9.182–184). From then on He constantly ate rice from Shridhara’s banana plates. And Vrindavana
dasa sums it up: <!-- F26 -->

“Thus the Lord relishes the gifts of His devotees and pays no attention to millions of offerings from people devoid of
devotion to God.” (CB, Madhya 9.185)

For the sake of this pastime, he says, the Lord arranged that Shridhara should sell banana leaves — and such pastimes
cannot be understood unless the Lord Himself reveals their meaning (Madhya 9.186–188). So the whole “trade” of
Kholavecha turns out to be service: the Lord takes away and eats the offerings of His devotee, but at the gifts of one
who does not love Him He will not even look. <!-- F27 Q5 I2 -->

Other poets too remember these pastimes. Murari Gupta writes that Gaurasundara, fond of learned fun and jokes, was
constantly sporting with Shridhara;[^7] Devakinandana in the “Vaishnava-vandana” glorifies “Shridhara Pandita, called
Kholavecha, with whom the Lord constantly bantered,” and Madhava “generous Shridhara, whose customer was Gauranga at his
stall of banana pith, bananas and banana flowers.” <!-- F56 F60 -->
