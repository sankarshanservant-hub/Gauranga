# Krishnadasa Kaviraja Gosvami
*Кришнадас Кавираджа Госвами · @krishnadasa-kaviraja*

## “I, Krishnadasa”
Anyone who has read the “Sri Chaitanya-charitamrita” remembers the line with which every one of its chapters closes:

“Praying at the feet of Shri Rupa and Shri Raghunatha, I, Krishnadasa, narrate the ‘Chaitanya-charitamrita.’” (CC, Adi
5.235)

So Krishnadasa Kaviraja Gosvami names himself in his book: simply Krishnadasa, “servant of Krishna.” By the same name
Nityananda calls him in the dream: “O Krishnadasa!” (Adi 5.195). Srila Bhaktisiddhanta Sarasvati, in his introduction
to the edition of the “Charitamrita,” writes that this is a spiritual name, not the one he was known by in his family.
Vaishnavas usually call him Sri Kaviraja Gosvami, and the later books, the “Prema-vilasa” and the
“Bhakti-ratnakara,” simply the Kaviraja.[^1] <!-- F1 F64 -->

“Kaviraja” is not a caste but a title: “king of poets.” According to Bhaktisiddhanta, it was given to those whose
writings had won renown among the learned; the same word is also a title of Ayurvedic physicians, and hence the opinion
that Krishnadasa was a vaidya by birth. One of the biographies says that the most learned Gosvamis of Vrindavana gave
him this title for the depth of his knowledge and his poetic gift; another, that he received it for the
“Govinda-lilamrita,” without saying who conferred it. In the spiritual family he was known just so — as Kaviraja
Gosvami. <!-- F2 -->

## Origins
Of his worldly life Krishnadasa says almost nothing. Srila Bhaktisiddhanta writes that neither his parents nor even
their names are known, and that recent claims to have found the names have nothing to support them.[^2] One of the
biographies adds that his father is mentioned here and there, but that these reports are not accepted as reliable.
<!-- F4 -->

His varna, too, is unknown. Those of differing opinions, writes Bhaktisiddhanta, assign him to one of the three higher
castes — brahmana, kayastha or vaidya. In favour of his being a brahmana speaks his extraordinary knowledge of the
shastras and sciences, evident in the “Charitamrita”; but the view that he was a kayastha is not without ground either:
telling of Raghunatha Dasa's youth, he praises the intelligence and practical gifts of the kayasthas. He could have been
born into any of the three. One of the biographies holds that he was a brahmana or a kayastha, since in those days such
learning was beyond the reach of people of other classes, and remarks that out of natural humility he nowhere said he
was born a brahmana. <!-- F3 -->

For a Vaishnava, however, this dispute does not matter. Vrindavana Dasa says:

“All the scriptures declare: in whatever family a Vaishnava is born, he always holds the highest position. Haridasa,
born in a family of yavanas, is the best example of this. He saw manifestations of the Lord inaccessible even to the
gods of this world headed by Brahma. Sinners who divide Vaishnavas by caste doom themselves to many births in lower
species of life.” (CB, Madhya 10.100–102) <!-- I3 Q14 -->

From the “Charitamrita” we know that he had a brother — whose name he does not give (Adi 5.172–178); of him we shall
speak below. Whether he himself was married is unknown: his own account is silent, and according to one of the
biographies some testimonies say he was not. Another biography cites the opinion that by the time he left for
Vrindavana he was still a brahmachari — otherwise, in telling how he left home, he would have mentioned the family he
left behind. Srila Bhaktisiddhanta looks deeper: on coming to Vrindavana, Krishnadasa was wholly absorbed in hari-katha
and indifferent to talk of his former family life, as befits one in the third or fourth ashrama, wholly given to service;
and the “Charitamrita” is the work of one who had attained the position of a paramahamsa, beyond all four ashramas.
<!-- F5 F6 -->

He lived in the village of Jhamatpur in the Bardhaman district of West Bengal — “in a village called Jhamatapura, near
Naihati,” as he writes himself (Adi 5.181); according to Bhaktisiddhanta, he was also born there. The “Anubhashya”
explains that one goes there by the Katwa line to Salar station; Bhaktisiddhanta adds that the village lies four miles
west of Naihati, in the Katwa subdivision. According to Srila Bhaktivinoda Thakura's “Amrita-pravaha-bhashya,” it is
two kroshas north of Katwa, and a Deity of Sri Mahaprabhu is worshipped there today. His Deities of Gaura-Nitai are
worshipped in Jhamatpur to this day, but there seem to be no descendants of his family there. Where his house stood
there is now a small temple of Sri Gaura-Nityananda with Nityananda's footprints; in it a wooden sandal is kept which is
held to be Krishnadasa's, and the temple preserves the memory of his life there.[^3] <!-- F7 F8 -->

The year of his appearance is not known for certain: by Srila Bhaktisiddhanta's reckoning he was born probably around
1520. The sources do not name the day of his appearance, nor is it found in the Vaishnava calendar of the Sri Chaitanya
Saraswat Math. Thus in the years when Mahaprabhu was manifesting His pastimes in Puri, Krishnadasa was still living in
Bengal. <!-- F57 -->

## Kirtana at Jhamatpur
All we know of Krishnadasa's life before Vrindavana is a single story, which he himself placed in the fifth chapter of
the Adi-lila to glorify Nityananda's mercy. He recalls:

“In my house there was sankirtana day and night, and he came there by invitation.” (CC, Adi 5.162)

One of the biographies calls it an eight-prahara kirtana festival lasting a day and a night; another, a
twenty-four-hour kirtana to which, among other guests, Minaketana Ramadasa, a devotee of Nityananda who apparently
lived nearby, was invited. Overwhelmed with divine love, Minaketana sat down in the courtyard, and all the Vaishnavas
bowed at his feet; in his rapture he climbed on someone's back, struck another with his flute, and at the very name of
Nityananda signs of ecstasy appeared in his body, and all were amazed (Adi 5.163–167). <!-- F9 F10 -->

In the house the brahmana Gunarnava Mishra served the Deity:

“A respectable brahmana named Gunarnava Mishra was serving the Deity there. Ramadasa sat in the courtyard, but the
brahmana did not greet him; seeing this, Minaketana said angrily: ‘Here is a second Romaharshana Suta, who did not rise
to greet Baladeva when he saw Him!’ Having said this, he danced and sang and rejoiced; but the brahmana went on with his
service to Krishna and showed no anger.” (CC, Adi 5.168–171)

All bowed before the servant of Nityananda, but the servant of the Deity did not even rise to meet him: so there was no
faith in Nityananda in him.[^4] <!-- F11 -->

Trouble came at the end of the festival, when Gunarnava had already left:

“At the end of the festival he was leaving, having given his blessings, when a dispute arose between him and my
brother. My brother had firm faith in Chaitanya Prabhu, but towards Nityananda only a semblance of faith. Knowing this,
Ramadasa was grieved at heart, and so I rebuked my brother: ‘The two brothers are one body, equally manifest. If you
do not accept Nityananda, you will be utterly ruined. You have faith in one but show the other no respect: your
standard is the logic of half a hen. If you accept neither, you are an atheist; and to accept one and not the other is
hypocrisy.’” (CC, Adi 5.172–177)

According to one of the biographies, Krishnadasa also made his brother understand that discourtesy had been shown to
Nityananda's servant, and that Nityananda is the avatara of Baladeva, so that to consider Him lower than Sri Chaitanya
is wrong. <!-- F12 F13 -->

“In anger Ramadasa broke his flute and went away, and at that very moment my brother was ruined.” (CC, Adi 5.178)

One of the biographies calls what befell the brother the result of a “curse,” but Ramadasa did not curse him: he only
broke his flute and left. His anger was the anger of a servant for the honour of his Lord, and the brother lost his
devotion as the fruit of offence to a Vaishnava and of unbelief in Nityananda. The lesson of the story is that Chaitanya
and Nityananda cannot be divided: whoever honours one and slights the other attains neither. <!-- Q1 -->

## The Dream
For Krishnadasa himself that night became the beginning of a new life:

“Because I had rebuked my brother — and He counted this as a virtue — that night the Lord appeared to me. In a village
called Jhamatapura, near Naihati, Nityananda-Rama appeared to me in a dream.” (CC, Adi 5.180–181)

Krishnadasa fell at His feet, and Nityananda placed His lotus foot on his head. He then describes the Lord's form — the
dark, glossy body, the silk garments, the golden ornaments, the red staff in His hand, the gait of an intoxicated
elephant — and His associates dressed as cowherds, who played horns and flutes and sang “Krishna! Krishna!” (Adi
5.182–193). And then, smiling, Nityananda spoke to him:

“‘O Krishnadasa, Krishnadasa, do not be afraid! Go to Vrindavana: there everything can be attained.’ Saying this, He
touched me with His hand and sent me forth; and the Lord disappeared with His associates.” (CC, Adi 5.195–196)

Waking at dawn, Krishnadasa understood that the Lord had ordered him to go to Vrindavana without delay, and he set out
at once:

“At that very moment I set out, and by the Lord's mercy I reached Vrindavana in happiness.” (CC, Adi 5.199)

One of the biographies says that Nityananda encouraged him and ordered him to go to Vrindavana for his highest good;
another, that, pleased with this one small virtue, the Lord revealed His form to him and granted him residence in
Vrindavana-dhama. Krishnadasa himself writes that by taking the side of Nityananda's servant and rebuking his brother he
earned the blessing of Nityananda Himself. The “Prema-vilasa,” too, remembers the dream: while living in Gauda he
worshipped Krishna in bliss, and in Jhamatpur Nityananda appeared to him with His associates and commanded, “All
perfection is yours — go to Vrindavana!” According to it, in his own book he calls himself a disciple of Prabhu
Nityananda, who showed him mercy without regarding his wretchedness; and on reaching Vrindavana he took shelter at the
feet of Raghunatha. <!-- F14 F15 F68 Q2 -->

The biographies draw a lesson from this. The Lord submits to His devotees and values even a small devotion to them: for
a single act of standing up for Nityananda's associate Krishnadasa received His mercy. Conversely, whoever despises the
devotees loses the Lord's blessing, whatever his outward merits: so Ramachandra Khan, who offended Haridasa Thakura, lost
family, property and home. Only a fool would dare to offend a saint. <!-- I1 -->

## By Nityananda's Mercy
All that Krishnadasa attained in Vraja he ascribes to Nityananda's mercy:

“Glory, glory to Nityananda, to Nityananda-Rama, by whose mercy I attained the abode of Vrindavana! Glory, glory to
Nityananda! Glory to Him who is full of mercy, through whom I attained the shelter of Rupa and Sanatana! Through Him I
attained the great soul Raghunatha; through Him I attained the shelter of Shri Svarupa. By Sanatana's mercy I came to
know the conclusions of bhakti; by Shri Rupa's mercy I came near to bhakti-rasa.” (CC, Adi 5.200–203)

Rupa, Sanatana, Raghunatha Dasa and the other Gosvamis taught him the philosophy and science of bhakti, and their life
of renunciation deeply influenced him, though detachment from worldly ties had been in him before. Of their mercy he
speaks here in humble gratitude to Nityananda, recalling a little of his former life as well. <!-- F16 F17 Q3 -->

By their mercy the vision opened in him by which one sees the Lord in the Deity. He was blessed to see the presence of
Sri Radha and Krishna and of Sri Madana-Gopala in Their Deities, and, in his words, received the transcendental
(*aprakrita*) darshan of Sri Govinda:

“The sight of Shri Madana-Gopala and Shri Govinda — all this is not fit to be told. <…> Nityananda's mercy showed Him to
me and gave me Shri Radha and Madanamohana as my Lady and Lord. To me, so lowly, He gave the sight of Shri Govinda. It
is not a thing to be told; it is beyond all telling.” (CC, Adi 5.211, 5.216–217) <!-- F18 -->

And here he warns his readers: the Deity is Krishna Himself, and to slight Him, seeing in Him a mere image, is the
gravest offence:

“He is the son of the king of Vraja Himself — of this there is no doubt. The fool who thinks of Him as a mere image has
no deliverance for that offence; he falls into a terrible hell. What more can I say?” (CC, Adi 5.225–226)
<!-- F19 Q5 -->

Telling of Nityananda's mercy, he speaks of himself thus:

“I am more sinful than Jagai and Madhai, lower than a worm in excrement. Whoever hears my name loses his piety; whoever
utters my name commits sin. Who in all the world but Nityananda alone would show mercy to one as despicable as I?
Intoxicated with love, Nityananda is the avatara of mercy; He makes no distinction between the high and the low. He
delivers whoever falls before Him; and so He delivered even a sinner like me.” (CC, Adi 5.205–209)

These words are not to be taken as an account of some dark past. This is the humility of a pure devotee who considers
himself “lower than the grass” — the mark of the highest Vaishnava; in this way he glorifies the boundless mercy of
Nityananda, who makes no distinction between the worthy and the unworthy. Jagai and Madhai, with whom he compares
himself, are the two villainous brothers whom Chaitanya and Nityananda delivered from the gravest sins.
<!-- F20 Q4 -->

The Vaishnavas of Vrindavana he regarded as his true kin. In the Vaishnava world he is honoured as the best of the
followers of Sri Rupa; one of the biographies calls him just that: “the revered Sri Krishnadasa Kaviraja, best of the
Rupanugas.” <!-- F21 -->

## Guru and Teachers
Krishnadasa names Raghunatha Dasa Gosvami as his guru. At the end of the book, explaining by whose mercy it was
written, he says:

“Shri Govinda, Shri Chaitanya, Shri Nityananda, Shri Advaita, the devotees and the revered listeners; Shri Svarupa, Shri
Rupa, Shri Sanatana; Shri Raghunatha dasa, my guru, and the feet of Shri Jiva — by the mercy of the feet of all these I
am made to write.” (CC, Antya 20.96–98)

Srila Bhaktisiddhanta explains in the “Anubhashya” that Raghunatha Dasa is the guru who taught him bhajana, the best of
the Rupanugas. So too in the closing prayer of the book Krishnadasa takes on his head the feet of “Shri Raghunatha, my
guru” (Antya 20.144–146). One of the biographies writes that the Kaviraja accepted as his guru Raghunatha Dasa, the best
follower of Rupa; according to the “Prema-vilasa,” he also received initiation from him (“the disciple of Dasa Gosani
is the Kaviraja”), lived beside him and took shelter at his feet.[^5] <!-- F22 -->

His teachers on the path of bhakti were six — the six Gosvamis of Vrindavana:

“First I offer obeisance at the feet of my mantra-guru and of all my instructing gurus. Shri Rupa, Sanatana, Raghunatha
Bhatta, Shri Jiva, Gopala Bhatta and Raghunatha Dasa — these six are my instructing gurus; at the lotus feet of them all
I offer millions of obeisances.” (CC, Adi 1.35–37)

One of the biographies notes in particular that Gopala Bhatta is one of his six shiksha-gurus; he honours Sri Jiva, too,
as his shiksha-guru. <!-- F23 -->

Krishnadasa lived at Radha-kunda, beside Raghunatha Dasa. On the bank of the kunda stood his hut — a bhajana-kutir, where
his disciple Mukunda Dasa later lived. Yadunandana Dasa's “Karnananda” pictures him there together with Raghunatha Dasa
and Lokanatha Gosvami: day and night they spoke of Krishna. The rest of his life, writes Bhaktisiddhanta, he spent in
Vraja-dhama. <!-- F25 -->

His closeness to Raghunatha gave him that without which the last chapters of the “Charitamrita” would not exist. From
Raghunatha he learned of the states of the highest mahabhava which Sri Chaitanya manifested in His last years, and he
placed these descriptions towards the end of the book. For Raghunatha kept in memory the notes of Svarupa Damodara — his
“kadcha” (diary), in which Svarupa had described the Lord's later pastimes in Sanskrit verses. Srila Bhaktivinoda
Thakura writes in the “Amrita-pravaha-bhashya” that Svarupa told Raghunatha to learn these verses by heart and to preach
them; they are written down nowhere else, and the “Charitamrita” is their essence. Krishnadasa himself says so:

“Svarupa held in his treasury the jewel-like essence of Chaitanya's pastimes, and he placed it in Raghunatha's throat.
The little I heard from Raghunatha I have expanded here, and I give it to the devotees as a gift.” (CC, Madhya 2.84)

“The pastimes that Svarupa Gosani recorded in his notebook (*kadacha*), and all that I heard from the mouth of
Raghunatha dasa — those pastimes I tell in brief. Though I am an insignificant soul, I write by Chaitanya's mercy.” (CC,
Antya 3.267–268)

“These pastimes are revealed in the diaries (*kadacha*) of these two, Svarupa Gosani and Raghunatha dasa. At that time
these two lived at Mahaprabhu's side, while all the other diarists lived in distant places.” (CC, Antya 14.7–8)

So, in the words of one of the biographies, Svarupa's notes, preserved in Raghunatha's memory, unfolded by the Kaviraja's
grace in his book. <!-- F24 Q10 -->

And Raghunatha returned his love. In the last verse of his “Mukta-charita” he humbly acknowledges that he wrote it by the
strength of his association with Krishnadasa Kaviraja, and prays to be with him in Vraja birth after birth. <!-- F52 -->

## The Vaishnavas' Order
It was the Vaishnavas of Vrindavana who moved Krishnadasa to take up the book. He names them himself in the eighth
chapter of the Adi-lila. The first of them is Haridasa Pandita, servant of Sri Govinda, the dear disciple of Ananta
Acharya, who was a disciple of Gadadhara Pandita; he constantly read the “Chaitanya-bhagavata,” and by his grace all the
Vaishnavas heard it:

“Out of his great mercy he gave me the order to describe Gauranga's final pastimes.” (CC, Adi 8.65)

Others too wished to hear of the Lord's last pastimes: Govinda Gosvami, disciple of Kashishvara and dear servant of Sri
Govinda; Yadavacharya Gosvami, companion of Rupa; Bhugarbha Gosvami, disciple of Gadadhara Pandita, and his disciples —
Chaitanya Dasa, the priest of Govinda, Mukundananda Chakravarti and Krishnadasa; Shivananda Chakravarti, disciple of
Ananta Acharya (Adi 8.66–70).

“And all the other devotees who live in Vrindavana wished to hear the final pastimes. In their compassion they all gave
me the order, and at their word I write, casting off all shame.” (CC, Adi 8.71–72) <!-- F29 -->

Why this was needed Krishnadasa explains in the same chapter. In the “Chaitanya-bhagavata” Vrindavana Dasa first set down
the Lord's pastimes in brief and then began to describe them at length; the book grew long, and some of the pastimes
named in the summary he never described:

“He became absorbed in describing the pastimes of Nityananda, and Chaitanya's final pastimes were left untold. The
devotees of Vrindavana longed to hear the account of all those pastimes.” (CC, Adi 8.48–49)

One of the biographies says it briefly: he took up the book to fulfil the wish of the Gosvamis of Vraja. <!-- F29 -->

## Madana-Gopala's Garland
But even the Vaishnavas' order was not enough for him: he wished to receive the permission of the Lord Himself.

“Having received the Vaishnavas' order, I went with an anxious heart to Madana-gopala to beg for His order too. When I
had seen Him, I bowed at His feet; the pujari Gosani-dasa was serving His feet. When I begged at the Lord's feet for His
order, the garland slipped from His neck and fell. All the Vaishnavas cried out ‘Hari!’, and Gosani-dasa brought the
garland and placed it around my neck. Receiving the garland of His order, I was filled with joy, and then and there I
began this book.” (CC, Adi 8.73–77) <!-- F30 -->

From then on Krishnadasa did not regard the book as his own:

“It is Madana-mohana who makes me write this book; my writing is like the recitation of a parrot. I write what
Madana-gopala makes me write, as a magician makes a wooden doll dance. My family Deity is Madana-mohana, whose servants
are Raghunatha, Rupa and Sanatana.” (CC, Adi 8.78–80)

Only at the end of the book does he reveal what, by his own words, he ought not to have revealed:

“Shri Madana-Gopala, by His order, makes me write. It is not fitting to say this, yet I cannot keep silent. If I did not
say it, I would be guilty of ingratitude. Though I speak as if out of pride, O listeners, do not be angry.” (CC, Antya
20.99–100)

The “Charitamrita,” therefore, is neither a composition of its author's own design nor a biography in the worldly
sense. Srila Bhaktisiddhanta explains in the “Anubhashya” on Adi 8.78 that this book is wholly made and inspired by
Madana-mohana Himself. Krishnadasa only revealed to the world what the Lord wished to disclose through him.
<!-- F31 Q6 -->

## What He Did Not Write
Before beginning, Krishnadasa also asked permission of the senior associates of the Lord. The “Bhakti-ratnakara” tells:

“Sri Gopala Bhatta gladly gave his permission, but forbade him to describe in the book anything concerning himself. Why
he forbade it — who can understand? He always considered himself the lowest of the low. The Kaviraja could not
transgress his order: he writes only his name and makes nothing else known. Lokanatha Gosvami too gave such an order;
this I heard from the lips of old Vaishnavas.” (“Bhakti-ratnakara” 1.222–225)

One of the biographies explains: a guru's order is carried out without deliberation, and so Krishnadasa told nothing
about Gopala Bhatta, only naming him as one of the six Gosvamis. Lokanatha, the eldest of the Lord's associates then
living, dreaded the slightest fame and set his condition even before the work began — that neither his name nor his deeds
be described; and only his name remained in the book. So too with Bhugarbha: with his and Lokanatha's blessing
Krishnadasa began to describe Mahaprabhu's last pastimes; his words (Adi 8.68–69) show how much Bhugarbha and his
disciples wished to hear of Gaura, yet they too asked not to be mentioned — and so so little is known of them.
According to one of the biographies, Prabodhananda Sarasvati, too, out of humility asked through Gopala Bhatta that his
deeds not be described.[^6] <!-- F32 F33 F34 F35 -->

This silence is neither a gap nor negligence. In it is Krishnadasa's obedience to the Vaishnavas and the humility of
those great devotees who wanted no fame for themselves. <!-- F34 -->

## Following Vrindavana Dasa
Srila Bhaktisiddhanta writes that the “Charitamrita” was conceived as a supplement to Vrindavana Dasa's book. So says
Krishnadasa himself:

“Therefore to describe them again would be mere repetition; and should I presume to describe them out of pride, I have
no such power. So I shall only touch briefly upon the pastimes he described in the ‘Chaitanya-mangala.’ As best I can,
I shall tell those pastimes he mentioned in his summary but did not describe. Therefore I bow down at his feet, praying
that I may not offend him.” (CC, Madhya 4.6–9)

“Vrindavana dasa is the Vyasa of Shri Chaitanya's pastimes, and he set them down with great sweetness. Fearing that his
book would grow too long, he left out certain parts, and of these I shall say something.” (CC, Adi 13.48–49)
<!-- F27 Q7 Q9 -->

Vrindavana Dasa he honours as the Vyasa of the Lord's pastimes:

“Veda-vyasa told the pastimes of Krishna in the ‘Bhagavata’; the Vyasa of Chaitanya's pastimes is Vrindavana dasa.” (CC,
Adi 8.34)

“Millions of obeisances at the feet of Vrindavana dasa! By writing such a book he delivered the world.” (CC, Adi 8.40)

Some even hold that it was Krishnadasa Kaviraja and Lochana Dasa who gave Vrindavana Dasa's book its name — the
“Chaitanya-bhagavata”: for to the Kaviraja Vrindavana Dasa is Vyasa, and Vyasa's book is the “Bhagavata.”[^7]
<!-- F28 Q8 -->

Narahari Chakravarti in the “Bhakti-ratnakara” sees in this a custom of poets. Vrindavana Dasa described the Lord's
pastimes, but not the journey to the South and the rest:

“Krishnadasa Kaviraja, humbly bowing to him, described the tour of the South and the rest in detail. Here and there he
left [things] to be described for the sake of the poets who would write afterwards. So a worshipful guru, having
happily eaten rice and other dishes, leaves remnants on the plate for his disciple.” (“Bhakti-ratnakara” 1.215–217)

“Krishnadasa Kaviraja, greatly delighted, wrote his book after receiving the command of many.” (“Bhakti-ratnakara”
1.221) <!-- F67 -->

## The Book's Treasuries
Besides Svarupa's notes and Raghunatha's accounts, Krishnadasa drew also on the Gosvamis' books. According to one of the
biographies, a number of the conclusions of the “Charitamrita” are taken from Sri Jiva's “Sandarbhas”; the book itself
he probably unfolded from the verses of two of Raghunatha Dasa's hymns to the Lord — the “Shachisunu-ashtaka” and the
“Gauranga-stava-kalpataru” — and the description of Radha's form in the Madhya-lila (8.164–180) is based on Raghunatha's
“Premambhoja-maranda.” Therefore, remarks the same biography, the events which the Kaviraja, Rupa's exclusive servant,
heard from Raghunatha of Svarupa's circle and set down cannot be called poetic invention. <!-- F37 -->

## The Structure of the Book
Without the mercy of Vishnu and the Vaishnavas their glories cannot be described — and so Krishnadasa begins every
chapter with obeisances to Mahaprabhu and His associates, Nityananda and Advaita, and ends it with a prayer at the feet
of Rupa and Raghunatha. In this he teaches how important it is to avoid all disrespect and offence to Vaishnavas.
<!-- F36 I2 -->

At the very beginning of the book he bows to the three Deities of Vrindavana:

“All glory to the merciful Shri Shri Radha and Madana-mohana! I am lame and dull of mind, and They are my only refuge;
Their lotus feet are all I have. I remember Shri Shri Radha and Shri Govindadeva, seated on a throne in a beautiful
temple of jewels beneath a wish-fulfilling tree in the radiant forest of Vrindavana, served by Their dearest companions.
May the beautiful Shri Gopinatha, who begins the sweet rasa dance, be our good fortune. Standing on the bank by
Vamshivata, He draws the gopis to Himself with the sounds of His flute. <…> These three Deities have made the Gaudiya
devotees Their own. I worship Their feet: these Three are my Lords.” (CC, Adi 1.15–17, 1.19)

According to an explanation going back to Srila Bhaktisiddhanta, the order here is not by chance. First he bows to
Madana-mohana — the Deity who establishes one's relationship with Krishna (*sambandha*); then to Govinda — the Deity who
accepts service (*abhidheya*); and to Gopinatha — Krishna, lord of the gopis, who draws them with His flute — the highest
goal (*prayojana*). <!-- Q15 I5 -->

## When the Book Was Written
The “Charitamrita” was composed while Sri Jiva was still present; Srila Bhaktisiddhanta adds in the “Anubhashya” on Adi
10.85 that soon afterwards Srinivasa, Narottama and Duhkhi Krishnadasa (Shyamananda) came to Vrindavana. According to
some, the book was finished before Srinivasa and Narottama began to preach — which is why their names are not in it, and
there is nothing unnatural in that. As one of the biographies adds, Sri Jiva sent the “Charitamrita” to Gauda with
Srinivasa together with the other books of the Gosvamis, having shortly before given him the title “Acharya.” The last verse of the book gives the time of its completion:

“In the Shaka year 1537, in the month of Jyaishtha, in Vrindavana, on a Sunday, the fifth day of the dark fortnight,
this book was completed.” (CC, Antya 20.158)

In the original the year is encoded in the words “sindhu-agni-bana-indu” — “oceans, fire, arrows, moon,” that is, 1537
(1615 AD); the “Anubhashya” notes that verses 156–158 are missing from many manuscripts.[^8] <!-- F38 F70 -->

## “Yet I Write”
Krishnadasa wrote as a very old man. Closing his summary of the last pastimes, he confesses:

“I am old and infirm; my hand trembles as I write, and my mind remembers nothing. My eyes do not see, my ears do not
hear, yet I write — this is a great wonder.” (CC, Madhya 2.90)

And at the end of the book:

“To think ‘I am writing’ is a false notion: my body is like a wooden puppet. I am old and infirm, blind and deaf; my
hands shake, and my mind and intelligence are not steady. Many diseases have seized me; I can neither walk nor sit.
Tormented by the pain of five ailments, day and night I am all but dying.” (CC, Antya 20.92–94)

The “Prema-vilasa” remembers him so too: when the young Duhkhi Krishnadasa came to him, the Kaviraja was very old, his
body frail, his voice barely audible, and later he could no longer walk. Yet in these words there is neither complaint
nor pride in a creative feat: that the infirm old man goes on writing is a miracle of mercy, and he himself says that it
is not he who writes. <!-- F39 Q11 -->

## A Treasury of Teaching
Though the “Charitamrita” is written in Bengali, it is a treasury of Sri Chaitanya's teaching: in it are gathered His
sweetest pastimes and His philosophy, and it deserves the love of readers in every language. One of the biographies
notes that it has English translations and that a Hindi book, “Sri Chaitanya-charitavali,” was compiled from it, and
cautions that the book's being in Bengali does not mean its author was no master of Sanskrit. Of himself, however, he
tells very little in it. <!-- F26 -->

## Other Works
Besides the “Charitamrita,” Krishnadasa left the “Govinda-lilamrita” — a poem on Krishna's pastimes through the day and
night (*ashta-kaliya* lila), filled with a special sweetness of love for Krishna. According to one of the biographies,
it was for this work that he received the title “king of poets.” Its beginning was laid by Sri Rupa. Narahari
Chakravarti writes of him:

“At the Vaishnavas' wish he composed eleven verses and gave them to Krishnadasa Kaviraja to expand; in them are the
pastimes of the eight periods of the day, a supreme elixir; only the fortunate relish it.” (“Bhakti-ratnakara”
1.818–819)

And in every canto of the “Govinda-lilamrita” Krishnadasa says that he gained the strength to write it only through his
association with Sri Jiva. So in the “Charitamrita” and the “Govinda-lilamrita” he revealed what is most confidential;
through Raghunatha Svarupa's raga-bhajana came to him, and from it both books were born. <!-- F43 -->

His too is the “Saranga-rangada,” a commentary on Bilvamangala's “Krishna-karnamrita,” in which both his learning and his
subtle realization of the sweetness of the pastimes can be seen. One of the biographies remarks that had Gopala Bhatta
written his own commentary on the “Karnamrita,” the Kaviraja, his follower, would probably have mentioned it in the
“Saranga-rangada.” <!-- F44 -->

He also composed many hymns (stotras) in various metres. Known under his name is the “Stava-panchaka” — five verses on
the greatness of Gopala Bhatta; Yadunandana Dasa quotes and renders them in the “Karnananda”: “thus did the Kaviraja sing
the virtues of the Bhatta.” <!-- F45 -->

His three books — the “Charitamrita,” the “Govinda-lilamrita” and the commentary on the “Krishna-karnamrita” — Vaishnavas
consider priceless. To him, as to Jiva, other works are also ascribed. <!-- F46 -->

## Among the Vaishnavas of Vrindavana
At Radha-kunda Krishnadasa lived to see the coming of a new generation of preachers. When Srinivasa, having received
initiation from Gopala Bhatta, came with Jiva to Radha-kunda, he was received by Raghunatha Dasa, Raghava Gosvami and
Krishnadasa Kaviraja. Narahari Chakravarti writes that Raghava, Krishnadasa Kaviraja and others showed Srinivasa the
utmost mercy; three days he spent at Radha-kunda and Govardhana, receiving instruction in bhajana.[^9] Later, when
Srinivasa and Narottama came together to Raghunatha Dasa at Radha-kunda, Krishnadasa Kaviraja came there too. They bowed
to him as was fitting, and Srinivasa he loved as his own life (“Bhakti-ratnakara” 5.701–702). <!-- F47 F66 -->

Ramachandra Kaviraja, Srinivasa's disciple, also came to Radha-kunda: having gone round the forests of Vraja, he received
the darshan of the feet of Raghunatha Dasa and Krishnadasa Kaviraja. <!-- F48 -->

The “Prema-vilasa” also tells of the coming of Duhkhi Krishnadasa, the future Shyamananda. Raghunatha Dasa sent the
youth “to another kunja to see the Kaviraja,” and he found him in his hut absorbed in remembrance; the old man
questioned the newcomer kindly. One of the biographies adds that Lokanatha, Bhugarbha, Gopala Bhatta, Raghunatha Dasa,
Krishnadasa Kaviraja and the other Gosvamis each blessed Shyamananda and his companions for success in Mahaprabhu's
mission. <!-- F50 -->

Narottama Thakura, in the words of one of the biographies, regarded him as the jewel at the centre of the circle of
devotees after the six Gosvamis and drew inspiration from him. In one of his songs Narottama calls him the foremost of
the rasika devotees, for he described Chaitanya's life, and laments that the pastimes of Gaura and Govinda melt even
stones, while his own mind has never been drawn to them;[^10] and in a song of lament for the Lord's associates he cries:
“Where is the Kaviraja?” And the “Prema-vilasa” tells how Raghunatha Dasa spoke to Krishnadasa of Narottama's virtues.
<!-- F51 Q13 F71 -->

When Virachandra, Nityananda's son, came to Vrindavana, those who went out to meet him were Sri Jiva, Krishnadasa
Kaviraja, Ananta Acharya, Haridasa Pandita, Krishnadasa Brahmachari and others. According to the “Prema-vilasa,” the
Kaviraja at Radha-kunda also bowed to Jahnava-devi, Nityananda's shakti, and honoured her. <!-- F49 F69 -->

Sri Jiva, too, conveys his obeisances in one of his letters: “here Sri Krishnadasa bows to your company.” According to
one of the biographies, this is the fourth letter to Srinivasa; in our translation of the “Prema-vilasa” the obeisance
stands in a letter to Govinda Kaviraja. And the “Bhakti-ratnakara” (11.268) tells how Krishnadasa Kaviraja and other
Vaishnavas embraced Govinda Kaviraja as they saw him off from Vrindavana. <!-- F53 F60 -->

Narahari Chakravarti in the “Namamrita-samudra,” calling on the Lord's associates for Vaishnava qualities, addresses him
too:

“O Shishu Krishnadasa! O Krishnadasa Kaviraja! Protect me this time — I have done wicked deeds.”
(“Namamrita-samudra” 266)

Who the Shishu Krishnadasa named beside him is, is unclear; he is another person. <!-- F65 -->

## Succession and Disciples
Svarupa Damodara's raga-bhajana, the biographies say, flowed in two streams: from Raghunatha Dasa to Krishnadasa
Kaviraja, and from Vakreshvara to Gopala Guru. In the shiksha-parampara as Srila Bhaktisiddhanta sets it out,
Krishnadasa stands after Sri Jiva: Mahaprabhu — Svarupa Damodara — Rupa and Sanatana (with Raghunatha Dasa, Raghunatha
Bhatta and Gopala Bhatta) — Jiva — Krishnadasa Kaviraja — Narottama — and on to Vishvanatha Chakravarti. He is also named
among those who taught the worship of Krishna together with the *ashraya-vigraha*: Rupa, Raghunatha, Jiva, Krishnadasa
Kaviraja, Narottama. <!-- F54 -->

In his bhajana-kutir at Radha-kunda lived his disciple Mukunda Dasa; later Vishvanatha Chakravarti, too, settled there,
studying under Mukunda. According to the appendix to the “Narottama-vilasa,” the Govardhana-shila which Raghunatha Dasa
had served was, after his departure, with Krishnadasa Kaviraja, and then with Mukunda Dasa. <!-- F55 -->

## Departure
Krishnadasa Kaviraja left this world after his guru, on the same lunar day — the shukla-dvadashi of the month of Ashvina.
In the calendar of the Sri Chaitanya Saraswat Math this is Gaura-dvadashi of the month of Padmanabha: on this day
Vaishnavas honour the departure of Raghunatha Dasa, Raghunatha Bhatta and Krishnadasa Kaviraja. One of the biographies
writes: “on this day we, following the line of the servants of the servants of the Kaviraja, bow to the six Gosvamis.”
The year of his departure, by Srila Bhaktisiddhanta's reckoning, is around 1616 or 1617. <!-- F56 F57 -->

Krishnadasa Kaviraja's samadhi, according to Bhaktisiddhanta, is in the courtyard of the Radha-Damodara temple in
Vrindavana; one of the biographies names Radha-kunda as the place of his hut and samadhi. Both places are honoured; at
Radha-kunda stands his bhajana-kutir as well. <!-- F58 -->

There are also late traditions about the Kaviraja's last days.[^11] One of them, a malicious one, claims that Jiva out of
envy threw the manuscript of the “Charitamrita” into a well, and that the Kaviraja, hearing of it, at once left his
body, the book surviving only because his disciple Mukunda had made a copy beforehand. Srila Bhaktisiddhanta in the
“Anubhashya” on Adi 10.85 calls this a contemptible fabrication born of enmity towards the guru and the Vaishnavas,
without any foundation and without any possibility of being true: for Jiva is the best of the Rupanugas and one of the
Kaviraja's own shiksha-gurus. Its falsity is seen also in that the very tradition of the Kaviraja's departure on which it
is built is self-contradictory. <!-- F59 F60 F61 F62 Q16 -->

## Ratnarekha-manjari
Who Krishnadasa Kaviraja is in Krishna's eternal pastimes, Srila Bhaktisiddhanta tells in his introduction to the
“Charitamrita”: his spiritual form in Vraja is Ratnarekha-manjari, one of the young maidservants of Sri Radha.[^12]
<!-- F63 -->

To this bears witness also Radha's mercy to Vishvanatha Chakravarti. Vishvanatha himself tells in his
“Mantrartha-dipika” how for a long time he could not understand where the “twenty-four and a half” syllables of the
kama-gayatri, of which Krishnadasa writes (Madhya 21.125), come from. Without grasping the mantra he did not wish to live;
giving up food and water, he lay down on the bank of Radha-kunda, and towards midnight the daughter of King Vrishabhanu
appeared to him: “All that Krishnadasa Kaviraja has written is the truth. She is My *narma-sahachari*, My confidante: by
My grace she knows all that is in My heart. Do not doubt her words.” As for the half-syllable, Radha told him to learn of
it from the book “Varnagama-bhasvat.” Radha speaks of Krishnadasa in the feminine — this is no figure of speech: She
speaks of his eternal nature as a maidservant in Her retinue. And the vision itself is not a legend about inspiration but
Radha's mercy to Her servant and the seal of the “Charitamrita's” authenticity. <!-- F42 -->

One of the biographies concludes that this shows how dear Krishnadasa is to Radharani and that the deepest truths were
revealed to him; therefore his every word is the highest spiritual truth. <!-- I4 -->

## Significance
Srila Bhaktisiddhanta said of the “Charitamrita” that if all the books of the world were to perish and only the
“Srimad-Bhagavatam” and the “Charitamrita” remained, people would still attain the highest goal; and even if the
“Bhagavata” perished and the “Charitamrita” remained, humanity would lose nothing, for what is not revealed in the
“Bhagavata” is revealed in it. The Supreme Truth is Sri Chaitanya, the combined form of Radha and Krishna, and the
“Charitamrita” is His incarnation in sound; in it the secret of Radharani's position and glory is revealed. These words
speak also of the special position of the one through whom the book came into the world. <!-- F40 Q12 I4 -->

But the same Bhaktisiddhanta warned that very few are able to grasp the greatness of the divine sweetness of love
(*rasa*) of which the Kaviraja wrote; careless people, unready for it, usually misunderstand what he wrote, reading it in
a worldly way, and so bring spiritual downfall upon themselves. Therefore the “Charitamrita” is approached with
reverence, under the guidance of true devotees, and not as a tale of worldly love. <!-- F41 -->

The life of Krishnadasa Kaviraja is the pattern of a Rupanuga. His kin were the Vaishnavas of Vrindavana, his support the
mercy of Nityananda and the Gosvamis, and his own possession humility alone. For standing up for Nityananda's servant he
received Vrindavana; he began his book only at the Vaishnavas' order and with Madana-Gopala's permission; of those who
forbade him to write about them he kept silent; and he wrote when almost blind and deaf, regarding himself as only a
puppet in the Lord's hands. That is why Vaishnavas honour him as the best of the followers of Sri Rupa. <!-- I6 F21 -->
