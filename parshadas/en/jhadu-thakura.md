# Jhadu Thakura
*Джхаду Тхакур · @jhadu-thakura*

## A Vaishnava of Bhedo
Jhadu Thakura is known to us from a single story — of how Kalidasa, the uncle of Raghunatha Dasa Gosvami, came to his
home. Krishnadasa Kaviraja tells it in the sixteenth chapter of the Antya-lila of the “Sri Chaitanya-charitamrita” as an
example of Kalidasa's practice: he sought the remnants of the Vaishnavas' meals without regard to the family in which a
Vaishnava had been born. <!-- F10 --> At first the “Charitamrita” calls him simply “a Vaishnava named Jhadu” (Antya
16.14), and then Jhadu Thakura — and so, says one of the biographies, the Vaishnavas called him. In Bengali his name is
pronounced “Jharu,” and so in some books — and in Srila Bhaktisiddhanta's journal “The Harmonist” — he is Jharu Thakura.
<!-- F1 -->

He was born into a *bhuinmali* family — the name in Bengal of a caste of sweepers akin to the haddis.[^1] Society counted
it among the untouchables; but caste is only an outward circumstance of the body, and it does not define a Vaishnava. So
both a biography and “The Harmonist” speak of his birth with reverence: he “appeared” in that family. <!-- F2 F3 --> The
original source calls him a Vaishnava; the biographies call him a true Vaishnava, a Vaishnava of the highest order, a
great devotee. Word of his greatness as a Vaishnava had spread, and having heard of it Kalidasa came to him. Srila
Bhaktisiddhanta Sarasvati, in a heading of the “Anubhashya” to this story, calls him “the guru of all brahmanas.”
<!-- F4 -->

His shripat is the village of Bhedo, or Bhaduya, in the Hooghly district. Srila Bhaktisiddhanta locates it thus: about
three miles south of Krishnapur, where Srila Raghunatha Dasa Gosvami appeared, and about a mile west of the Bandel
junction; the post office is Devanandapur. Kalidasa's shripat was in the same village (“Anubhashya” to Antya 16.5–14).
<!-- F5 --> Krishnapur is one of the “seven villages” of Saptagrama; one of the biographies lists them as Saptagrama,
Bamshibati, Shivapura, Basudevapura, Krishnapur, Nityanandapura and Shankhanagara.[^2] <!-- F6 -->

Jhadu Thakura served a Deity, Sri Madana-gopala. In the time of the “Anubhashya,” Srila Bhaktisiddhanta writes,
Madana-gopala was served in Bhedo by a Ramayat named Ramaprasada Dasa; according to one of the biographies, He is served there to this day.[^3] <!-- F7 -->
Jhadu lived with his wife. She too was a Vaishnavi, a faithful and virtuous companion to her husband; the sources have not
preserved her name. <!-- F8 -->

Nothing more is known of his life in this world. Neither the original sources nor the biographies name the years of his
life, his parents, children, guru or disciples; it is not known when and where he left this world or where he was laid
to rest. Who he is in Krishna's eternal pastimes the “Gaura-ganoddesha-dipika” does not say. Nor are his days of
appearance and departure found in the Vaishnava calendar of the Sri Chaitanya Saraswat Math. The sources tell of no
meeting between him and Mahaprabhu Himself. <!-- F9 -->

## The Guest with Mangoes
One day Kalidasa came to Jhadu. He brought him the finest sweet, ripe mangoes of the season, bowed at his feet and paid
his respects to his wife:

“There was a Vaishnava named Jhadu, of the *bhuinmali* caste. Kalidasa went to his home, taking mangoes with him. He presented the mangoes, bowed at his feet, and then paid his respects to his wife. Jhadu Thakura was sitting with his wife; seeing Kalidasa, he received him with great respect. After talking with him a while, Jhadu Thakura said to him sweetly: ‘I am of low birth, and you are a most honoured guest. How shall I serve you? Give me leave to take provisions to a brahmana's house. There you can take prasada, and that will give me life.’” (CC, Antya 16.14–19)

<!-- F11 F12 F13 --> One of the biographies adds that Jhadu was deeply abashed: a man of high birth was bowing to him and
bringing him gifts! He could not believe his good fortune and felt he had no right to accept such honour. And he offered
to have his guest fed in a brahmana's house so as to receive a worthy guest worthily, at least as far as lay in his
power.[^4] This is not the timidity of one born low, but what Srila Bhaktisiddhanta, in the headings of the
“Anubhashya,” calls Jhadu Thakura's nature: he is *amani*, free of pride, and *manada*, giving honour to others. For
himself he seeks no honour at all, while his guest he honours as the highest.

Kalidasa heard in these words the humility befitting a Vaishnava. He called himself a fallen sinner, said that by seeing
Jhadu he had become pure and his life had borne its fruit, and asked for one thing only: the dust of his feet — let the
Thakura place his foot on his head (Antya 16.20–22). <!-- F14 I4 --> At this request, says the biography, Jhadu trembled: for
him even to hear such a thing was a sin leading to hell; joining his palms, more abashed than ever, he tried to
dissuade his guest:

“The Thakura said, ‘It is not fitting to say such things. I am of low birth, and you are a noble gentleman.’” (CC, Antya 16.23)

In the biography's retelling he begs his merciful guest not to speak so unfittingly: such words are his ruin, for he is
low already, and at the very thought he would fall lower still. Here too Srila Bhaktisiddhanta sees Jhadu's humility —
*amani* and *manada* (“Anubhashya,” headings to Antya 16.18, 16.23). <!-- F15 -->

## “I Have No Such Power”
Then Kalidasa recited to him three verses on the glory of a devotee. In the first, from the “Itihasa-samuchchaya”
(quoted in the “Hari-bhakti-vilasa”), the Lord says that a knower of the four Vedas without devotion is not dear to Him,
but His devotee is dear even if born among dog-eaters, and is to be worshipped as He Himself is. In the second Prahlada
says that a devotee of Vishnu, even a dog-eater, is better than a brahmana without devotion, and purifies his whole
family (“Srimad-Bhagavatam,” 7.9.10). In the third Devahuti glorifies those who utter the Lord's name: even if born
among dog-eaters, they are worthy of honour, for they have already performed every austerity and sacrifice
(“Srimad-Bhagavatam,” 3.33.7; CC, Antya 16.25–27).[^5] <!-- F16 Q1 -->

The “Charitamrita” says that on hearing these verses Jhadu Thakura was greatly pleased (Antya 16.24): the glory of the
devotees was a joy to him. But he would not take it to himself:

“Hearing this, the Thakura said, ‘What scripture says is true: one in whom there is devotion to Krishna is not low. But I am of low birth, and I have no devotion to Krishna. Others may be so blessed, but I have no such power.’” (CC, Antya 16.28–29)

<!-- F17 Q2 --> The scriptures are right, he said — only they do not apply to him; and such words from the lips of a
great soul would rather cast him, low and wretched as he is, into hell; let Kalidasa speak so no more.

In his “Anubhashya” to these verses Srila Bhaktisiddhanta brings testimonies from the “Mahabharata” (“Vana-parva,”
chapters 180 and 211; “Anushasana-parva,” chapter 163), the “Srimad-Bhagavatam” (4.21.12 and 7.11.35), the “Padma” and
“Garuda Puranas” and other scriptures, and shows that brahmanhood belongs eternally to a Vaishnava — not a worldly
brahmanhood, but a transcendental one (*aprakrita*). Therefore anyone of humble birth who has gained devotion to
Krishna can no longer be of low caste. <!-- F18 I2 --> And the words “I have no devotion,” Srila
Bhaktisiddhanta explains, only show Jhadu Thakura's magnanimity, worthy of a Vaishnava, and his freedom from pride.
Maha-bhagavatas do not identify themselves with the body, and by their very nature they speak thus: “All the devotees of
Krishna but me stand, by the truth of scripture, on the highest step; I alone am without devotion and born in a low
family, and I have no strength to rise higher.” And the heading to verse 16.29 says that a devotee of Krishna is free of
material self-conceit and his sense of self is transcendental. So speaks not one who lacks devotion, but one who has risen above every thought of his own
greatness. <!-- F19 I3 -->

## Mangoes for Krishna
Kalidasa bowed and asked leave to go. Jhadu Thakura, honouring his guest, walked after him to see him off (*anuvraji*,
as the “Anubhashya” explains), and then went back home (Antya 16.30–31) — to his hut, as the biography says. <!-- F20 --> But Kalidasa did not leave. He
went to the spot where the Thakura's footprints lay, smeared their dust over his whole body, seeing a Vaishnava in Jhadu
Thakura, and, longing to receive his remnants, hid near the house (Antya 16.31–32).[^6] <!-- F21 -->

Meanwhile, in the house:

“Entering the house, Jhadu Thakura saw the mangoes and offered them all to Krishnachandra within his mind. His wife took the mangoes out of their wrappings of banana leaf and bark and handed them to him, and he ate them, sucking out the juice. He sucked the stones and threw them into the wrappings; and when his wife had fed him, she ate after him. She filled the wrappings with the sucked stones, carried them outside and threw them into the pit for remnants.” (CC, Antya 16.33–36)

Kalidasa's gifts, say the biographies, Jhadu placed in a bowl of banana bark (*donga*) and offered them to Krishna within
his mind. This does not mean he “thought of Krishna,” nor is it imagination: Jhadu Thakura, “the guru of all brahmanas,”
says Srila Bhaktisiddhanta, worshipped within his mind the Deity manifest in the mind (*manomayi-archa*), and the mangoes
became Krishna's prasada; Jhadu ate them as the Lord's remnants (“Anubhashya,” heading to Antya 16.33). <!-- F22 --> His
wife took this prasada from the wrappings and handed it to her husband, and with great contentment he sucked the juice
from the fruit and threw the stones back (the wrappings, the “Anubhashya” explains, were of banana leaf and bark).
<!-- F23 --> Then she too ate — she honoured the remnants of her Vaishnava husband, as befits a Vaishnava's wife
(heading to 16.35) — and the stones and skins she gathered into the wrappings, carried out of the house and threw into the
refuse pit. <!-- F24 -->

From there Kalidasa took them out:

“Kalidasa sucked those wrappings, stones and skins, and as he went on, he grew ever more jubilant with divine love.” (CC, Antya 16.37)

What he tasted was not “scraps from a pit” but the remnants of a Vaishnava who had eaten Krishna's remnants — and,
as Srila Bhaktisiddhanta says, he saw them as transcendental (“Anubhashya,” heading to Antya 16.37). Such
remnants the “Charitamrita” calls by the highest name:

“The remnants of Krishna are called *maha-prasada*; when they become the remnants of a devotee, they are called *maha-maha-prasada*.” (CC, Antya 16.59)

<!-- F25 --> Kaviraja begins the story of Kalidasa with words about him — “he knew nothing but the name of Krishna”
(Antya 16.5–6) — and ends it with an instruction: a devotee's foot-dust, the water that has bathed his feet and what is
left of his meal — in these three lies all the strength of spiritual practice, and Kalidasa is the witness to this
(Antya 16.58–63).[^7] <!-- Q3 --> And when Kalidasa later came to Puri, Mahaprabhu let him drink the water that had bathed His
feet and gave him what was left of His meal:

“Lord Chaitanya, crest jewel of the all-knowing, knew Kalidasa's heart and his faith in the Vaishnavas. Pleased with him for that quality, the Lord showed him a mercy rarely granted to others.” (CC, Antya 16.48–49)

“Such is the greatness of the remnants of Vaishnavas,” Kaviraja concludes (Antya 16.57). <!-- Q4 --> This is told in
full in the article on Kalidasa.

## Significance
A Vaishnava, in whatever family he is born, deserves worship; whoever judges a Vaishnava by caste goes, as the
scriptures say, to hell. A Vaishnava is above the qualities of material nature, and neither nation nor caste defines
him. Jhadu Thakura is a living witness to this: born in a caste of sweepers, he is a Vaishnava of the highest order, and
Kalidasa himself, a man of high birth, begs for the dust of his feet. <!-- I1 --> “The Harmonist,” the journal published
under Srila Bhaktisiddhanta's editorship, writes (1929) that Kalidasa honoured the remnants of the meal of Jharu Thakura,
who had appeared in a family of sweepers, and that the Lord, by granting Kalidasa the water from His feet, rejected the
objection that a Vaishnava's remnants may not be honoured because of his birth, and proclaimed the boundless power of
mahaprasada to bring one who is new on the spiritual path to success. <!-- F26 -->

This is not a challenge to the social order nor a social reform, but faith in the transcendental nature of a Vaishnava.
Mahaprabhu showed Kalidasa His mercy for his faith in the Vaishnavas, not for a struggle against custom. But the lesson
here is given not by Kalidasa alone, but by Jhadu Thakura too: his humility, as Srila Bhaktisiddhanta says, is *amani*
and *manada* — freedom from pride and honouring others (headings of the “Anubhashya” to Antya 16.18, 16.23, 16.29, 16.30).
He seeks no honour for himself, yet gives it in full to his guest; whatever he receives he offers to Krishna within his
mind, and he eats only His remnants. That is why Srila Bhaktisiddhanta calls him “the guru of all brahmanas” (heading to
Antya 16.33). And in the “Amrita-pravaha-bhashya” Srila Bhaktivinoda Thakura, summarising the chapter, says that Kalidasa
received even the nectar of Jhadu Thakura's lips (*adharamrita*) — the remnants of his meal. <!-- F27 F4 I4 -->
