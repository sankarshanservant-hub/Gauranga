# Bhagavan Acharya
*Бхагаван Ачарья · @bhagavan-acharya*

## The Lame Bhagavan
Among those who left home and family to live at Sri Chaitanya's feet in Nilachala was a learned brahmana, Bhagavan
Acharya. Krishnadasa Kaviraja calls him Bhagavan Acharya or simply the Acharya, and in one place “the lame Bhagavan
Acharya”; Vrindavana Dasa calls him “Bhagavan Acharya, a great soul” (*mahashaya*); Kavi Karnapura, in the
“Gaura-ganoddesha-dipika,” calls him “Acharya Bhagavan Khanja,” that is, the lame, and in his mahakavya, “known here as
Bhagavan, the best of virtuous acharyas”; Narahari Chakravarti in the “Bhakti-ratnakara” calls him “the lame Bhagavan,” and at the opening of one of its
chapters glorifies him among the Lord's associates: “Glory… to the lame Bhagavan” (15.5). Svarupa Damodara, his close
friend, calls him a *gopa*, a cowherd boy. In the
biographies he is Sri Bhagavan Acharya and “the lame (*khanja*) Bhagavan Acharya,” and in one of them Khanja Bhagavan
Dasa.[^1] <!-- F1 F46 -->

He was indeed lame; the sources do not say why. But his lameness did not keep him far from the Lord: when Mahaprabhu once
ran headlong towards a sand hill by the sea, the lame Bhagavan Acharya, as we shall see, hurried after Him with all the
rest. <!-- F6 -->

## A Portion of Gaura, a Cowherd Boy of Vraja
Who he is, Kavi Karnapura tells us:

“Acharya Bhagavan, the lame one, is said to be a portion (*kala*) of Gaura.” (“Gaura-ganoddesha-dipika” 74)

The biographies cite this verse, and Srila Bhaktisiddhanta Sarasvati refers to it in the “Anubhashya” when he speaks of
Bhagavan Acharya. *Kala* means a portion: Bhagavan Acharya is an eternal associate who carries the Lord's power within
him and exists for His service — not the Lord Himself, nor His equal. <!-- F2 Q1 -->

In Krishna's pastimes, in Krishnadasa Kaviraja's words, he is an incarnation of a cowherd boy (*gopa-avatara*), and his
heart is filled with the mood of friendship (*sakhya-bhava*) with Krishna (CC, Antya 2.85). The sources do not give
this cowherd boy's name. Hence the affectionate *gopa* with which Svarupa addresses him. <!-- F3 -->

The “Charitamrita” names him among the branches of Sri Chaitanya's tree that grew in Puri:

“Other branches are King Prataparudra, Krishnananda of Orissa, Paramananda Mahapatra, Shivananda of Orissa, Bhagavan
Acharya, Brahmananda Bharati, Sri Shikhi Mahiti and Murari Mahiti.” (CC, Adi 10.135–136)

One of the biographies cites a further verse from a list of the branches, which glorifies Bhagavan Acharya as one whose
body is full of radiance and the mere remembrance of whom awakens love for Gaura.[^2] <!-- F4 Q2 F5 Q13 -->

## From Halisahar
Bhagavan Acharya's home was Halisahar, the ancient Kumarahatta, in what is now the district of North 24 Parganas; so also
writes Srila Bhaktisiddhanta in the “Anubhashya.” In the same Kumarahatta lived Shivananda Sena, Srivasa Pandita and
Vasudeva Datta, and the biographies, listing its residents, name among them “the lame Bhagavan Acharya.” According to
one biography, he appeared in Navadvipa and moved to Halisahar later.[^3] <!-- F7 F8 -->

His father was a wealthy man, wholly worldly. Krishnadasa Kaviraja writes:

“His father, Shatananda Khan, was deeply engaged in worldly affairs, but the Acharya had turned his back on them:
renunciation was foremost in him.” (CC, Antya 2.88)

One of the biographies speaks of the father more sharply still: he was “terribly worldly.” Bhagavan Acharya's younger
brother, Gopala Bhattacharya, went another way: he became a Mayavadi, as we shall see below. <!-- F9 F10 -->

Bhagavan Acharya himself was a man of great learning. The “Charitamrita” calls him “very learned” (*supandita*, Antya
2.84), and the biographies add that he particularly excelled in logic (*nyaya-shastra*) and received the title
Nyayacharya. <!-- F11 -->

What follows is known only from the biographies. Seeing that his son had been detached from the world since youth, the
father arranged his marriage with a daughter of Madhusudana Ghataka of Navadvipa, and two sons were born to Bhagavan,
Raghunatha and Ramanatha. Raghunatha, who was called Raghunatha Acharya, is mentioned in the “Bhakti-ratnakara” as well, and Srila
Bhaktisiddhanta, citing it, names him as Bhagavan Acharya's son. But family life could not hold the Acharya. Overcoming
its obstacles, he came to the Lord's feet in Nilachala; the Lord told him to return home, and he returned. Yet he could
no longer live in the family: entrusting his wife and sons to his brother-in-law and his own disciples, he left
home and went to Nilachala to be always at Mahaprabhu's feet.[^4] The sources do not give his disciples' names.
<!-- F12 F13 F14 F15 F16 -->

Many years later, when Sri Jahnava-devi was travelling from Khardaha to the festival at Kheturi, near Halisahar there
joined her, in the words of the “Bhakti-ratnakara,” “Raghunatha Acharya, son of the lame Bhagavan, noble in every
quality” (10.382).[^5] The “Narottama-vilasa” speaks of the same meeting and calls Raghunatha, “son of the lame
Bhagavan,” the dearest disciple of Jagadisha Pandita (6.214–215); and Srila Bhaktisiddhanta writes in the “Anubhashya”
that Raghunathacharya, son of Khanja Bhagavan, was a disciple of Jagadisha Pandita Gosvami (on Adi 11.30).
<!-- F18 Q12 F44 -->

## At the Lord's Feet
When Mahaprabhu returned from His pilgrimage through South India and devotees began to stream to Him in Puri from every
side, Bhagavan Acharya too remained with Him:

“Ramabhadra Acharya and Bhagavan Acharya also stayed at the Lord's feet, setting aside all other affairs.” (CC, Madhya
10.184) <!-- F19 Q3 -->

Govinda-deva tells the same in the “Gaura-krishnodaya”: before the bathing festival of Jagannatha, before the devotees
of Bengal had arrived, there came after Paramananda Puri, Svarupa Damodara and Brahmananda Bharati “two bearing the title
Acharya — Bhagavan and Ramabhadra; they entered Gaura's abode, and He received them with due honour” (11.29). <!-- F45 -->

Vrindavana Dasa, listing the devotees who gathered to the Lord in Nilachala, says of him:

“Bhagavan Acharya, a great soul, came too — one whom worldly matters did not touch even through his ears.” (CB, Antya
3.188)

One of the biographies, following the order of the “Chaitanya-bhagavata,” remarks that he came to the Lord after the Lord
had shown His mercy to Sarvabhauma. And Kavi Karnapura writes in his mahakavya:

“And another, a magnanimous soul known here as Bhagavan, the best of virtuous acharyas, ever bowed in devotion to Sri
Gaurachandra, served the glorious Lord Jagannatha.” (Mahakavya “Sri-Krishna-Chaitanya-charitamrita” 13.147)
<!-- F20 Q11 -->

What he was like, Krishnadasa Kaviraja tells at the beginning of his account of him:

“In Purushottama, near the Lord, lived Bhagavan Acharya, a supreme Vaishnava, very learned and noble. His heart was
filled with the mood of friendship (*sakhya*): an incarnation of a cowherd boy, he was a close friend of Svarupa Gosani.
He had taken shelter at Chaitanya's feet with single-minded devotion, and from time to time he would invite the Lord for
a meal. At home he would cook rice and many kinds of vegetable dishes, and he would bring the Lord alone and feed Him.”
(CC, Antya 2.84–87) <!-- F21 F22 F23 Q4 -->

The biographies see in him a great Vaishnava, learned, noble, exceedingly generous and simple of heart; Svarupa will say
of him, “most generous” (Antya 5.101). For this simplicity and generosity the Lord loved him especially, and treasured
him so much that He could not refuse when he invited Him to a meal. <!-- F21 F22 F23 -->

The “Charitamrita” says the same in the account of Ramachandra Puri. When, because of his reproaches, the Lord cut down
His food, in the homes of Gadadhara Pandita, Bhagavan Acharya and Sarvabhauma He still ate as they wished:

“But when Pandita Gosani, Bhagavan Acharya or Sarvabhauma invited Him, the Lord ate as they wished. In their homes He
had no independence: all was as they desired.” (CC, Antya 8.88–89)

The Lord, the independent Master, Himself gives Himself into the power of His devotees' love.[^6] <!-- F24 Q10 -->

When the devotees of Gauda came to Puri headed by Advaita Acharya, Mahaprabhu went out to meet them with His
companions; in the long list of those who joyfully went with the Lord, Vrindavana Dasa names Bhagavan Acharya too (CB,
Antya 8.54–61).[^7] And in the months of chaturmasya he, like the other brahmanas, cooked for the Lord himself:

“Gopinatha Acharya, Jagadananda, Kashishvara, Bhagavan, Ramabhadra Acharya, Shankara and Vakreshvara would from time to
time invite Him to rice cooked at home.” (CC, Antya 10.154–155) <!-- F25 F26 Q11 -->

## The Mayavadi Brother
Gopala Bhattacharya, Bhagavan Acharya's younger brother, had gone to Kashi (Benares) to study Vedanta and, his studies
completed, came to his elder brother in Puri. Bhagavan was glad that his brother had become a scholar, and brought him to
the Lord. But what Gopala had learned in Kashi was Mayavada, Shankara's interpretation, hostile to bhakti, and
Mahaprabhu knew it:

“The Acharya brought him to the Lord's feet, but the Lord, who knows every heart, found no happiness within. Because
Gopala was related to the Acharya, the Lord outwardly showed him a semblance of affection; but where there is no devotion
to Krishna, He feels no joy.” (CC, Antya 2.90–91) <!-- F27 F28 -->

For Bhagavan's sake the Lord was courteous to his brother, but His heart did not open to one in whom there was no
bhakti. Bhagavan, however, simple and straightforward, saw no danger, and proposed to his friend Svarupa Damodara:

“‘Gopala has come here after studying Vedanta. Let us all come together and hear the commentary from his lips.’” (CC,
Antya 2.92–93)

What was meant was the “Shariraka-bhashya,” Shankara's commentary on the “Vedanta-sutra.” <!-- F29 -->

Svarupa Damodara, the guardian of the purity of siddhanta at the Lord's side, answered his friend in loving anger
(*prema-krodha*):

“‘Your intelligence has been spoiled in Gopala's company, and you have taken a fancy to hearing Mayavada. A Vaishnava who
listens to the “Shariraka-bhashya” gives up the relationship of servant and served and considers himself the Lord. Even
a *maha-bhagavata*, whose life's treasure is Krishna, will surely have his heart turned by hearing Mayavada.’” (CC,
Antya 2.94–96) <!-- F30 Q5 -->

Srila Bhaktisiddhanta Sarasvati sums up the meaning of these verses in the subheadings of the “Anubhashya”: here Svarupa
Damodara, the guru of all Vaishnavas, describes the faults of Mayavada and shows how one who desires to serve Vishnu
purely should regard Shankara's Mayavadi commentary; and next, “how strong the poison of Mayavada is; one who hears it
is in danger of falling.” Shankara, Bhaktisiddhanta explains, established his Mayavada on speculation, and therefore his
commentary is wholly opposed to pure bhakti. Hence the lesson the biographies too draw from this account: even the
greatest devotee, whose life is Krishna, is not proof against this poison; hearing Mayavada corrodes the heart and can
lead one out of bhakti, and so one who seeks pure service keeps far away from the Mayavada-bhashya. <!-- F30 I1 -->

Bhagavan objected to his friend:

“The Acharya said, ‘Our hearts are fixed on Krishna; a commentary cannot turn our minds.’ Svarupa said, ‘Even so, what
one hears in Mayavada is only this: “Brahman is pure consciousness, and maya is false; the very notion of the jiva is
imaginary, and in the Lord there is nothing but ignorance.” When a devotee hears this, his heart and soul break.’” (CC,
Antya 2.97–99) <!-- F31 Q6 -->

In the “Anubhashya” this answer too has its heading: “Svarupa reveals the meaning of Mayavada, which tears the heart of a
pure devotee.” Bhagavan's words about firm faith were not empty: his heart truly was given to Krishna. But Svarupa
teaches that the question is not whether Mayavada will shake a steady devotee: merely to hear that the Lord is subject to
ignorance and that the soul is a fiction is torment for one who loves the Lord. <!-- F31 -->

And Bhagavan Acharya accepted his friend's lesson:

“Ashamed and afraid, the Acharya fell silent, and the next day he sent Gopala back to his native place.” (CC, Antya
2.100)

His shame and fear were not the fright of a culprit, but the humility of a devotee who has heard instruction and at once
carries it out. Svarupa did not accuse his friend but protected him; and this account is a lesson not for Bhagavan but
for us: whom and what one who seeks pure devotion should hear. Nor is Gopala a villain: he is a devotee's brother, and
the Lord was courteous to him; the danger lay not in him but in the teaching he brought. <!-- F32 -->

## Rice from Madhavi-devi
Bhagavan Acharya's name is linked with another lesson the Lord gave in Puri — the story of the Junior Haridasa:

“One day the Acharya invited the Lord. At home he cooked rice and made various vegetable dishes. The Lord had a singer
named Chota Haridasa. The Acharya himself called him, brought him over and said, ‘Go in my name to Shikhi Mahiti's
sister, ask her for one *mana* of white rice and bring it.’” (CC, Antya 2.101–103)

A *mana* was a measure of grain used in Utkala; the biographies specify that the rice was fine. Shikhi Mahiti's sister was
Madhavi-devi — an elderly ascetic, a supreme Vaishnavi, whom the Lord counted among the intimate companions of Srimati
Radharani: of the “three and a half” such companions in this world, she is the “half.”[^8] <!-- F33 -->

The Acharya had already brought much prasada from the temple of Jagannatha, but he wished to cook many dishes for the
Lord at home as well, and for this he needed good rice. When he received it he was overjoyed, and with affection he
cooked it together with the Lord's favourite dishes. At midday Mahaprabhu came, tasted the meal and asked where the
Acharya had got such rice. He replied that it had been begged from Madhavi-devi, and when asked who had gone for it, he
named the Junior Haridasa. Praising the rice, the Lord finished His meal, and on returning to His residence He told
Govinda: “From today, do not let the Junior Haridasa come here.” <!-- F34 F35 -->

When, three days later, the intimate devotees asked the reason, the Lord answered:

“‘A renunciant who converses with women — I cannot bear to see his face. The senses are hard to restrain and seize
their objects; even a woman made of wood steals the mind of a sage. <…> Petty souls adopt a monkey's renunciation and
wander about, letting their senses graze and conversing with women.’” (CC, Antya 2.117–120)[^9] <!-- F35 Q7 F36 -->

Bhagavan Acharya was in no way at fault: he only wished to feed the Lord better. And Madhavi-devi, an eternal companion
of Radha, was beyond every worldly suspicion. The Lord's strictness is a lesson for those who have accepted
renunciation: a *vairagi* does not converse with women, whoever they may be, and the Lord, the teacher of the world,
showed this law through His own singer. <!-- F35 -->

## The Bengali Poet's Drama
Once there came to Puri a Bengali brahmana, an old acquaintance of Bhagavan Acharya, and lodged with him. He had
composed a drama about Sri Chaitanya and wished to recite it to the Lord. One of the biographies calls him a mundane poet
in the guise of a brahmana, another a poet from East Bengal. First he recited the play to Bhagavan Acharya and other
Vaishnavas, and they all liked it. <!-- F37 -->

But Mahaprabhu had a rule: He would hear a song, verse or book only once Svarupa Damodara had first heard and approved it,
for a mixing of incompatible rasas (*rasabhasa*) or anything opposed to siddhanta roused His anger (CC, Antya
5.95–98). So Bhagavan Acharya asked Svarupa to hear the drama. He replied:

“‘You are a simple “cowherd” (*gopa*), and most generous: you wish to hear any writing whatever.’” (CC, Antya 5.101)

And Svarupa explained: in the words of a poet who does not know what rasa is there is rasabhasa; what is opposed to
siddhanta brings no joy to hear; only one for whom Gaura's feet are the treasure of life can describe the pastimes of
Krishna and Gaura — like Sri Rupa, the mere openings of whose plays bring happiness (Antya 5.102–108). <!-- F38 F39 Q8 -->

Here too Svarupa's reproach is loving: “you are generous,” he says — and indeed Bhagavan's readiness to hear anyone came
from breadth of heart, not from lack of discernment. Two or three days Bhagavan kept urging his friend, and at last he
agreed (Antya 5.109–110). <!-- F40 -->

The poet recited his opening verse (*nandi-shloka*):

“He of golden lustre has here found His self in the One named Sri Jagannatha, whose eyes are like blossoming lotuses, and
has appeared, bringing consciousness to the whole of inert nature. May that Krishna Chaitanya-deva grant you good
fortune.” (CC, Antya 5.112)

Svarupa asked him to explain it, and the poet said that Jagannatha is a lovely body and Sri Chaitanya its
indweller; He has appeared in Puri to bestow consciousness on the inert world (Antya 5.114–115). Everyone was pleased; only
Svarupa answered in anger:

“‘O fool, you have brought about your own utter ruin! You have no faith in either of the two Lords! Lord Jagannatha is
full of bliss, a form of pure spirit, yet you have made Him out to be an inert, perishable, material body! Chaitanya,
full in all six opulences, is Svayam Bhagavan, the Supreme Lord Himself, yet you have made Him out to be a tiny jiva,
like a spark! <…> You have made yet another great blunder (*pramada*): by distinguishing between body and embodied in
the Lord, you have committed an offence! In the Lord there is never any difference between body and embodied.’” (CC,
Antya 5.117–122) <!-- F41 Q8 -->

Sri Jagannatha is the Lord Himself, the Daru-brahman manifest as the Deity, not a wooden image; and the Lord's body is
not different from Him. One of the biographies, retelling this dispute, names two offences of the poet: he saw a jiva in the Lord and
separated the Lord's body from the Lord Himself; but the first thing Svarupa says in the “Charitamrita” is that the poet
made Sri Jagannatha out to be an inert body of prakriti. The assembly was amazed. And Svarupa, seeing the poet's shame,
took pity on him and instructed him to hear the “Bhagavata” from a Vaishnava and take shelter at the Lord's feet:

“‘Go and study the “Bhagavata” under a Vaishnava, and take exclusive shelter at the feet of Chaitanya. Always keep the
company of Chaitanya's devotees; then you will come to know the waves of the ocean of *siddhanta*.’” (CC, Antya
5.131–132)

He also revealed in the poet's verse a hidden praise that Sarasvati had placed in his mouth, and the poet gave himself up to
the devotees of the Lord. <!-- F41 F42 Q8 -->

## At Chataka Hill
Krishnadasa Kaviraja recalls Bhagavan Acharya also in the account of how Mahaprabhu, on His way to the sea,
caught sight of the sand hill Chataka and, thinking it was Govardhana, rushed towards it with the speed of the wind:

“A cry went up, and there was a great uproar. Everyone, wherever he was, got up and ran. Among them were Svarupa,
Jagadananda, Gadadhara Pandita, Ramai, Nandai and Shankara Pandita. Puri Gosani and Bharati Gosani came to the
seashore, and the lame Bhagavan Acharya followed very slowly.” (CC, Antya 14.88–90)

Lame, falling behind everyone — and yet hastening to where the Lord had sped: so the “Charitamrita” remembers him.[^10]

## Departure
When and where Bhagavan Acharya left this world, the sources do not say; neither the day of his appearance nor the
place of his samadhi is known, and his days are not found in the Vaishnava calendar of the Sri Chaitanya Saraswat Math.
<!-- F17 -->

## Significance
Bhagavan Acharya is a portion of Gaura and a cowherd boy of Vraja, a friend of Krishna; in Mahaprabhu's pastimes he is a
friend of Svarupa Damodara and one to whose wishes the Lord submitted at table. He left a wealthy home, wife, sons and
scholarly renown to live at the Lord's feet. His magnanimity was so great that he was ready to hear anyone — the brother
who brought Mayavada from Kashi, and the poet he knew; and so his friend Svarupa twice lovingly protected him, and
through him all of us. Simplicity and generosity of heart made him especially dear to the Lord. And his obedience to his
friend, his meals for the Lord and his slow walk in His wake to Chataka hill speak of a devotion hindered neither by
lameness nor by learning. <!-- F2 F3 I2 -->
