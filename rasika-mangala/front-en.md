# Sri Rasika-mangala

*"The Auspicious Song of Rasika"*

*A poem by Gopijana-vallabha Dasa (Bengali, 17th century). Four vibhagas, sixty-four laharis*

*Translated from the Bengali into English*

---

## Translator's Note

The *Sri Rasika-mangala* is a Bengali poem on the life of Rasikananda (Rasika-Murari, 1590–1652), the disciple of Shyamananda who, together with his guru, turned Utkala — Orissa, Mallabhum and the forest country of Jharikhanda — to Krishna. Its author, Gopijana-vallabha Dasa, is the son of Rasamaya of Dharenda, one of the first Shyamanandis; as a child he himself played one of the eight sakhis at the great Rasa-yatra in Gopivallabhapura (Pashchima, lahari 2) and describes much as an eyewitness. According to the colophon the poem was begun on Vasanta-panchami of the ninth regnal year ("anka") of the Gajapati Balabhadra and finished two years and eight months later (by the editors' reckoning, 1658–1660), that is, some six to eight years after Rasikananda's departure.

**What the book contains.** The poem is divided into four parts — the Purva (eastern), Dakshina (southern), Pashchima (western) and Uttara (northern) vibhagas — of sixteen laharis ("waves") each. The Purva-vibhaga contains the vandana of the associates of Shyamananda and Rasikananda, Shyamananda's birth and youth, his initiation by Hridayananda at Ambika and his pilgrimage; Rasika's birth at Rohini (Dipavali of 1512 Shaka), his childhood, studies and marriage, his vision of Krishna in the forest and his meeting with Shyamananda at Ghatshila. The Dakshina-vibhaga tells of Rasika's initiation, his stay in Vraja, the founding of Gopivallabhapura, the conversion of Dharenda, of Raja Vaidyanatha Bhanja and of Uddanda Raya, and gives long teachings on bhakti and on the jiva. The Pashchima-vibhaga describes the Rasa-yatra at Gopivallabhapura, the deliverance of the maddened elephant at Banapur, the submission of the governor Ahmad Beg and of the Gajapati, Shyamananda's departure (1552 Shaka) and the festival of separation with a list of all the branches of the Shyamanandis. The Uttara-vibhaga tells of the community's annual festivals, the troubles in Shyamananda's family, the journey to Nilachala and Jagannatha's chariot moving at Rasika's touch, and finally Rasikananda's departure — his entering the Deity of Kshirachora Gopinatha at Remuna. Much of this — names, dates, the customs of the community — is known only from the *Rasika-mangala*.

**Source.** The translation follows the edition of Sripat Gopivallabhapura (ed. Gopala-Govindananda Deva Gosvami, 2nd ed., 455 Chaitanyabda = 1941), from the scan; for the Purva and Dakshina vibhagas the edition of Kishori Dasa Babaji (Halisahar, 1997), set from the same edition, was also consulted, and its variant readings are noted. **Only the text of the poem** is translated; the editors' prefaces and notes have not been used.

**How the translation was made.** The couplets (payar and tripadi) are translated one by one, keeping the division into lines; words supplied for sense are in square brackets; the Sanskrit mangala-shlokas at the beginning of a lahari are marked "S1", "S2"… The couplet numbers are those of the edition (separately in each lahari); the titles of the laharis follow the poem's colophons. The italic summary at the head of each lahari is the translator's. Doubtful readings are discussed in the notes; questions for a specialist in Bengali are collected separately.

**The author and Gaudiya Vaishnava teaching.** Gopijana-vallabha writes as a devotee of his gurus and of their house, and his praise at times goes beyond theological precision: he calls Shyamananda "Mahaprabhu" and "Prabhu" and speaks of him as Srimati Radhika Herself, descending age after age; of Rasika his characters say "a second Narayana," "a partial incarnation," "Ishvara." The translation renders these words exactly, softening nothing, and in the notes briefly states the Gaudiya Vaishnava position according to Srila Bhaktisiddhanta Sarasvati: the Lord is Krishna Himself, who appeared as Sri Chaitanya; Shyamananda is His eternal servant and the servant of Radha, the disciple of Hridaya Chaitanya; Rasikananda is a great devotee and associate, not an incarnation of the Lord. The birth and departure of such mahatmas are not subject to karma: Rasikananda's departure at Remuna is a disappearance, an entering into the lila of the Deity, not a merging (sayujya). Likewise marked are the places where the author, as the genre requires, narrates Krishna-lila (the rasa-lila, the gopis' separation): the gopis' prema is not mundane love (kama) but the pure desire to give joy to Krishna. With these reservations the *Rasika-mangala* is a priceless witness to the life of Utkala and Bengal in the 17th century and to the faith and customs of a community that grew from Sri Chaitanya's preaching.
