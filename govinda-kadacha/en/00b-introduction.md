## The Language of the Karcha

The objectors say that the language of Govinda Dasa's Karcha is modern. As has already been said, they have set up the Chaitanya-charitamrita as the standard in every field, as historical evidence, in philology and in theology alike, and it is in the light of this standard that they judge the language[^1]. They ought to know that the language of the Chaitanya-charitamrita is not pure Bengali at all. Kaviraja Gosvami went to Vrindavana at the age of sixteen <!-- p. 46 --> and set about composing the Chaitanya-charitamrita at eighty-seven. Because he lived in Vrindavana continuously for those seventy-one years and for six more years after that, his language mixed with Hindi and became a khichuri [a hotchpotch]. Here is a sample of that language:

> "Tell me," he says, "how Rupa and Sanatana live,  
> how they practise renunciation, how they worship,  
> how for all eight praharas [round the clock] they worship Sri Krishna."  
> Then, praising them, those devotees say:  
> "Homeless, they live beneath the trees,  
> under each tree sleeping for a single night only.  
> Only a water-pot, a torn quilt and a loincloth;  
> talk of Krishna, the name of Krishna, dancing and rapture."  
> (Chaitanya-charitamrita, Madhya, ch. 19)

Seeing the poems that Bengali poets composed in Brajabuli in the sixteenth century, many people have also fallen into the error of supposing that the Bengali language of that time was like that. In fact the Brajabuli of the Bengali poets is a wholly artificial language.

In this country the language of the village does not change very much in 400 or 500 years. I saw my grandfather, and my grandsons are alive. One may suppose that there was no great difference between my grandfather's language and my great-grandfather's. Over these six generations (200 years by the customary reckoning) the language has certainly changed somewhat, but not very much. In 400 years a language does not become very hard to understand. If someone were to write a book in pure Bengali, it would not look markedly different from the language of today.

Language differs for several reasons. First, through mixture with other languages. Seeing how abstruse the language used by some Sanskrit pandits even in this twentieth century is, one might guess that it was the language of Sriharsha's time. Pandit Mrityunjay wrote: "That Malaya breeze, garrulous with the sweet chatter of cuckoos, comes enveloped in spray from the crystal-clear waters of the surging cascades." There is a vast difference between the language of Michael's [Madhusudan Dutt's] poem Tilottama-sambhava and the language of Rabindranath's Kshanika, and yet the two books are practically contemporary (in reckoning the history of a language, a gap of 30 or 35 years does not count).

The second reason why language differs is regional peculiarity. Not long ago an advertisement for a medicine was published in one part of Bengal. The advertisement used the language of the country where Nabin Chandra Sen wrote The Battle of Plassey, where Nabin Chandra Das made his mellifluous Bengali translation of the Raghuvamsha [i.e. Chittagong]. Here is a passage from the advertisement: "Iba dabai na? Iba bar gam dabai. Ab kalte bhair poar lai ek ana di ek durga ning khabailam je, ajjer andaj chir bair hail. Ar gura poare khabaite kon bhoy nai." [Approximate sense: "Isn't this a medicine? This is a fine medicine. Yesterday [?] I bought one anna's worth for my brother's son and gave him a dose [?], and today a good quantity of [worms?] came out. And there is no danger in giving it to small boys."] This piece was in use twelve or fourteen years ago <!-- p. 47 --> in a corner of Bengal; some inexperienced writer of today, reading it, might think it was a Prakrit of Ashoka's time.

In any one particular place, language does not change very much in 400 or 500 years. In centres of trade, where many foreigners come and go, various languages mix and produce a complex, mixed tongue. But in the secluded villages of Bengal no rapid or radical change in language is to be seen even over a thousand years.

In the writings of learned authors the language of earlier books creeps in unnoticed. That is why archaic forms of words turn up here and there in the language of learned writers. Govinda Dasa's book-learning was slight. He wrote in pure, spoken Bengali, and that is why his language is so simple.

Those who have fixed on the Hindi-laden Bengali of the Chaitanya-charitamrita and the Maithili-mixed Bengali of Brajabuli as their model of sixteenth-century language will go wrong at every step. In printed books the language often looks very ancient because of a different spelling. For instance, if the word "paiya" ["having got"] is written as "paiña" or "pyaña", the latter forms seem older. But at bottom there is no real difference in pronunciation between these variants. Likewise, if "ek" ["one"] is written "yek", or "laiya" ["having taken"] is written "laña", the eye is dazzled, but in actual speech no particular difference is noticeable between these forms.

When our pandits in former days set about editing old manuscripts, they would change the manuscripts' use of such letters as "ña" [ঞ]. Their aim was that people should have no trouble reading the book; they did not trouble their heads about philology. If one compares the old manuscripts of Krittivasa and other poets that are to be found with the printed books of Battala, signs of such changes will be found on every page. If the old rules of spelling are kept, the book will seem older even though the language is the same. What the publishers did with Krittivasa and the rest, Jaygopal Goswami did with the Karcha to some extent. His aim was not to make any great change in the manuscript. By altering archaic words here and there he made the book easy to understand.

The singers have simplified to some degree the poems of Chandidasa that are found today, and yet they are still known under Chandidasa's name. The same applies to authors such as Krittivasa and Vrindavana Dasa.

This is the form in which we have the poems that Chandidasa composed at least 125 years before Govinda Karmakar:

> (1) "After many days you have come, beloved;  
> had we not met, death would have come.  
> The unhappy one's days passed in sorrow;  
> but you, in Mathura, you were well.  
> Of my own joys and sorrows I know nothing:  
> your welfare I count as my welfare."

> (2) "Friend, who made me hear the name of Shyama?  
> Through the ears it entered my very heart, ah, and set my soul in turmoil.  
> I know not how much honey is in Shyama's name, ah;  
> my lips cannot let it go.  
> Repeating and repeating the name I have grown helpless, ah;  
> how shall I win him, friend?"

> (3) "Beloved, what more shall I say?  
> In life and in death, birth after birth, be the lord of my life.  
> To your feet the noose of love has bound my soul.  
> Surrendering all, with a single heart, I have surely become your slave."

> (4) "Who says that love is good?  
> Laughing and laughing I fell in love, and my life has passed in weeping."

A little after Chandidasa, before Lord Chaitanya's birth, the poet Narahari of Srikhanda composed verses like these: "In the courtyard remains the golden necklace of my heart; may my beloved wear it on his neck just once. With my own hand I planted jasmine; string a garland of its flowers and put it on him."

Vrindavana Dasa, almost a contemporary of Govinda Dasa, uses language like this in his Chaitanya-bhagavata: "Vishvambhara dances, the Lord of all, along the banks of the Bhagirathi. The dust of his feet, in delight, all placed upon their heads. Wondrous transformations, nectar from his eyes; hearing his roars and thunder, laughing and laughing, arms raised, they cry 'Hari, Hari!'"

The Ramayana of Krittivasa is at least 60 or 70 years older than Govinda Dasa's Karcha. Everyone knows what shape its language has taken in the Ramayana printed at Battala. Still, I give a few samples:

> (1) "Sita in the middle, the two great heroes before and behind;  
> two kroshas of road they travel along the bank of the Ganga.  
> Sri Rama says: 'Near Bharadvaja  
> we shall lodge today free from danger.'  
> Surrounded by munis sits Bharadvaja,  
> as the king of the twice-born [the moon] shines among the stars.  
> At that time the three arrived there;  
> the three bowed to the sage's feet.  
> Sri Rama says: 'Hear, O great sage,  
> we three will make ourselves known to you.  
> We two are the sons of Sri Dasharatha;  
> my name is Sri Rama, the younger is Lakshmana.'"

> (2) "'Where are kinsmen and friends, who else is left?'  
> he ponders once more in his mind.  
> 'Virabahu was [as if] in heaven; he came and died.  
> Whom shall I send? I can think of no plan.  
> Indrajit is gone; who will go to battle?'  
> Streams of tears flow from his twenty eyes.  
> <!-- p. 49 --> In wounded pride his body has wasted, his face is darkened;  
> now he rises, now he sits, King Dashanana.  
> Again and again he swoons and falls to the ground:  
> 'At last, it seems, Parvati and Shankara are forsaking me.'  
> Ravana's mother, Nikasha by name,  
> weeping and weeping, went to Ravana.  
> Grieving at heart for love of her child,  
> the old woman admonishes Ravana in every way:  
> 'Your brother Vibhishana is most righteous;  
> he came to counsel you, and you kicked him.'"

> (3) "Fallen on the ground, King Vali writhes.  
> Rama ran up to him,  
> as a hunter who has killed a deer runs to it;  
> Rama ran up to Vali's side.  
> With bloodshot eyes Vali looks at Sri Rama,  
> grinds his teeth and reviles him:  
> 'Tara forbade me in every way,  
> and I trusted a chandala, taking him for a good man.  
> You were born in a royal house, yet you have no sense of dharma;  
> you have slain me, Rama: by what law is this?  
> What country did I plunder, whom did I cause pain?  
> For what fault have you ended my life?  
> You were not born in some other line, you were born in Raghu's line;  
> all praise you as righteous.  
> What righteous deed is this that you yourself have done,  
> to destroy my life without offence?  
> All say Ramachandra is the abode of mercy;  
> all your mercy is made manifest on me!  
> In the guise of an ascetic, Rama, you roam from forest to forest,  
> always thinking whose life you shall take.'"

The aged pandit of Shantipur, Sriyukta Harilal Goswami, has written that many people know the old manuscript of the Karcha was in the possession of Jaygopal Goswami, "but I have doubts about the Karcha's being sixteen annas [i.e. wholly] original."

Why only he? I know for certain that the Karcha printed by Jaygopal Goswami is not sixteen annas genuine. He himself admitted as much to me. Like other editors of old manuscripts, he altered to some extent the vernacular conventions of the old spelling. Besides that, here and there he also replaced obsolete words, and wherever he found some irregularity in the payar metre he regularised it by taking out or putting in a word or two. In those days, as soon as an old handwritten manuscript went to the press its dress was changed in this way; it would not be right to blame the Karcha alone for this offence.

If, despite such changes, Chandidasa, Krittivasa, Kavikankana, Kashidasa and other old poets are accepted, for what fault should the Karcha remain an outcaste? On the contrary, the Karcha has been changed far less than the works of the poets named.

As has already been said, when the Karcha was printed, Jaygopal Goswami's eldest son Banwari Lal was not less than forty years old. He helped his <!-- p. 50 --> father in every respect in this work. The manuscript was worm-eaten in places, and in some places the text could not be read. The old Goswami filled in those places by relying on conjecture, and here and there he replaced obsolete words with modern ones. But on the whole these changes are not many. As for the places altered in this way, Banwari Lal Goswami, so far as he remembers them, has in good faith restored their old readings in this edition.

Many Hindi words are found in old Bengali. I am not speaking of Brajabuli or of the language of the Charitamrita; the influence of such Hindi words is to be seen in genuine old Bengali manuscripts too. Vijaya Gupta's Padma-purana is a genuinely Bengali book. In it too one finds Hindi words such as "jetake", "tetake", "pokheri", "dono". In poems of Chandidasa such as "If the power of his name has done this much, ah, what will the touch of his body do? Staying there where he lives, ah, how shall a young woman keep her dharma?", Hindi words are used. In the Karcha too there are such Hindi words here and there, for example "bhog lagaila", "bat", "puchhe", "kanha shata shata gopi, kanha sei nat" ["where are the hundreds of gopis, where is that dance?"]. Again, the Karcha has a number of words in very ancient usage, for example "rage dagamaga prabhu deya santaran" ["brimming with raga, the Lord swims"], p. 5. Here *raga* means not anger but attachment, love. Nowadays, of course, the original meaning of *raga* is no longer met with in Bengali; it has come to signify anger.

## "After Sannyasa Mahaprabhu, far from touching women, did not even speak with them"

Besides the episodes of Satyabai and Lakshmibai, the Karcha tells of the deliverance of the courtesan Baramukhi and of temple servants such as Indira. According to the objectors, after taking sannyasa Mahaprabhu did not speak with any woman, and therefore the Karcha is a forgery.

Because Chaitanya cast off Junior Haridasa for the offence of speaking with a woman, some also say that after sannyasa he not only kept away from women himself <!-- p. 52 --> but would drive away any of his followers who spoke with a woman. Junior Haridasa was a young man with a fine voice and handsome looks. What Chaitanya did on perceiving some weakness in him, he did not apply everywhere indiscriminately as a rule. Ramananda Raya, for one, mixed a great deal with the devadasis of Puri. For this reason Vasudeva Sarvabhauma and many others mocked and ridiculed him as a "Sahajiya Vaishnava". "Maharaja, sa khalu sahaja-vaishnavo bhavati, purvam ayam asmakam upahasa-patram asit" ("Maharaja, how much we used to ridicule Ramananda, calling him a Sahajiya Vaishnava!" — words of Vasudeva, Sri Chaitanya-chandrodaya-nataka, Act VII, 5). Everyone knows how Chaitanya loved this Ramananda Raya. Of Nityananda he said: "Even if someone should prove that Nityananda drinks wine and is attached to a yavani [a Muslim woman], my faith in him will remain unshaken." So it is not right to say that he turned against anyone who spoke with a woman or touched a woman. He did not weigh the elephant and the sparrow in the same scale. People of every sort judge him by their own measure.

Nor is it true that after sannyasa he did not touch women or speak with them. The Chaitanya-charitamrita itself says: "Shathi's mother by name, the Bhattacharya's wife; devoted to the Lord, in affection she is a mother" (Chaitanya-charitamrita, Madhya, 15.174). When Shathi's mother and Sarvabhauma were dejected after the quarrel with Amogha, "seeing the sorrow of both, he consoled them both; at the wish of both he ate, well pleased" (Madhya, ch. 15, verse 33 [as given in the original]). If Chaitanya did not speak with Shathi's mother, how could he have consoled her? Haricharana's Advaita-mangala says that after taking sannyasa Chaitanya went to Advaita's house and talked at length with Advaita's wife Sitadevi about the food: "Mahaprabhu says: 'Sita, today you will have your hands full [?].'" "Sita says: 'As much rice as you want, so much there will be; by your grace nothing is lacking'" (Advaita-mangala, no. 10). The Chaitanya-charitamrita itself says: "In the temple of Puri various instruments sound, the devadasis dance." The devadasis were enacting a play about Mahalakshmi: "Seeing the boldness of Mahalakshmi's maidservants, the Lord laughed together with his companions" (Chaitanya-charitamrita, Madhya, 14.151). Kavi Karnapura too wrote in the Chaitanya-chandrodaya-nataka of his enjoying the performance of these devadasis. At Nilachala a woman, in her eagerness to see Jagannatha, pushed through the crowd and climbed onto Mahaprabhu's shoulder; when his attendant wanted to get her down, Mahaprabhu forbade him, saying: "Let her see Jagannatha to her heart's content." The Chaitanya-charitamrita further records that, hearing a song of Jayadeva from the lips of a temple servant, Chaitanya in a trance ran to embrace her. Had he not <!-- p. 53 --> come to his senses on being stopped by Govinda, he would certainly have embraced that woman, just as in trance he trampled Tirtharama underfoot and touched Satyabai. On this point Achyutacharan Tattvanidhi has written: "If the devotee Govinda, who was close by, had not caught hold of him and prevented him then, what would have happened? Would not the same thing have happened as in Satya's case?" (Sri Sri Vishnupriya-Gauranga Patrika, year 3, no. 5, p. 158).

Thus the Chaitanya-charitamrita, the Chaitanya-chandrodaya-nataka and other books prove that (1) Mahaprabhu spoke with women even after sannyasa; (2) when a woman touched him, he did not forbid it; (3) he not only watched the performances of the temple servants but enjoyed them; (4) in a trance he even ran to embrace one of the temple servants. After this, any attempt to raise a hue and cry over the episodes of the Karcha and so inflame people is a mere waste of effort. He came down to deliver the fallen. To try to put a fence across the stream of the Mandakini that flowed down from heaven to carry them away on a flood of love is a mistake. He did not descend into the world to go round only to the houses of the virtuous. In the unbounded current of his love all were caught, women and men alike. Chaitanya's love was like the boundless sky! The attempt to impute narrowness to it and make of him a "one-eyed" god of love will not be accepted by educated people, and history will not admit it. By the touch of his spiritual body, purified by the love of God, sinners and sufferers were saved; to imagine the least trace of impurity in that body is sheer madness. Placing our trust in Mahaprabhu's promise of universal love, we recite these few lines of Govinda, words from Sri Chaitanya's own lips:

> "Chandala, youth, householder, child, old man, woman,  
> drunk with the Name, will stand in rows.  
> The boy will say 'Hari', the girl will say it;  
> the stone and the Aghorapanthi will be drunk with the Name.  
> Piercing the sky the banner of the Name will fly;  
> king and subject together will roll in the dust."

The objectors go about saying that in the Karcha Chaitanya is portrayed as a Sahajiya. The word "Sahajiya" is mostly used in Bengali society in a contemptuous sense. Sahajiyas are those who regard the love of a woman as the only means of attaining the highest goal.

In the Karcha Chaitanya refutes this doctrine in many places: "People cry 'love, love', but who knows love? Is the essence of love the service of a woman? When you know that man and woman are not different, then the essence of love will shine forth in the heart. *** He who yearns for Atmarama [God, who delights in Himself], can lust remain in his mind? As near light no darkness remains, <!-- p. 54 --> so near Krishna lust is burnt to ashes" (p. 10). Condemning the dissolute he said: "With your lips you say another's wife is like a mother to you; find her alone and at once you are blinded by lust" (p. 10). Of those given to worldly pleasures and attached to other men's wives he said: "With the mind set on another's goods and another's wife, how will one then worship Krishna?" (p. 18). And again: "A woman's love is like poison; the fool drinks it, calling it nectar" (p. 34).

Everywhere in the Karcha there is such a refutation of the Sahaja doctrine, and yet the objectors, aiming to rouse people against the book, go about spreading groundless slander. In one place the Karcha says: "There are one or two intimates besides, with whom he performs secret worship [gopan bhajan]" (p. 4). By wringing these words "secret worship", the objectors have extracted a smell of Sahajiya from them; but their own "authoritative book" puts it in almost the same way: "With his intimates [antaranga] he tastes rasa; with outsiders [bahiranga] he performs the sankirtana of the Name." Do these two expressions, "secret worship" and "tasting rasa", not mean the same thing? The Antya section of the Chaitanya-charitamrita describes at length how Chaitanya tasted with Svarupa Damodara the hidden rasa of the lila of Radha and Krishna; while that rasa was being tasted, outsiders could not go there. When Rama Raya [Ramananda] began to speak of that secret rasa-lila, Mahaprabhu stopped his mouth. The inner meaning of this "secret worship" is given in the Karcha itself: "Like the longing of a young man on seeing a young woman, I can think of no other longing like it. Therefore devotees worship Yadupati [Krishna], fixing their minds on him in the attitude of a wife" (p. 10). "As a beautiful heroine, seeing a beautiful hero, looks on him filled with passion [ragatmika], in that same spirit call on Krishna again and again, and the darkness of the mind will go of itself" (p. 60). Before Rupa, Mahaprabhu described the taste of that hidden rasa in the verse from the Vasishtha-Ramayana, "A woman whose heart is set on another, though busy with her housework..." and so on.

So this charge of Sahajiya is not the sincere conviction of the objectors' hearts. It is a deliberate twisting of meaning with the aim of discrediting the Karcha.

## Did Krishnadasa Go to the South with Chaitanya?

The Chaitanya-charitamrita records that a brahmin named Krishnadasa went to the South with Mahaprabhu. There is no mention of Govinda Karmakar there. So those who regard every last anusvara and visarga of the Charitamrita as scientifically sound historical evidence take the absence of any mention of Govinda Dasa in that book as a principal argument against him.

<!-- p. 55 -->

That a brahmin named Krishnadasa followed Chaitanya for part of the way is found in the Karcha itself (p. 21).

Kavi Karnapura's Chaitanya-chandrodaya-nataka and Vrindavana Dasa's Chaitanya-bhagavata are both authoritative books esteemed by the whole Vaishnava community, and both are earlier than the Chaitanya-charitamrita. Neither of them mentions a brahmin named Krishnadasa going to the South. The Chaitanya-bhagavata makes no particular mention of the South. But the Chaitanya-chandrodaya states plainly that Chaitanya did not permit any brahmin to go to the South with him. The brahmins who went part of the way with him were obliged, on reaching the bank of the Godavari, to turn back at his command. When King Prataparudra expressed his displeasure with Vasudeva Sarvabhauma for not having appointed a brahmin companion to accompany Chaitanya on his southern travels, that foremost of pandits told the king plainly that Mahaprabhu had refused to take any brahmin with him, and that the brahmins who had followed the Lord as far as the Godavari had therefore been obliged to come back. These words of the Chaitanya-chandrodaya firmly prove that Krishnadasa went only as far as the bank of the Godavari. So, accepting the testimony of the Karcha as true, we can say only this: Krishnadasa accompanied the southern journey for part of the way (as far as the bank of the Godavari). Relying on hearsay about this brahmin's going part of the way, the author of the Chaitanya-charitamrita made him Chaitanya's companion on the long journey.

It is particularly noticeable that Krishnadasa goes to Nadia and gives Shachidevi the news of the Lord's return to Puri. In this connection it is quite possible that at a later time someone gave Kaviraja Gosvami wrong information. None of the very old books that their authors wrote in Bengal proper, the Chaitanya-bhagavata, Jayananda's Chaitanya-mangala, Kavi Karnapura's play, Lochana Dasa's Chaitanya-mangala and others, mentions Krishnadasa as the Lord's companion on the southern journey. So we cannot accept as authoritative a tradition reported by the Charitamrita alone.

The Charitamrita was written about 104 years after the Karcha[^2]. Its author, the aged Kaviraja Gosvami, lived continuously in Vrindavana from the age of sixteen to the age of ninety-three. The road to Vrindavana was then very difficult. So he had to rely to a large extent on hearsay. <!-- p. 56 --> Rupa and Sanatana lived in Vrindavana, and the little they knew about Mahaprabhu at first hand was certainly authoritative, but the tradition about other matters does not rest on a very firm foundation. The author of the Chaitanya-charitamrita, in recording Chaitanya's southern travels on the strength of mere hearsay, confessed his fault and begged pardon, and also expressed the fear that the order of events in his account had been broken (Madhya 7.105; Madhya ch. 9, 8; Madhya ch. 10, 175–176). His account of the southern travels is extremely brief; even the writer in the Prabasi has had to admit that Krishnadasa [Kaviraja] kept no order. He writes: "If the names in the Charitamrita are marked on a map, one can see clearly that after the return to Puri the names of a number of holy places were simply given from memory." It is very strange that the writer of the article spreads his net of arguments against the Karcha by relying on this incomplete, disjointed, brief account, full of breaks in sequence. None of the books, Lochana Dasa's Chaitanya-mangala, Jayananda's Chaitanya-mangala, Kavi Karnapura's Chaitanya-chandrodaya or the Chaitanya-bhagavata, mentions any Krishnadasa as Chaitanya's companion on his southern travels. Had Krishnadasa been his companion throughout the southern journey, they would certainly have mentioned it. Lochana Dasa, on the other hand, mentions several times Chaitanya's being accompanied by a brahmin named Krishnadasa on the occasion of his journey to Mathura. In this matter, it seems, "Udo's lump of rice has landed on Budho's neck" [a Bengali proverb: one man's doing is laid at another's door].

## The Geography of the Karcha

The geographical account that Govinda Dasa has given of Mahaprabhu's southern travels is so detailed and so real that no one who reads it can help placing trust [?] in this venerable guide.

From the Karcha [the start of the line is barely legible in the scan] it appears that Chaitanya, setting out from Puri, travelled through the following places one after another. He left Puri on 7 Baishakh, 1510 CE.

From Alalnatha to the town of Trimanda on the bank of the Godavari, then the chief centre of the Buddhists; there he turned Dhundirama Tirtha, a dweller on the Tungabhadra, to the path of bhakti; then from Trimanda by way of Pancha-guha [?] to Siddhavateshvara (near the town of Cuddapah); then to Vateshvara near the town of Panna; there the deliverance of Tirtharama. A stay of seven days at Vateshvara and a journey to Nandishvara. Then the crossing of a forest 20 miles across, then to the town of Munna (the town of Munna is on the bank of the river Munna; Munna is near Madras); from Munna to the town of Venkata (between the towns of Siddhavateshvara and Tripadi), <!-- p. 57 --> a stay of three days there; then, in a forest called Bagula, the deliverance of a robber named Panthu Bhil. Three days spent beneath a tree without food, in a state of divine madness. From Bagula to Girishvara, with a stay of two days there. From Girishvara to the town of Tripadi (Tripadi is 40 miles north-west of Madras); from there to Panna-Narasimha, then to Vishnu-Kanchi (Conjeeveram, 47 miles south of Tripadi); from there to Kalatirtha and Sandhitirtha; then to Chaipalli (Trichinopoly); from there to Nagar (145 miles to the east, on the sea-coast). Then to Tanjore[^3], Tanjore being 14 miles south of Nagar; then, crossing the Chandalu hills, to Padmakot (60 miles north-west of Tanjore); then to the town of Tripatra (60 miles south of Padmakot); from Tripatra the crossing of a forest called Jhariban, 300 miles across, which took a fortnight. Having crossed the forest, to Rangadham (modern Srirangam, south-west of Tripatra), where he saw the image of Nrisimha; from Rangadham to the town of Ramnath (on the coast, very near Rameshvara). From Ramnath by way of the Rishabha hill to Rameshvara[^4]. From there to the Madhavi forest and, crossing the Tamraparni, to Kanyakumari, and from there to the town of Trivanku (Travancore); from Trivanku to Payoshni (modern Panoni); from there, passing Matsyatirtha, Ramagiri, Kachar, the river Bhadra and Naga-panchapadi, to Chitol (the modern fort of Chitaldrug, on the northern border of Mysore); from Chitol to Chandapur, the town of Gujjari (not Gujarat, but near the State of Hyderabad), the country of Kandar, and then to the town of Purna (Poona; the river near it is still called the Purna); from Purna, passing Choleshvara and Devaleshvara, to the town of Patas, and from there to Jejuri; here the story of the unfortunate muraris, the temple servants of the god Khandoba, is told. Then, in Choranandiban, the deliverance of a brahmin robber named Naroji; crossing the river Mula, to Khandala, then to Nasik; from Nasik to Trimbuk (modern Trimbak); from there to the town of Daman; crossing the river Tapti, to the town of Broach; from there to Baroda; the death of Naroji and the description of the splendour of Ahmadabad ("wondrous Ahmadabad, city of splendour"); the crossing of the river Shubhramati [Sabarmati]; the meeting with Ramananda and Govindacharana of Kulin-gram, and taking them along; <!-- p. 58 --> then at Ghogha the deliverance of Baramukhi, Jafarabad, then the journey to Somnath. After Somnath, Junagadh, the crossing of the Gunar [Girnar?] hills; from there Amarapuri, Gopitala, Raivataka and Prabhasa. On 1 Ashvin to Dvaraka; on 16 Ashvin from Dvaraka to the town of Dohad on the Narmada, and from there to Kukshi, Amjhora, Mandura, Devaghar (not Vaidyanath), Shinani, Chandipur, Raipur, Vidyanagar and Ratnapur, and, crossing the Mahanadi, entering Svarnagarh; from there Sambalpur[^5], Pamra, Pratapnagar[^6], Daspal, Rasal-kunda, Rishikulya, Alalnatha, and from there Puri.

From this account it is known for certain that Chaitanya, starting from Puri, travelled in order through the whole southern part of the east coast and then visited the west coast in order as far as Gujarat. From Gujarat he returned to Puri almost in a straight line by the level road along the Narmada and the Vindhya hills. He set out for the South on 7 Baishakh 1510 CE and returned to Puri on 3 Magh 1511 CE. So this journey was accomplished in 1 year, 8 months and 26 days.

Because, as in a traveller's daily journal, a variety of curious incidents is described at nearly every place, the story of these almost two years of Mahaprabhu's life stands out so vividly that no other biography contains a single picture so real. To our eyes this account is more engaging even than Mandeville's description of Jerusalem, Narahari's of Navadvipa and Mathura, or Jaynarayan's of Kashi.

## Finding Fault with the Geography

The Karcha says: "Having given bhakti to the courtesan Baramukhi, the Lord hastened off to see Somnath. Towards Jafarabad the Lord goes; with great hardship, in three days he reaches that place." The objector in the Prabasi says (Shravan 1332, p. 478): "From Ghogha to Jafarabad is somewhat more than 160 miles as the crow flies [literally 'by the air route']. Even if one imagines a metalled straight road, it is impossible to cover 53 or 54 miles a day. 'Rising in the morning, we go to Somnath; after six days we reach there.' From Jafarabad to Somnath is at most 60 miles. Covering these 60 miles took six days, and covering the 160 miles just before took three days!!!"

We could not quite make out his survey "by the air route". This time the affair is an out-and-out "theft of a pond" [Bengali idiom: barefaced fraud on a grand scale]. We have looked at a good many maps, and all of them <!-- p. 59 --> agree. For convenience of checking I cite the map of India made by J. C. Walker and published by John Murray of Albemarle Street. Every map shows that from Ghogha to Jafarabad is 77½ miles, not "over 160 miles". We have fixed the number of miles by reckoning the route very exactly, and there is no possibility of error in it. So from Ghogha to Jafarabad they travelled at 25⅚ miles a day. Govinda Dasa writes that they covered this stretch with great hardship in three days. The reason for the "great hardship" too can be guessed by looking at the map. Between Ghogha and Jafarabad no village or town of any consequence is to be seen. So that hilly, uninhabited tract had to be crossed very quickly. Of course, walking 25⅚ miles of road a day is no great matter. By making 77½ miles out to be over 160 miles the writer does achieve his object, but the truth is grossly falsified. From Jafarabad to Somnath is 60.7 [?] miles on the map. If one goes in a leisurely frame of mind, looking well at all the sights, these sixty-odd miles might take not merely six days but fifteen. Especially as, after having toiled 25⅚ miles a day just before, weariness on the road is only natural.

## The Dispute over Ata-chuna

The writer in the Prabasi has raised all manner of sophistical arguments, holding it impossible that Lord Chaitanya received ata-chuna as alms in Travancore and on the bank of the Kaveri. He says that ata-chuna is not to be had in that country: "For this one fault alone the Karcha can be called unhistorical and fictitious." His arguments seem so strong to himself that in many places he has used language of this kind. But the point about ata-chuna is as hollow as the conclusion that it is "over 160 miles from Ghogha to Jafarabad". We put the question to Professor Rao Bahadur Anantakrishna Iyer of Palghat in South Malabar. From his home one can walk to Travancore in a day, so he is particularly well informed about everything in that region. My question was whether ata-chuna was likely to be found in their country in or about 1510 CE. He said: "The food prepared by mixing three things, rice flour, wheat flour [maida] and the meal of fried pulse [kalai-shuti], is known in our country as 'ata-chuna'. The native people of the Travancore region ate it in old times and eat it still. It is eaten stirred into water with sugar or gur [jaggery]. On journeys it is the mainstay." Rao Bahadur <!-- p. 60 --> Iyer has also communicated this to me in a letter, which is given in the footnote[^7].

It now appears that it is not safe to accept any of the writer's conclusions, and so there is no point in wrangling about this.

But even if ata-chuna had not existed in that country in this form, even if it had meant only wheat flour, there could still have been many unknown reasons why that article was readily available there 415 years ago. Suppose that at that time, whether because of the unrest caused by wars with the Muslims or because they were drawn by the unprecedented glory of the Hindu kingdom of Vijayanagara, many people from the north-west had reason to go and settle in the South. They may in the end have become rice-eaters; but in the first one or two centuries they would have eaten the food customary in their own country, and so wheat flour should have been readily available there at that time.

Be that as it may, since incontrovertible proof has been found that what they understood by "ata-chuna" was in very common use in that country at the time, there is no need to sharpen imaginary weapons and do battle.

[^7]: [In English in the original:] "Atta Chuna is a kind of flour—a mixture of wheat, rice and the green pulse fried and powdered. It is an ordinary diet for persons who go on long journey. It is taken with sugar or jaggery after mixing with a little water. It was and is even now an article of diet with orthodox men and women."

## King Rudrapati

In the history of Travancore the name of King A. Ravivarma is found for that period, but in the Karcha the king's name is written as Rudrapati, and over this they have raised a great hue and cry.

A copper-plate grant of 1510 CE shows that the king of Travancore at that time was Martanda Varma. He issued that grant from the Virapandyan palace in the capital Kalkand [Kalkulam?]. But P. Shungoonny [Menon] has written in his History of Travancore that Martanda Varma was not reigning at that time, and that the king then was A. Ravivarma. Martanda Varma reigned after A. Ravivarma, from 1528 to 1537 CE. So there is confusion as to who was king at that time. Travancore was then (until 1565 CE) a small kingdom subject to Vijayanagara. And, as an educated and respectable gentleman of that country has informed me, there were moreover many chiefs in this small kingdom at that time. So it is not known whether Chaitanya went to the territory of some such petty chief among them.

<!-- p. 61 -->

The name found in the documents of kings is a formal name. But among the common people they are often known by other names. Alamgir and Aurangzeb, Salim and Jahangir, Mehr-un-nisa and Nurjahan: such a profusion of names is seen everywhere in the genealogies of kings and princes. The historian of Sylhet writes that the popular names and genealogies of the Dewans of Baniyachang hardly ever agree with the names given in other documents. Many instances of a similar disagreement are found with regard to the Dewans of Jangalbari too, as I have set out at great length in the introduction to the Purbabanga Gitika [Eastern Bengal Ballads].

Prataparudra, the king of Orissa at that time, is usually styled "Gajapati". One of his predecessors as king there is called "Bhramara" in Bengali literature, and yet "Bhramara" is not his name but only a title; that king's name was Kapilendra Deva.

At this period the kings of Vijayanagara had so many titles that many Western writers have made fun of them[^8]. The kings of Travancore were then subject to Vijayanagara. When the emperor had so many titles, the vassal kings could be expected to have something similar. The objector has said that the kings of Travancore were Vaishnavas and that the title "Rudrapati" was not possible for any of them. If one examines the history of this period, one finds that three kings reigned one after another in Travancore: Martanda Varma, A. Ravivarma and Udayaditya Varma. All these names are solar [saura]; not one of them is Vaishnava. Above all, if "Rameshvara" can mean Shiva, what objection can there be to "Rudrapati" meaning Vishnu?

<!-- p. 62 -->

Historians have told us very little about the history of Travancore at this period. To answer this doubtful and tangled question one would need to know the contemporary history of the kings and petty chiefs of the Travancore state in minute detail, and for that a great mass of material would have to be sifted. Abandoning the arduous road to this impossible task, the objector has reached an easy verdict in a single phrase: "For this reason alone the Karcha is unacceptable."

Sharpen his argument and turn it upon the Chaitanya-charitamrita. In the eighth chapter of the Madhya section of that book, at verse one hundred and thirty, the author has written "Bardhanarudra" in place of "Prataparudra". Now if the Chaitanya-charitamrita were rejected for this one reason alone, what would the writer say?

## Lord Chaitanya's Need of a Brahmin Cook, and Varnashrama

The objectors say (Prabasi, no. 4, pt. 25, vol. 1, p. 480) that if the brahmin Krishnadasa did not go with Mahaprabhu, who would cook his food for him? "We have never heard of anyone taking a brahmin cook with him after accepting sannyasa. Rupa, Sanatana and Jiva too were born in brahmin families," yet there is no account of their taking a brahmin cook along. From the Buddha to Srinivasa and Narottama, and even down to Lala Babu, no one is seen anxious to take a brahmin cook along.

As has already been said, Kavi Karnapura states in plain words that Chaitanya did not allow the brahmins who followed him as far as the bank of the Godavari to go on with him. When Prataparudra reproached Sarvabhauma, saying, "Why did you let him go without a brahmin companion?", Sarvabhauma replied that because of Mahaprabhu's prohibition no brahmin had been able to follow him (the King: "Brahmanas tavad a-setubandha-paryantam kim u na preshitah?" ["Why then were brahmins not sent with him as far as Setubandha?"]; the Bhattacharya: "Tat tu ananumateh godavari-paryantam eva" ["Since he did not permit it, only as far as the Godavari"]. Chaitanya-chandrodaya-nataka, Act VII).

In short, some modern Vaishnavas, in their excessive eagerness to re-establish the dharma of varnashrama, want to turn this living god-man into a stone image inscribed with shlokas. They are making him subordinate to the smriti-writers and smaller than they. Reading the life of Chaitanya, one sees that he did not keep the rules of the order of sannyasa. Breaking the rules of sannyasa, he tasted Jagannatha's bhoga again and again to nourish his prema-bhakti, and for this reason Sarvabhauma <!-- p. 63 --> was at first displeased with him: "As often as Jagannatha takes his meal, so often does that sannyasi eat. When a man eats so much in his youth, how can his lust be stilled?" (Lochana Dasa's Chaitanya-mangala, Madhya section).

When Sarvabhauma reproached Chaitanya for disregarding these rules of sannyasa, he said with the greatest humility: "Do not take me for a sannyasi, truly." "Give up regarding me as a sannyasi" (Chaitanya-bhagavata, Antya). Damodara Pandita, when he saw departures from the dharma of sannyasa, used to admonish him ("I am a sannyasi, yet Damodara the brahmachari always stands over me with the rod of correction," Chaitanya-charitamrita, Madhya, ch. 7, verse 19). It is true that he belonged to the Madhva sampradaya, but he did not follow the doctrine of Madhvacharya. Madhvacharya laid stress on the majesty [aishvarya] of God. But who does not know that Mahaprabhu was devoted to the sweet [madhurya] lila of God?

It is of course true that, for the preaching of religion, he engaged Sanatana, infinitely skilled in the shastras, to compose the great Vaishnava book Hari-bhakti-vilasa, with precedents, according to the teachings of the ancient rishis. But he himself stood above all the smriti-shastras; from a divine world far above humanity he sang a wondrous, rapturous song of love, at which the immovable moved and rivers flowed backwards.

Though Rama Raya [Ramananda Raya] was a shudra, Chaitanya embraced him. According to the Chaitanya-charitamrita, the brahmins did not like this. They said: "We see this sannyasi's radiance is like that of Brahman; why does he embrace a shudra and weep?" (Chaitanya-charitamrita, Madhya, ch. 8, 16 [?]). In fact the dharma of varnashrama that Chaitanya had loosened was once more raising its head in Vaishnava society. Krishnadasa Kaviraja took this passage from Kavi Karnapura's book, but the latter does not contain this astonishment of the brahmins. Some of those who oppose the Karcha have hinted that without the brahmin Krishnadasa beside him Mahaprabhu would have been in great difficulty abroad. He embraced not only Rama Raya; he embraced the shudra Sri Govinda too.

He had kept Nityananda engaged in Bengal to establish brotherhood by overstepping the bounds of caste; Nityananda was the "artless Maheshvara" [Shiva], with boundless compassion for the fallen. Chaitanya understood that he was the fittest of all for the work of delivering the fallen. That is why he did not let Nityananda leave Bengal and stay anywhere else for long. How the people of the lower orders might win a little regard in a society proud of its high birth, this he would discuss with Nityananda in private behind the bolted doors of the house (Chaitanya-bhagavata).

<!-- p. 64 -->

Nityananda, after entering the householder's life, ate rice not only from the hands of Uddharana Datta of the gold-merchant caste; he ate in the homes of all, Haris and Doms included ("There was no caste in whose house he did not eat," Chaitanya-bhagavata, ch. 24). When Kalidasa, the crest-jewel of the Kayastha line, picked up out of a drain a mango left half-eaten by a Bhuinmali named Jharu, sucked it and thereby struck a thunderbolt on the head of caste distinction, Chaitanya, the god of love, approved this act and encouraged him. Kalidasa not only ate Jharu's leavings but smeared the dust of his feet on his body, proving the hollowness of caste distinction. Chaitanya praised him for it. That the Goswamis today give the mantra to all castes without distinction and eat in everyone's house is the fruit of the generosity of that Lord, purifier of the fallen, compassionate to the lowly. True Vaishnavas used to take the reproaches of the Shakta poet as flowers and sandal-paste: "Drunk with joy, crying 'Gaura', the thirty-six castes eat together: Bagdi, Hari, Dhopa, Kalu, all together." It is a great pity that the Vaishnavas, who have bathed in this Ganga of joy, are once again delivering themselves into the clutches of caste pride. The kirtan singers proudly sing "All that is unlawful is Nadia's law"; what is unlawful or unscriptural elsewhere is "the law of Nadia". In the Chaitanya-charitamrita we see that the Lord gave the Yavana Haridasa honour and respect equal to a brahmin's at a brahmin's shraddha, and at his death made the assembled company of brahmins drink the water that had washed the feet of that best of devotees. In a voice deep as thunder he proclaimed: "I have no caste, my servants have no caste" (Chaitanya-bhagavata, Antya, ch. 11 [?]). To destroy the pride of the sannyasi pandits, Chaitanya had the shastras expounded by the shudra Rama Raya: "To destroy the pride of the sannyasis and pandits, he makes dharma manifest through a lowly shudra" (Chaitanya-charitamrita, Antya 5.34 [?]). Nowadays that world-embracing, liberal tradition of Vaishnava society is narrowing. Ishvara Puri repeatedly called himself "the lowest of shudras" [shudradhama]. Now in some editions of the Chaitanya-bhagavata I find "kshudradhama" ["lowest of the mean"] written in place of the reading "shudradhama". I have no doubt about the learning of some of the editors. They themselves have written that they edited the Chaitanya-bhagavata after consulting many old manuscripts. Elsewhere in their footnotes they have given variant readings, but here, even if "kshudradhama" was in some manuscript, having written it they have not mentioned any other variant. We have seen many old manuscripts of the Chaitanya-bhagavata, and in all of them we have found the reading "shudradhama". On this point Sriyukta Basanta Kumar Chattopadhyay, M.A., in charge of the University's department of old manuscripts, has written to me [?]: "I have read through many of the manuscripts of Vrindavana Dasa's Chaitanya-bhagavata kept in the manuscript library of the University of Calcutta. In this book Ishvara Puri introduces himself as 'I am the lowest of shudras'. It is clearly written on p. 72 of manuscript no. 481, on p. <!-- p. 65 --> 36 of manuscript no. 482, on p. 50 of manuscript no. 473 and on p. 66 of manuscript no. 472."

Chaitanya received the ten-syllable mantra from the great devotee Ishvara Puri, and extolled him as the holiest of shrines, greater than all tirthas. If such a man were of a caste other than brahmin, that would of course be against the social order!

Chaitanya was born in a brahmin family, but no family could set its mark on him. He was the world-loving family deity of all families. To remove the inequality between himself and all the varnas he gave up the shikha and the sacred thread. He was a devotee of Krishna, but he was not what we now understand by a "Vaishnava". He did not paint his tilaka with a mirror before him and with careful measurement, nor did he use any kind of body ornament. In the Chaitanya-chandrodaya-nataka Kavi Karnapura writes: "No bathing, no rules for serving the tulasi, no contemplation of the chakra, no taking of the name, no bowing, no praises" (Act X). He did not step inside the bounds of rules to enter any sampradaya. The whole earth was his family. As family, the Vaishnavas were not strangers to him, but he gave Vaishnava society no opening to proclaim him exclusively its own. Shaivism, Shaktism and other creeds were no obstacle on his royal road of love. That is why he did not hesitate to pay homage to his Beloved, taking now the name "Hara", now the name "Bhavani". It is laughable to see the excessive eagerness of the preachers of varnashrama to attach a brahmin cook to this great sannyasi as his indispensable companion. Haricharana wrote of Mahaprabhu in his Advaita-mangala: "Varnashrama and chandalas and the rest he made one."

## Chaitanya's Matted Locks

Nowadays, when images of deities are made, it is not the custom to give them beards. On hearing that Advaitacharya had a beard ("his beard had come down, covering his breast"), a Goswami of Khardaha was greatly upset, because the image of Advaita at Shantipur has no beard. It is the modern custom of this country to imagine those who are gods in youthful form. But that Advaitacharya had a beard is found not only in the Karcha but in many old songs too. The Gaurapada-tarangini mentions his hair spreading down over his breast. [illegible: the start of the line is covered by a blot] I draw attention to the picture given on p. 757 of the first volume of the Banga-sahitya-parichaya. On the other hand, those who are familiar with the youthful, beardless and moustacheless images of Chaitanya and Nityananda would certainly be somewhat surprised if they saw the old pictures. In one old pata painting Lord Nityananda is given a huge beard.

<!-- p. 66 -->

So the statement that Chaitanya had grown matted locks [jata] did not please many orthodox Vaishnavas. The Karcha mentions Chaitanya's jata five months after his taking sannyasa. When they had to travel for a long time, sannyasis used to wear artificial jata, and do so still. On pilgrimage there is no provision for shaving the head ("Abroad, on pilgrimage, on the death of mother or father, the hair is to be shaved; one should not be shaven needlessly," Prayashchitta-tattva). The practice of wearing jata before a long journey has come down from before the time of the Ramayana. Ramachandra himself put on jata on the very first day of his going to the forest: "So be it: I shall go from here to dwell in the forest, wearing jata and bark-cloth, keeping the king's vow." Krittivasa makes particular mention of Rama's wearing jata. The Prabasi writes that such wearing of jata is the act of a false sannyasi. So in his opinion Ramachandra too was a fraud. He has written: "The Lord who was so incensed at hypocrisy..." and so on. Relying on the argument that Rama was a kshatriya but Chaitanya a brahmin, he rejects the precedent of Rama. Even in comparing him with Rama, Chaitanya's brahminhood must be paraded!

## Discrepancies between the Accounts of the Charitamrita and Other Books

Some have considered the Karcha unauthentic on the ground that "Chaitanya, having gone to Travancore, did not see the temples of Adi Keshava and Janardana situated some distance away, and came away without obtaining the Karnamrita." Govinda knew plain Bengali. Sanskrit he did not know at all, and so wherever learning was concerned he remained dumb. He laments that he could not write down even a hundredth part of the discussion that Lord Chaitanya had with Rama Raya. Can we expect from an ignorant servant even such a small detail as that Mahaprabhu obtained the Karnamrita? Why, on going to certain holy places, he did not see certain temples, we would ask him if we could. There may be a hundred and one reasons for not seeing them. Can anyone ever swear that he saw all the temples of the South? Even the books earlier than the Chaitanya-charitamrita, such as the Chaitanya-chandrodaya and the Chaitanya-bhagavata, say nothing about obtaining the Karnamrita or seeing the temples of Adi Keshava and Janardana. What does the objector say about that? Even so big a thing as Mahaprabhu's seeing the temple of the Lord of Dvaraka is not in the Charitamrita; the objector has forgotten this, and has not spent a word on it.

<!-- p. 67 -->

Even leaving aside such supernatural matters as that Mahaprabhu became Ananta-deva and Advaita Prabhu saw "great nagas with seven hoods, arms raised, singing praises with all their hoods lifted", or that he took the form of a lion and was tearing the Kazi's breast with his claws, even what is related in a comparatively natural way in the Charitamrita and similar books cannot at times be believed in full. The author of the Charitamrita, describing Lord Chaitanya's body, has written that by the measure of his own huge hand his body was four cubits [hasta] in length and in breadth (Chaitanya-charitamrita, Adi 3.31, 5.96). That makes his height nearly 9 feet. Most of the doors in the houses of Calcutta householders are 6½ or at most 7½ feet high. In such circumstances, had he had to enter a room in any of these houses, he would have had to crawl on all fours. But it is astonishing that those who are raising a great uproar about the pedigree of such petty matters as whether "shrigali" stands in place of "shiyal" [two words for "jackal"] and whether the Lord wore artificial jata have not given a moment's thought to the fact that if everything in the Chaitanya-charitamrita is accepted, it becomes impossible even for the Lord to enter a house. Those who grow impatient whenever the Karcha disagrees with the Charitamrita on any point never once notice in how many places the Chaitanya-charitamrita disagrees with the Chaitanya-bhagavata, the Chaitanya-chandrodaya and the Chaitanya-mangala. The Chaitanya-bhagavata says that on the night before taking sannyasa Mahaprabhu was not with Vishnupriya ("Haridasa and Gadadhara lay down near him," Chaitanya-bhagavata, Madhya, ch. 26). But the description Lochana Dasa gives on this occasion of Mahaprabhu's night with Vishnupriya comes close to Bharatchandra in taste (of course, if one says "lila", no one has anything left to say): "Again and again, twining her creeper-arms about him, she embraces him, like a fresh lotus in the trunk of a lordly elephant. ** The playful lover enjoys many rasas; let alone others' doings, it is beyond even Kama's ken. ** He lays her upon his breast, not upon the bed; they cannot turn on their sides, the two are one flesh. Breast to breast, mouth to mouth, they pass the night; at the end of rasa both fall happily asleep" (Lochana, Chaitanya-mangala, Madhya section). The Chaitanya-mangala states that four days after taking sannyasa Chaitanya met Shachidevi at Advaita's house in Shantipur, but in the Chaitanya-bhagavata we find that for twelve days after the sannyasa Shachidevi lay at Navadvipa without food, and that then Nityananda came back and for the first time informed her of Chaitanya's taking sannyasa. The Chaitanya-mangala records that after sannyasa Mahaprabhu went to Navadvipa again ("At his mother's word he went again to Navadvipa; he showed mercy near his own home"). This does not agree with the other biographies. The Chaitanya-bhagavata has no mention of the Lord's southern travels nor the name of his companion Krishnadasa; and Kavi Karnapura, who wrote in Puri itself (from where Mahaprabhu set out on his southern travels) shortly after his disappearance, wrote that no brahmin went with Mahaprabhu into the South beyond the bank of <!-- p. 68 --> the Godavari. And yet Kaviraja Gosvami, sitting in distant Vrindavana a century later, writes that a brahmin named Krishnadasa wandered through the South with Mahaprabhu. The old poet Balarama Dasa, writing at about the same time as the Charitamrita, says that Govinda was Mahaprabhu's companion on the southern journey, and yet the Charitamrita does not say so. Now that this is the age of historical criticism, when the days of accepting anything blindly are gone and some orthodox Vaishnavas have sat down to sit in judgment on Govinda Dasa, it will be necessary to sit in judgment on all of them.

As has been said before, what false outcry was once raised about travellers like Mendes Pinto and Marco Polo, and even now the attempt to prove Shakespeare a fraud has not been entirely extinguished. On the charge that no manuscripts or books in the great poet's own hand have been found, an agitation is still going on that all his plays and poems were written by Bacon[^9].

To us the outcry against Govinda Dasa seems to be just such an agitation. This book has come to occupy so large a place in modern Vaishnava literature <!-- p. 69 --> and its roots have gone down so deep that to throw it out now on the strength of a few baseless assertions is next to impossible.

It is not I alone: those who have girded their loins to clear the rubbish out of the Vaishnava religion have also harassed Dr Rabindranath Tagore with letters. In the Pallivasi of 24 February 1926 it is written, concerning the blow Dr Rabindranath has dealt the Vaishnava religion: "One's heart trembles even to think of the terrible upheaval that the leading men of society, unable to hold to the ancient current and making many mistakes, have now brought about in every department." The last letter they wrote to Rabindranath Tagore, on 6 Shravan (1331), has been published in the same Pallivasi. Quoting a few verses from the Gitanjali, they wrote to him: "We must know your final word on how far it has become you to make ducks and drakes of the inner meaning of Vaishnavism in the manner described above."

In rendering an account before this great tribunal, the great poet may well have palpitations of the heart. Nagendranath Basu of the Vishvakosha is also being persecuted. The Pallivasi of 24 February 1926 reports that another warrant has been issued against him; I quote a passage from it: "In the dictionary called the Vishvakosha you have imputed Sahajiya leanings to the spotless characters of Srila Krishnadasa Kaviraja Gosvami Prabhu, author of the Sri Sri Chaitanya[-charit]amrita, and of the old composers of padas and the acharyas."

The Amrita Bazar Patrika of 22 May (1925) reports that Sriyukta Rakhalananda Thakur, a descendant of the famous Narahari Thakur Prabhu of Srikhanda, the family guru of the Vaishnavas, has been attacked on the charge of Sahajiya on the strength of [Narahari's] views.

Besides this, they are also agitating to have it believed that celebrated old books such as the famous Bhakti-ratnakara, the crown-jewel of Vaishnava literature, Jayananda's Chaitanya-mangala and Nityananda [Dasa]'s Prema-vilasa are wholly untrustworthy. They have moulded Mahaprabhu to their liking like a wax doll, and, lest any breath of wind should melt that image, they take every precaution and play the patron to the object of their worship. A correspondent of the Gaudiya of 6 Chaitra 1332 writes: "Dr Dinesh Babu in prose and Dr Rabindranath in prose and verse have expressed no true meaning of the name 'Vaishnava'." But where did they find proof that Dr Rabindranath and this humble writer are Vaishnavas?

Since, in setting out to clear the rubbish from the Vaishnava religion, they have served notices on a great many books, from the Gitanjali to the Bhakti-ratnakara and the Vishvakosha, it may be said that the Karcha is certainly in good company. <!-- p. 70 --> Of course, to clear away so much rubbish and so save the Vaishnava religion and Mahaprabhu as well, a great deal of money will be needed. What are they doing about that?

I shall give one more example of how the opponents are carrying on this propaganda. Maharaja Manindra Chandra Nandi Bahadur, after reading their inflammatory articles, wrote to a certain Vaishnava: "Dinesh Babu is destroying the Vaishnava religion." When I wrote to the Maharaja Bahadur about this, he informed me in reply that he had written so under the sway of a passing mood, and that the person concerned had not done well to [make use of] such a letter without his permission.

The position of the disciples of Vaishnava gurus is somewhat precarious. If a guru says something, it is a sin for the disciples to doubt it. But that does not mean that one who writes history can make a palm tree of a sesame seed and a sesame seed of a palm tree.

The chief charge against me is that I am destroying the Vaishnava religion. In the petition that went to the Government of Bengal this was a special charge.

But whatever one or two orthodox Vaishnavas may say about this, the view of outsiders is different. Professor A. C. Underwood published a long article entitled "Chaitanya and the Bengal Sect" in the Calcutta Review, no. 295, of 1919; I give the substance of one passage of it[^10] — "After this, with the publication of the books of Rai Sahib Dinesh Chandra Sen, the path of our knowledge of Vaishnava literature became much easier. In 1911 his History of Bengali Language and Literature, written in English, was published. As soon as this book, the fruit of his enormous labour, appeared, it won the esteem of famous European scholars such as Barth, Senart, Rhys Davids, Grierson, Barnett, Kern and Oldenberg... It is generally <!-- p. 71 --> agreed that the Vaishnava theme occupies a considerable place in Dinesh Babu's Bengali Language and Literature. He then took up the Vaishnava theme again in books such as Chaitanya and His Companions, The Vaishnava Literature of Medieval Bengal and the Banga-sahitya-parichaya. Although the author is not himself a Vaishnava, he is deeply devoted to Vaishnava religion and literature, and in writing on this subject he has shown abundant sympathy and an overflowing poetic power. But his natural historical sense has always kept his pen in check. In Mr Sen's books we have obtained such rich materials on the Vaishnava religion of the age of Chaitanya as were inaccessible before." Dr Sylvain Lévi, in the preface to my book Chaitanya and His Age, after praising this unworthy writer in many ways, has called me a "religious fanatic" (fanatic) for my devotion to Chaitanya; and the very aged historian Mr Beveridge, after saying many kind things in the Journal of the Royal Asiatic Society about my history of Bengali literature written in English, showered ridicule on me for my devotion to Chaitanya. And here in Bengal itself the cry has gone up that I have ruined the Vaishnava religion!

## A Fuller Account of Govinda Karmakar

We have learnt from the Karcha that Govinda Karmakar was born at Kanchananagar (Burdwan) in Uttara Rarh. His father's name was Shyamadasa Karmakar and his mother's Madhavi. His wife Shashimukhi one day abused him, calling him "good-for-nothing" and "fool" (p. 1). Stung by that insult, he left home in 1508 CE (Shaka 1430).

At this time Chaitanya was gaining fame as an incarnation of God. Govinda Karmakar went to Katwa, heard of this, and set out for Navadvipa.

At Navadvipa he saw Chaitanya bathing in the river surrounded by his companions. A fisherman told him who those companions were. The moment he saw the Lord, Govinda sold his heart at his lotus feet; at the very sight of Chaitanya's form he was enchanted: "A towel tied about his waist, a wondrous sight; with him an Avadhuta of serene face. ** Sitting on the ghat, I beheld this lila with my eyes. I know not what feeling arose in my mind: my body bristled like a kadamba flower; my whole body began to tremble; my body broke into sweat and my clothes were soaked. I long to wash his feet with my tears" (p. 3).

From 1508 CE, probably until Lord Chaitanya's disappearance, Govinda was his follower. When Chaitanya, resolved on taking sannyasa, set out for Katwa by way of Burdwan, <!-- p. 72 --> Shashimukhi once caught hold of Govinda. Though Mahaprabhu tried to dissuade Shashimukhi, he was moved by her weeping and in the end ordered Govinda to go back home. But when Chaitanya had left, Govinda disobeyed that order and, disregarding the entreaties and pleas of his relatives, followed the Lord at a run. It seems to us that, for fear of falling into Shashimukhi's clutches again and of Mahaprabhu forcing him to go back home, he kept the Karcha entirely hidden ("I keep the karcha in deep secrecy," p. 62), that is, he let no one see it.

Next, we find in the Karcha that Chaitanya, after returning to Puri, ordered Govinda to go to Shantipur with a letter. But his utterly devoted follower, thinking of a few days' separation, broke down in tears ("Hearing these words, tears flowed from my eyes; my soul cannot bear the arrow of separation from the Lord," p. 86). There was another reason for this weeping: if he went to Bengal, Shashimukhi might try to take him back; his whole life was in Mahaprabhu, and without him he was "a shadow without a body".

Here the Karcha ends. Whether the Karcha contained any further account after this cannot be said, for the manuscript may also have been incomplete.

But this much is certain: unless Govinda died suddenly after this, struck by lightning, he could not have stayed away from Mahaprabhu. A man who was distraught at the prospect of two days' separation, who disobeyed Mahaprabhu's order, fled from his wife at full speed and made his life blessed by following him like a shadow, could never have left Mahaprabhu while he lived.

What I believe to be the account of him that has since been found is given below.

I have already written that the University's manuscript library has an old manuscript called the Chaitanya-chandrodaya-kaumudi, composed by Premadasa. Although this manuscript is based mainly on Kavi Karnapura's Chaitanya-chandrodaya-nataka, it contains some extraneous matter. On p. 148 of this manuscript it is written that after Mahaprabhu's return from the South a person named Govinda Dasa arrived at Srikhanda. The manuscript also hints that this person was a shudra[^11]. It appears that he was keeping everything about himself very secret. When asked <!-- p. 73 --> where his home was, he said: "My home is in Uttara Rarh." And Kanchananagar is indeed in Uttara Rarh. Intent on passing himself off as a complete stranger, he described himself as a "foreigner". Govinda Dasa went from Srikhanda to Shantipur, met Advaita, and then went back to Puri again with Shivananda Sena. This account is in the Chaitanya-chandrodaya-kaumudi. Premadasa refers to him as "Sri Govinda".

Now, if this incident is joined on to the point where the Karcha ends, it seems as though we had found part of the sequel to Govinda Dasa's being ordered by Mahaprabhu to go to Shantipur.

In the Chaitanya-charitamrita we see that when Shivananda Sena came to Puri, a man of the shudra caste named Govinda Dasa, introducing himself as "I am the servant of Ishvara Puri", took up the service of Mahaprabhu. Mahaprabhu had very few devotees and intimates like this servant. He is the famous "Sri Govinda" of Vaishnava history. Had Govinda Dasa revealed his identity here, Shashimukhi, on getting the news, would not have rested until she had taken him away. For nearly two years Govinda had followed Chaitanya; Shashimukhi had certainly heard that Mahaprabhu had taken him along to the South, and was waiting. In such a situation it was absolutely necessary for Govinda Dasa to conceal his identity. That without a word of introduction, just at this time, a man of the shudra caste called Govinda, suddenly announcing himself as Ishvara Puri's servant, became so intimate with Mahaprabhu is indeed very remarkable.

The Chaitanya-charitamrita, following the Chaitanya-chandrodaya, writes that although so many brahmins were eager to serve him, he showed favour to the shudra servant merely on hearing Ishvara Puri's name. In fact we are already familiar with this favour. We have been able to learn that Govinda, ordered by Chaitanya to go to Shantipur, went there and came back to Puri with Shivananda Sena's party. Having come to Puri, he certainly could never have lived apart from Mahaprabhu, his life, his soul, the object of his meditation and knowledge; and when we see that just at that time a Govinda Dasa (of the shudra caste), introducing himself as Ishvara Puri's servant, set about serving the Lord, we readily form the impression that Mahaprabhu had no other servant so intimate besides Govinda Dasa of Kanchananagar, and that the two Govindas are one and the same person, who had to cover himself up in this way purely out of necessity.

<!-- p. 74 -->

From the accounts of Mahaprabhu at Puri we learn that Govinda was engaged in his service until the close of his lila. If our conjecture is right, Govinda served Mahaprabhu for these 25 years, from 1508 to 1533 CE. Where was the home of this companion of so many years, whom the Vaishnavas honour with the name "Sri Govinda"? That he was a Bengali is certain, but no one has given any other particulars about him, and this too is very strange. The other companions all have their particulars given in the Vaishnava books. Of so great a devoted follower, and a loving Bengali at that, Vaishnava literature gives no address at all. Does this not lead one to infer that the disguise of that Govinda who kept his Karcha "in deep secrecy" (p. 62), who, having cut the bonds of maya at Kanchananagar, cherished no higher aim than to be allowed to serve Mahaprabhu's feet, and who, if our view of the Chaitanya-chandrodaya-kaumudi is right, hid the name of Kanchananagar and introduced himself as "a foreigner from Uttara Rarh", was deliberate, and that he concealed his own identity completely, just as he concealed the Karcha?

Compare the service of Sri Govinda with Govinda Dasa's service of Mahaprabhu in the South, and the doubt that the two are one person will be removed. Keeping track of food is one of the chief concerns of Govinda Dasa in the Karcha. It is the same with Sri Govinda at Puri. Compare these verses of the Chaitanya-charitamrita, Madhya ch. 14, 20; Madhya ch. 12, 101; Madhya ch. 12, 85; Antya ch. 7, 65; Antya ch. 10, 30–33, with such lines of the Karcha as "I shall get ... with a curry of banana flower" (p. 4), "How many fruits and roots ... juicy" (p. 4), "Greens, soup ... was" (p. 14), "Offering the bhoga ... mouth" (p. 14), "Prasada: 'Nimba-sukta ... king'" (p. 15), "Chinachur, khurma, laddu ... bak" (p. 20).

One charge is that certain events have been left out of the Karcha. At that time the roads were unsafe because of the constant wars between Vijayanagara and the Muslims. So perhaps they were not able to visit every holy place; that does not prove that errors have remained in the Karcha and that it is therefore unauthentic!

Copyists' errors are common in old manuscripts, and besides that, recovering the reading is often very difficult. Proper names in particular often cause much confusion. On the last page of Manik Ganguli's Dharma-mangala, published by the Sahitya Parishad, there is a strange word "gapherio". At one time several great heads were scratched trying to explain this word. Then the writer of this introduction was able to give an explanation of it and resolve the doubt. "Gapherio" is nothing but a corruption of "shake ritu" ["in the Shaka year ... ritu (a number-word)"], and <!-- p. 75 --> a laughable slip of the copyist. With geographical names, proper names and the like, guessing does not help, so copyists' errors are more frequent in them; and so if any mistake comes to light, it is not right to load them all on the author's shoulders.

The late Jaygopal Goswami, being unable in many places to make out the reading, put in words himself, and in some places, unable to understand a worm-eaten line, filled up that line himself; he told me this himself, and his eldest son Banwari Lal Goswami and his second son Mohan Lal Goswami have also stated it. When the Karcha was published Banwari Lal Goswami was 40 years old and helped his father. The places where such changes occurred he has pointed out so far as he remembers them, as has already been written; the present edition has been printed and published accordingly.

As has already been said, changes of this kind occurred in all the books of that time. In books such as the Ramayana, the Mahabharata, the Chandi and the Padakalpataru there is no end to such changes. The Charitamrita is the principal scripture of the Vaishnavas. Its text has been preserved with great care. So fewer changes are seen in it. Even so, there are a great many variant readings. Since all the books of that time underwent more or less change, why should the Karcha alone be held guilty of that sin? Those who know anything about handwritten manuscripts know that between a hundred-year-old manuscript and a two-hundred-year-old manuscript of the same book there are a great many variant readings and differences in narration.

Last of all, I have one request to make. In this introduction I have been obliged to subject great books such as the Chaitanya-charitamrita, the Chaitanya-bhagavata and the Chaitanya-chandrodaya to the judgement of my small intelligence. Weighed in the scales of history, the accounts in the books mentioned are not in every respect worthy of credence. These books are full of descriptions of divine lila. There is not so much inclination in them towards the human lila. Their chief aim is to establish that Chaitanya is Sri Krishna. That is why I have hesitated to value them as secular history.

But the Chaitanya-charitamrita and the Chaitanya-bhagavata are things of the highest glory in the Bengali language. These two great books are two pillars of fame of sixteenth-century Bengali literature. They are endowed with many virtues. As a mountain peak, though covered with dense jungle, dry leaves and broken rock, spreads its sky-touching majesty, so these two great books too, in spite of a number of historical flaws, shine bright with the majesty of the national theology of Bengal. The late Haradhan Datta Bhaktinidhi wrote of the Chaitanya-charitamrita: "The day I do not read this great book, that day is a day of misfortune." The hearts of many Vaishnavas will respond to these words.

<!-- p. 76 -->

The historical value of these two books too is not small. So if my writing contains any discussion of them, I have had to undertake it out of necessity, for the sake of historical criticism, not to wound the hearts of devotees. If I have done so through inadvertence, I beg their pardon. But have those who at every turn let fly arrows of ridicule at so great a devotee as Govinda Dasa no cause for remorse? For a Vaishnava, the offence of reviling a Vaishnava is very grave. In this connection I shall cite the impartial remark of the orthodox Vaishnava Achyutacharan Tattvanidhi. Referring to a Vaishnava devotee who reviles Govinda Dasa, he says with humility: "It is worth considering whether it is not a very bold thing for a devotee to do this, and whether a devotee is not being slighted thereby."

In the Karcha we see that when the Lord was in a trance Govinda Dasa guarded him with due vigilance. He knew that Mahaprabhu, when in his senses, kept himself especially apart from the company of women in order to teach people. But for fear that, if he went to the muraris, the intoxication of the Hari-nama might come over the Lord and rob him of his senses, he forbade Mahaprabhu to go there: "I said, there is no need to go to that place; Chaitanya Gosai did not heed my words" (p. 55). In the Chaitanya-charitamrita we see that when the Lord, hearing a song of Jayadeva from a devadasi's lips, ran in his frenzy to embrace her, this vigilant servant saved him (Antya, ch. 23 [as printed], 26).

In many respects the Govinda Dasa of the Karcha and Sri Govinda, the famous attendant at Puri, appear to be one person. The service of both is of the same stamp; both are shudras. The Govinda of the Karcha could not have been happy even in Vaikuntha apart from the Lord; if he survived the southern journey, he certainly never went anywhere away from Chaitanya. After the return from the South, Chaitanya sent him to Shantipur. At this short separation Govinda wept and was overcome. From the Chaitanya-chandrodaya-kaumudi it is clearly seen that Govinda went round by Srikhanda and Shantipur, joined Shivananda Sena's party and came back to Puri. Then Kavi Karnapura and Krishnadasa Kaviraja suddenly announce that a shudra Govinda, introducing himself as Ishvara Puri's servant, came to the Lord and remained his intimate companion for the rest of his life. Although Govinda served him for 25 years, no Vaishnava writer gives any information about his home or anything else (though many books certainly mention his devotion). On the other hand, we see that Govinda Dasa of the Karcha had a special <!-- p. 77 --> need to conceal himself; had he not done so, he would very probably have been forced to leave Mahaprabhu's company at Puri and play the "rotten householder" at Kanchananagar in Shashimukhi's company. The Karcha too hints at his concealing himself. Govinda Dasa described himself as a glutton, but this is only his Vaishnava simplicity and humility. That he had taken charge of collecting and storing provisions, however, is clearly seen in both books, the Karcha and the Charitamrita; and the accounts of this in the two books agree remarkably. His service and his devotion to Mahaprabhu are also of the same kind. Reading the two books one clearly gets the impression that the same Govinda who followed him like a shadow in the South and gathered food also followed him like a shadow in the temple at Puri and guarded the store of provisions. The same Govinda who forbade him to go to the quarter of the muraris saved him at Puri from the touch of the devadasi. So we are firmly convinced that these two persons are one. Otherwise it is not credible that so intimate a servant, on coming back from the South, should have dropped out of his company altogether and vanished. And yet at just that auspicious moment, when he particularly needed to conceal himself, a shudra named Govinda, introducing himself as Ishvara Puri's servant, suddenly became the Lord's most intimate companion. If this riddle is carefully examined, the identity of the two persons will clearly come to light. Govinda in his boundless humility and simplicity described himself as "the crest-jewel of gluttons"; Mahaprabhu himself in many places described himself as "low" and "untouchable" ("The Lord says: I beg alms at householders' doors; I am utterly untouchable, do not touch me," p. 55). To take such utterances of holy men in a bad sense and try to make them out guilty is altogether unjust. Govinda ate nothing but Mahaprabhu's prasada, "Unless it is prasada I do not eat" (p. 30), which means that he fasted many times along with Mahaprabhu. In the Bagula forest both fasted in this way for three days and three nights (p. 29). But Govinda felt no hardship in this fasting: "I feel no hunger or thirst by the Lord's grace; that is why I lie wherever I may be" (p. 50). Is a man who forgot hunger and thirst at the sight of Chaitanya's face a glutton?

Govinda was with Mahaprabhu until his disappearance. In the Antya section of the Chaitanya-charitamrita, ch. 10, verses 20, 31, we see that Govinda and Svarupa were alarmed at the frenzied state of Mahaprabhu's last years. At that time Mahaprabhu, though in the world, was no longer in the world. Having become part of the lila of Radha and Krishna, he had taken up residence in the Vrindavana of his heart. No outside word reached his ears any more. In the Antya section (ch. 17, verse 5) <!-- p. 78 --> we see that Mahaprabhu has run off somewhere at night from the Jagannatha temple and Govinda is searching for him like a madman.

Where Govinda went after Mahaprabhu's disappearance has not been traced. But it does not seem likely that so intimate a servant, whose whole life was in Mahaprabhu, lived long after being parted from his heavenly company.

## A Few More Words about the Karcha

Although we regard Govinda's Karcha as an authoritative work on the life of Mahaprabhu in every respect, we set down a few of our views for the reader's information.

It cannot be said that Govinda Dasa is always free from error. He may not have had the opportunity to write the Karcha every day; on the road there were times when he had to spend many days in the jungle. Often he travelled amid all sorts of hardships, and in such conditions it is not impossible that he wrote up the Karcha every 10 or 15 days. In the meantime his memory of small matters may have grown dim, and so some mistakes have crept in. For the early part of the Karcha he kept some notes, which he afterwards turned into payar verse. That is why the early part is very brief. On the southern journey, having plenty of leisure, he turned his notes into payar in solitude as he made them or immediately afterwards, and so the account of that period is very lively and engaging.

The Karcha is brief, and so many things may have been left out of it. Mahaprabhu, travelling in that country, learnt Tamil and Telugu ("Now Gora Raya speaks in the Tamil tongue, now he explains to people speaking Sanskrit. Having wandered long in this land, Shachi's darling understands everyone's speech," p. 62). So Govinda Dasa from time to time asked the Lord about various things and jotted them down. But he could not understand everything.

For those who have no use for history, Achyuta Babu has set up a proof. That proof may not be acceptable to everyone, but to an orthodox Vaishnava it will count for more than many historical proofs. Achyuta Babu writes: "Over and above all these proofs there is one more proof, a spiritual proof. I will not name him, but we know that a highly educated devotee, a follower of Gaura, in a state of meditation directly beheld certain places and lilas as described in this Karcha of Govinda, not in a dream or a swoon, but awake. What more can be said <!-- p. 79 --> about the authenticity of this Karcha of Govinda?" (Sri Sri Vishnupriya-Gauranga Patrika, year 3, no. 5, p. 160).

We would not have cited this proof. History has no room for proofs of this kind. But it shows that even among orthodox Vaishnavas there is no lack of people who hold the Karcha in special reverence and even practise meditation with this book. The campaign of Jogendra Mohan Ghosh, a follower of Pandit Rasik Mohan Vidyabhushan, though on a very large scale, is confined to very few people. In this age the business of collecting signatures has become so cheap that it has no particular value. Still less do the big tilakas and the tokens of Bhagavata learning of those who know nothing of the Bengali language, who believe in supernatural lilas more than in reality, and who take no interest in history, count for anything with us. Well-known historians of the present day have acknowledged the authenticity of this Karcha; of them I shall name two: Sriyukta Rakhaldas Bandyopadhyay and Manomohan Chakravarti[^12]. Some among the opponents may be learned, but none of them has any concern with history.

The objector in the Prabasi has put forward another argument: that at that time the Karmakars [smiths] were wholly illiterate. Long ago, among the Theris of the Buddhist age, we find the name of a learned woman of the Karmakar caste. But we have obtained a copy of Kavikankana's Chandi written a little later than Govinda Dasa's time and found in Kanchananagar itself; on its last leaf it is written that a man of that place named Ramjiban Karmakar won fame by following the profession of reciting the Chandi and other poems. This manuscript is with me at present. Sriyukta Harshit Keshari Ray of 9 Chintamani Das Lane lent it to me to see.

The last two lines of the Karcha are: "How shall I bear the arrow of separation from the Lord? Cruel anguish has arisen in my mind." Thus is Govinda's state of mind described just before he was to take Chaitanya's letter to Advaita at Shantipur.

Whether the book ends here or whether there was more after this is not known.

## Why Did the Karcha Remain Hidden So Long?

If Govinda had died immediately after returning from the South, the Karcha would have been found when the things he left behind were searched, and so valuable a history would have become known at once.

If, after coming back, Govinda had returned to his home at Kanchananagar, he would have had no reason left to hide either himself or the Karcha. In that case too the Karcha would have become well known.

We have already given the one reason why this book should have remained hidden. He concealed the Karcha out of longing to be Chaitanya's companion for ever; fearing that he might lose that company and be forced to return home, he must have blotted out his own identity entirely.

Govinda felt no need to describe the later events of Mahaprabhu's life, because after returning to Puri Mahaprabhu was surrounded by many learned and gifted men, who were eager to record the events of his life. Govinda had only a slight acquaintance with Bengali letters. So, among them, he did not presume to take up the pen.

Moreover, if he had gone on writing after this, his Karcha and his identity would have been discovered among Lord Chaitanya's associates. The need that Govinda Dasa had felt when Mahaprabhu had few companions and was travelling alone far from Bengal no longer existed later on.

So it seems to me that the published Karcha is not incomplete. Perhaps, having written up to that point, Govinda went no further.

But who untied the string with which he had tied up his Karcha, and after how long, is not known.

## Our Explanation

If anyone points out faults in my books and essays, I accept them at once. In historical inquiry mistakes and slips are inevitable. As language and literature progressively flourish, it is desirable to correct the mistaken notions of the past. I have not so lost my wits as to cling to my mistakes and stubbornly maintain my position. But even so, in spite of the errors of the Karcha being proclaimed with so much beating of drums, I have not been able to see <!-- p. 81 --> that my view of the Karcha is mistaken. In the Pallivasi of 13 Magh 1332 someone has written: "We have questioned one or two of his (Dinesh Babu's) students, and he himself is not free from doubt about this Karcha, yet a dreadful prestige or vain pride has taken such hold of him that he cannot by any means admit the truth."

What more shall I write about the many shapes in which falsehood is appearing in the discussion of this Karcha? Govinda Dasa's Karcha has been my inseparable companion for 30 years; hundreds and hundreds of my tears have fallen on its every page. As a lotus in bloom fills every quarter with fragrance, so the story of Mahaprabhu given in the Karcha is full of his heavenly love and the sweetness of his lila. The day I first read this book was a memorable day for me. The echo of the song of divine lila that sounded in my ears that day is ringing still. The true form of Lord Chaitanya that the Karcha has shown me I have found nowhere else. Elsewhere, amid attempts to establish complicated doctrines of avatars and amid mystifications, only glimpses of Mahaprabhu's life are to be had. Like a flash of lightning gleaming for a moment in a bank of clouds, that glimpse vanishes the next moment into the fog of every kind of display of learning and the doctrine of avatars. But look just once at this madman of love in the Karcha. In this little book he seems to have revealed himself entirely. To me this book is the altar at the foot of Mahaprabhu's footstool. That I have doubts about this book is an utter falsehood, unworthy of credit.

I am not an orthodox Vaishnava, not even a Vaishnava at all: I am a Shakta. I am not a Karmakar, nor a Kayastha; I am a Vaidya. I have no interest in this faction-fight of Karmakars and Kayasthas. I am not concerned to understand the Lord's supernatural lilas; I am merely studying the history of his life. So for what interest of mine should I make it a point of honour to cling to a false opinion about this book? In other books there are attempts to dress him up by attributing supernatural divine power to him, but here I see his true portrait. "Kim api hi madhuranam mandanam nakritinam" ["What is not an ornament to lovely forms?"]. Must a lotus be adorned? Must a rose be adorned? Is the beauty of the hundred-petalled lotus increased by showing it encircled by serpents? Is not the garb of a destroyer on the avatar of love a self-evident blemish? Reading this introduction of mine you will understand with how much heartfelt love I have savoured this book; will you find anything of obstinacy in it?

Moreover, among the Karmakars of Kanchananagar there is not one disciple of the late Jaygopal Goswami. In former times people would write a book and pass it off under the name of some great author, <!-- p. 82 --> and the book would thereby circulate more widely. In this way many shastras were circulated under the name of Krishna Dvaipayana Vedavyasa. What prestige would Gosainji have added to the book, and what glory would he have added to himself, by writing his own composition under the name of a blacksmith! When Moti Babu and his colleagues of the Amrita Bazar Patrika declared 51 of the 227 pages of the previous edition of this book to be doubtful, Moti Babu himself wrote: "With what intention the writer wrote this supernatural part we do not know" (Sri Vishnupriya Patrika, Gauranga era 410, month of Kartik). In fact the Goswami had no intention whatever of making out a Kayastha to be a Karmakar. That Mahaprabhu had a companion named Govinda Karmakar, neither he nor anyone else had the faintest inkling at that time. Afterwards, when the name of Govinda Karmakar was found in an old manuscript of the Chaitanya-mangala, that agitation died out completely.

Although in this introduction we have given various external proofs in demonstrating the authenticity of the Karcha, I myself have no need whatever of all these proofs. As, when one stands before a fire, one knows by its heat that the fire is there even with one's eyes shut, so the wondrous intoxication of love in this book is to me the great witness of its authenticity. The soul-stirring divine portrait of Mahaprabhu that Govinda has drawn is its chief witness. The geographical picture he has given is its chief witness. The portrait in this book is a photograph of that extraordinary god of love, messenger of the heavenly world; no one could draw it by learning, by devotion or by his own invention.

The objectors have resorted to many falsehoods, but I shall say in the words of Kashidasa: "How long does a tilaka of water stay on the forehead? How long does a stone stay in the sky when it is thrown up?"

## Chaitanya's Religious Convictions

It is quite true that Chaitanya is neither a Shakta nor a Vaishnava, belongs neither to the householders nor to the sannyasis: he belongs to all.

It cannot be said that the way the Vaishnavas have understood him is the only right one. He himself never described himself as an avatar; with boundless humility he declared again and again that he was a man. What some writers have described, on the strength of things he said or did in a trance, in order to establish that he was an avatar, seems to us exaggerated and incredible. There is not a single statement of that kind in the Karcha. On the contrary, what the Chaitanya-bhagavata writes, that in his presence no one could <!-- p. 83 --> call him anything but a servant of God and get away with it (Chaitanya-bhagavata, Antya, 10), seems to be the truth.

Though he received initiation in the Madhva sampradaya, he preserved the independence of his own views. Nowhere did he preach unconditional faith in the guru. On the contrary, if Rama Raya was one of the foremost of his devotees, and if his views can be accepted as Chaitanya's views, then it seems that he did not accept the doctrine of the guru [guruvada]. Rama Raya's famous song says:

> "I sought no go-between, I sought no other:  
> at the meeting of us two the go-between was the Five-arrowed One [Kama]."

What is the meaning of this verse? Does it not plainly hint that between the devotee and God there is no guru or third person? In the Karcha this is put still more plainly:

> "Faith itself brings one to God and unites one with him."

From this it is clearly seen that God draws the hearts of people himself and does not do so through representatives. He [Chaitanya] himself made no one his disciple.

But is the array of avatars that was constructed out of his associates in later times, when he came to be regarded as the incarnation of the united form of Radha and Krishna, correct? Would he have approved of it? Every associate, and even later devotees, came to be counted as incarnations of the gopis of Vraja and of various gods. It is they who are the owners of the Vrindavana lila. No one has the right to understand love for Krishna by going over their heads. Many Sanskrit and Bengali books, such as the Gaura-ganoddesha, have been written about these avatars. The keys of the storehouse of that religion of love to which the whole world has a right are held by these incarnations of gopis and their descendants. In this age the lila of Chaitanya will have to be understood anew. The age of the domination of mullahs and priestcraft has come to an end. All the fancies of supernatural lila, all the rubbish, will have to be cleared away and the religion that Lord Chaitanya taught explained in its purity. And even if the supernatural lilas were true, what of it? Beside the wondrous ideal that Mahaprabhu displayed through his boundless humility and endless tears, these supernatural lilas have no value; they are a means of beguiling idle people, and educated people will not accept them. So those who have set a guard of shlokas over the Vaishnava religion and keep it under close watch need not trouble with their guardianship. Chaitanya's religion is much greater than the formulas they have devised. We shall not be taken in by tilakas and body-paint, nor be frightened by the profusion of "Sri"s and "Srila"s before names. If they try to clear away the rubbish, we too shall try from the other side to clear away the rubbish and make the truth shine forth. Now the simple heart of the matter will prevail over empty learning, actual events over cock-and-bull stories and blind superstition, and the human lila acceptable to all over the divine lila of the Puranas, attracting greater reverence; <!-- p. 84 --> and in this endeavour to clear the rubbish from the Vaishnava religion it will be necessary to rely less on the Meru and Mandara of medieval learning than on the flowing, clear and pure stream of history, such as the Karcha.

Finally, I must say that everything written here about Govinda Dasa's Karcha is my own opinion, and neither the University nor anyone else is responsible for it. But I believe that judicious readers of this introduction will come round to my view. Many large-hearted Prabhupada Goswamis too have shown their nobility by stepping beyond the bounds of their own narrow interests. We express our boundless respect to them. A great deal of labour has gone into editing this book. But we shall not take a cowrie for it. 250 copies of the book will be distributed free and the rest at cost price only.
