## References to Govinda Dasa in Old Vaishnava Literature

"Having delivered Nilachala, taking Govinda with me, I shall go to the southern country."
— A pada of the poet Balarama Dasa, 375 years old. Gaurapada-tarangini, Sahitya Parishat edition, p. 404.

"Mukunda Datta the Vaidya, Govinda the Karmakar! Come with me to Katwa, on the bank of the Ganges."
— Jayananda's Chaitanya-mangala, 400 years old. Sahitya Parishat edition, Vairagya-khanda, p. 83.

"With Nityananda, Gadadhara and Mukunda; Govinda behind, Keshava Bharati in front."
— Vrindavana Dasa's Chaitanya-bhagavata, 400 years old, Antya, ch. 2.

"Then, with Nityananda and Gadadhara, taking Bharati along, [the Lord] set out in many a joyful mood. Behind them I follow, chalk in hand [for writing]."
— Karcha, p. 12.

"Hearing this, Sri Govinda, filled with joy, went to Advaita, pondering in his heart."
— Chaitanya-chandrodaya-kaumudi, composed by Premadasa in Shaka 1634. Manuscript library of the University of Calcutta, MS No. 2145, folio 148.

## Dedication

He whose love of God —
released by the matted-lock austerities
of that Shiva-like best of men —
turned the abode of Navadvipa into a second Hardwar
and took on visible form;
the herald of the good tidings of bhakti, beloved disciple of Madhavendra Puri —
Sri Advaita Prabhu, purifier of the world:
to his descendant —
tried by endless persecution and ingratitude, established in truth,
Prabhupada the late Jaygopal Goswami, a man of great fortune,
who, following in the steps of his holy forefathers,
discovered Govinda Dasa's Karcha — a small branch of the Ganges of bhakti
lying hidden beneath the sand-banks of oblivion —
and so unveiled to view the painted image of the human play (nara-lila) of Gauranga Thakura —
to his sacred name
I dedicate this new edition of the Karcha.

Sri Dinesh Chandra Sen

## The History of the Recovery of Govinda Dasa's Karcha

<!-- p. 9 -->

About forty-five years ago Kalidas Nath of Shantipur brought several Vaishnava books (manuscripts, puthi) to my father, the late Jaygopal Goswami.[^1] Among them were "Govinda-dasa's Karcha" and a book called "Advaita-vikasha." Taking these two to be old, unpublished works, my father kept them to read. At first Kalidas hesitated to give them up, but at my father's earnest request he left the two old manuscripts with him for a few days. My father could write very fast, and within a few days he had copied out both manuscripts.

Some years later my father took two or three formes of this manuscript — in his own handwriting — to Sisir Babu [Sisir Kumar Ghosh]. My father had not known Sisir Babu before; but learning that he was a great devotee of Gauranga, he became eager to show it to him. The owner of an orchard is anxious to feed mangoes to one who loves mangoes and knows their worth. It was because Goswami Mahashaya thought Sisir Kumar a true connoisseur of the rasa of love for Gauranga that he took this song of Chaitanya's virtues to him. Sisir Babu knew merit when he saw it: after reading only a few pages of the Karcha he was charmed, and he asked my father to hand over the whole manuscript to him. But my father said, "I am a poor Brahmin, and I have resolved to publish this book myself. Some of my friends, learned devotees of Gauranga, tell me that publishing it may bring in a little money." Sisir Babu replied, "Then publish it yourself. Leave the few pages you have brought. I will read them and within seven

<!-- p. 10 -->

days send them back to you by registered post." Seven days slowly went by. The pages did not come back in time. My father grew anxious, because he had already — this was forty-five years ago — returned the original manuscript to Kalidas Nath. He wrote Sisir Babu several letters. Sisir Babu was a busy man. After waiting long for a reply and finally despairing, my father came to Calcutta himself and called on Sisir Babu. The moment he saw my father, Sisir Babu said, "I could not answer your letters; I am very much ashamed before you. I gave those few pages of Govinda Dasa's Karcha to Shambhu Babu to read, and he has lost them." My father came back disappointed and asked Kalidas Nath to get the old manuscript for him again, but Kalidas Nath said, "I have returned the book to the man I got it from; there is no chance of getting it back."

Some time after this my father learned that in Shantipur, in the house of the Pagla Goswamis, Harinath Goswami had another manuscript of Govinda Dasa's Karcha. This manuscript was badly marred by textual corruption and was incomplete. By comparing its text with some notes my father had, the lost leaves were recovered with great difficulty; the book was then given to the managers of the Sanskrit Press Depository to publish. In this way Govinda Dasa's Karcha was published in Shaka 1817 (1895 CE).

Sisir Babu tried to make out that Govinda Dasa was a Kayastha. But after Jayananda's Chaitanya-mangala was published, the public understood that Govinda was no Kayastha but exactly what the printed book says, that is, a true Karmakar (blacksmith). Whether Sisir Babu was somewhat put out by this, I cannot say. Perhaps he was also somewhat put out because my father could not grant his request and put the book into his hands. Be that as it may, he expressed doubt about the genuineness of the first few pages of the printed Karcha of Govinda Dasa. Nevertheless, he wrote the account of Mahaprabhu's southern tour in his Amiya-Nimai-charita on the basis of the Karcha, and his brother Moti Babu [Motilal Ghosh] in the Sri Vishnupriya Patrika again and again extolled the merits and historical value of the Karcha.

So, for various tangled reasons, a slight doubt arose about the first few pages; and now, after many years, that very doubt has become the ground for rejecting the whole manuscript. This agitation started from the Amrita Bazar [Patrika].

<!-- p. 11 -->

The publication of the Karcha was warmly encouraged by the late Madana Gopal Goswami of Shantipur, foremost among pandits and a great bhagavata. Many people saw the old manuscript at that time, and some of them are still alive. Elsewhere [in this book] the letters of Rai Bahadur Sarat Chandra Chattopadhyay and of the eminent pandit Lakshmikanta Tarkachudamani are printed; they saw that old book with their own eyes. I am the eldest son of the late Jaygopal Goswami; I am now 70 years old. The old manuscript was in our house for some time. I saw it too.

Whether Govinda Dasa was a Kayastha or a Karmakar is no concern of ours. The minute description of the Southern Road (Dakshinapatha) in this book is something no one could imagine who had not travelled all over the Deccan. Some of the villages mentioned in it are not marked even on the maps of the Survey Office. Alas, for those who are attacking my late father so very unjustly and want to make him out a cheat, there is no atonement for this grievous offence of reviling a Vaishnava. What pain these unjust and false charges are causing our hearts — what more can I write?

In some places in the book my father, while editing, replaced old difficult words. Perhaps now and then, where part of a line had been eaten by insects, he filled it in. Everyone who edits an old manuscript does this.

What I have written is the plain truth. In this dark Kali age there is never a lack of witnesses to prove night is day. So now "evidence" is turning up that we were "outcasted" (ek-ghare) for the offence of forging the manuscript. If our society were so wide awake today, the evil days of the country would be over! How many things happen in this country out of feuds between kinsmen! I pray to God to forgive those who are doing us all these wrongs.

Where now is that Vaishnava humility, and where that outpouring of bhakti? My ancestor Advaita Acharya won Chaitanya by severe austerity. Chaitanya is nearer to our family than the nearest kin; he is the object of our family's meditation and its stay. You may try to dim Chaitanya's glory, but a descendant of Advaita will never rush to do such a thing. There is no passage in the Karcha that detracts from Gaura's greatness; had there been, my father would not have published this Karcha.

Sri Banwari Lal Goswami.

[^1]: In a letter Banwari Lal has written me a few more things, namely: "I remember Kalidas said, 'The language of the Karcha is quite flawless; nowhere is it exaggerated; the book is full of clarity (prasada-guna).' An old unpublished book, and on top of that the simplicity and fluency of its language — this attracted my father intensely. The reading of Govinda Dasa's Karcha began at once. After only a few pages had been read, the late Madana Goswami Mahashaya arrived, and my father said eagerly, 'Madana, a wonderful book! Let me read it again from the start; listen.'" — Sri Dinesh Chandra Sen.

# Introduction (Bhumika)

## 1. The Old Manuscripts of the Karcha

<!-- p. 15 -->

Sri Banwari Lal Goswami, one of the editors of this book, has given a detailed account of how the Karcha was obtained. He is well known in the field of Bengali letters; his books "Khichuri," "Polao" and others have won a notable place in Bengali poetry. Goswami Mahashaya is now 70. Those who know him know his straightforward nature and his fire. In his zeal to speak hard truths he sometimes cannot keep the caution that is due.

Sri Banwari Lal Goswami's father, the late Jaygopal Goswami of Shantipur, published Govinda Dasa's Karcha. Banwari Lal was then about 40 and was his father's right hand in everything; so all he writes are events he witnessed himself. Jaygopal Goswami obtained two old manuscripts of the Karcha. The first was very decayed and worm-eaten. He got it from the late Kalidas Nath and made a copy of it. Several leaves of that copy were lost by Dr. Shambhu Mukhopadhyay, editor of the journal "Reis and Rayyet." Long before that, Goswami Mahashaya had returned the old manuscript. When he asked Kalidas Nath for it again, he did not get it: Nath Mahashaya had returned the manuscript to its owner and there was no chance of getting it back. So the incomplete copy lay with Goswami Mahashaya for some years. Then by good fortune another manuscript of the Karcha turned up with the late Harinath Goswami of Shantipur; it was fragmentary and full of errors. From the first part of this fragmentary, faulty manuscript and from the notes he had of the old manuscript, Jaygopal Goswami with great difficulty restored the lost portion. In 1895 (Shaka 1817) Govinda Dasa's Karcha was first published by the Sanskrit Press Depository, Calcutta.

### "Bring out the old manuscript!"

Some of those who have raised doubts about the authenticity of this Karcha say: "Bring out the old manuscript of the Karcha, and then we will believe." Both manuscripts

<!-- p. 16 -->

from which Goswami Mahashaya published the Karcha were returned to their owners, and it is now impossible to get them.

It is certain that in many places in this country treasures lie buried underground; but can anyone dig them out the moment he is told to? Such manuscripts are not to be had by writing to Newman's or Thacker's. The very people in whose houses old manuscripts lie often do not know of them. Recovering old handwritten manuscripts is no easy business. The collecting of Bengali manuscripts has barely begun. The Karcha itself hints that Govinda Dasa for some reason kept his book hidden; so its old copies certainly cannot be plentiful. On top of that, a vicious conspiracy is now being carried on against this manuscript.

Old Bengali manuscripts are mostly kept in the houses of people of the lower classes. In the rainy season streams of water pour through the holes in thatched roofs, and year after year hundreds of manuscripts perish. Then there are fires, floods and the mischief of children. Many people, moreover, now and then throw old manuscripts into the Ganges. Even of books by authors famous throughout the country, old manuscripts often cannot be found. There was an old manuscript of the Ramayana in the library of the Sahitya Parishat containing Krittibasa's long autobiographical account; I saw it, and so did Sri Hirendranath Datta and many others. That manuscript cannot be found now. Another Ramayana with the same autobiography, written in 1501 [so printed], was in the house of the late Haradhan Datta Bhaktinidhi of Badanganj, Hooghly; Rai Bahadur Jogesh Chandra Ray saw it. But it too is no longer to be found. In the libraries of the Sahitya Parishat and the University there are more than a hundred old manuscripts of Krittibasa's Ramayana, but none of them contains that autobiography, and it is not to be found anywhere else. Can anyone search it out of Bengal on demand? And must the autobiography be thrown out for the offence of not being found?

Nityananda Dasa, author of the Prema-vilasa, mentions again and again that he wrote a large life of Nityananda, but no old manuscript of that book can be found. Krishnadasa Kaviraja mentions in his Chaitanya-charitamrita, Madhya, chapter 25, verse 8, that he composed a large book called "Pancha-tattva-akhyana." No trace of it has been found to this day. Lala Jayanarayan Sen's "Harilila" was very famous in the Dacca region in the eighteenth century. After much effort the University of Calcutta bought an old manuscript of it for Rs. 200 from Sri Anandanath Ray, the historian of Faridpur,

<!-- p. 17 -->

and it was stolen. I could not get another copy of it by any means. I had a 200-year-old manuscript of a Bengali translation of a Smriti text; it was ruined by damp. For all my efforts I have not been able to find its match. All the authors mentioned have an established place in Bengali literature, and their books were widely circulated; yet when even their manuscripts have become rare, let no one cherish the vain hope that a manuscript of Govinda Dasa's little-circulated Karcha will turn up the moment it is asked for. At most it may turn up suddenly by chance. Old handwritten manuscripts apart, how many books printed a hundred years ago are now quite unobtainable — and a thousand or two thousand copies of them were printed!

This reminds me of something. There is still a group of people who say that there was no poet called Shakespeare and that the plays attributed to him were really written by Bacon. Their chief argument is this: "No manuscript in Shakespeare's hand is to be found. Tradition says he dedicated two books, Venus and Adonis and Lucrece, to the Earl of Southampton — but where is the actual letter of dedication?"

In answer to this question Mr. G. L. Pugh wrote in jest: "No letters preserved, therefore none were written."

[By the same logic:] "No manuscripts preserved, therefore no manuscript was ever written."[^2]

[^2]: "No letters preserved, therefore none were written." No Mss preserved, therefore none were written." — Statesman, February 10, 1926.

### Village Intrigue

When the wave of agitation against the Karcha reached Shantipur, there happened what usually happens in a village: some of Jaygopal Goswami's enemies seized on the matter and spread the story that they had seen with their own eyes Goswami Mahashaya sitting and composing this book out of his imagination, and that for the offence of composing it he had been "outcasted" (ek-ghare) in Shantipur.

In reply to this charge against his father Banwari Lal Goswami has written: "Now 'evidence' is turning up that we were 'outcasted' for forging the manuscript. If our society were so wide awake today, the evil days of the country would be over!"

<!-- p. 18 -->

We made inquiries about this of Sri Nalini Mohan Sanyal of Shantipur, retired school inspector of the Burdwan Division. Sanyal Mahashaya is now 64. The substance of his letter, written in English, is given below:[^3] "Dear Dr. Sen, you have asked whether Pandit Jaygopal Goswami of Shantipur was 'outcasted' by the Vaishnava community there on the charge of forging Govinda Dasa's Karcha. I belong to the Vaishnava community of Shantipur. Our family and Goswami Mahashaya's have been intimate for generations. I am now 63. I never heard that he was 'outcasted' in Shantipur. I believe Govinda Dasa's Karcha to be an excellent authentic work.

Yours,
Sri Nalini Mohan Sanyal.
26th March, 1925."

Besides Sanyal Mahashaya's letter I have received several more. I quote some of them in part in a footnote.[^4] On a later page I shall quote the letter of Rai Bahadur Sarat Chandra Chattopadhyay. Anyone who reads all these letters will see that this agitation, having entered Shantipur, has created village factionalism, and that there is therefore no reason for us to break into a sweat

<!-- p. 19 -->

over it. Even if it were true that Jaygopal Goswami had been "outcasted," in the field of letters that would not count as proof of any offence on his part, since village "outcastings" are almost always a matter of enmity.

[^3]: The English original: "My Dear Dr. Sen, You have asked me to let you know if the late Pandit Joy Gopal Goswami was boycotted by the Vaisnava community of Santipur for having published the Karcha of Govinda Das alleged to have been forged by the Pandit. I belong to the Vaisnava community of Santipur and am now 63 years old. My family and the venerable Pandit's family were in close touch with each other for genrations [sic]. I never heard of his being boycotted at Santipur. I believe the Karcha published by him to be a genuine historical work of great merit. Yours sincerely, (Sd) Nalini Mohan Sanyal, 26th. March, 1925."

[^4]: On this point Pandit Harilal Goswami of Shantipur, a man of about eighty, writes: "For all my inquiries I could not learn that the revered Jaygopal Goswami Mahashaya was ever 'outcasted' over Govinda Dasa's Karcha; on the contrary, everyone said he was never outcasted." Sri Kirtishchandra Goswami of Shantipur writes: "Respected Sir, I have received your letter of 5.3.25. That the venerable Jaygopal Goswami Mahashaya, after bringing out Govinda Dasa's Karcha, suffered social punishment for forgery or was 'outcasted' is a baseless report. I myself never heard any such report, nor could I learn of it from anyone. I asked Sri Ramachandra Goswami, Vice-Chairman of the local municipality, about this; he said he knew of no such thing."

### Those Who Saw the Old Manuscript

The manuscript came into Goswami Mahashaya's hands some 14 or 15 years before the Karcha was published. That was 45 or 46 years ago. At that time many people saw the manuscript Kalidas Nath had given. Many of the people of that day are no longer alive, but happily there are still two or three educated and accomplished men who saw it. The name of the famous pandit of Bakla, Lakshminarayan Tarkachudamani,[^5] is known to many. He is now 80. He has written me the following letter:

"Forty-five or forty-six years ago, while I was living at Keota near Hooghly, I saw a manuscript of Govinda Dasa's Karcha with a Brahmin devoted to Hari, the late Gorachand Chakravarti. The manuscript was worm-eaten and decayed. He was copying it and often called me to make out obscure passages; that is why I remember much of it. Now I see Govinda Dasa's Karcha, compiled by the late Jaygopal Goswami, in print. I believe the manuscript I saw with Chakravarti Mahashaya and this printed book to be one and the same. The more I read the book compiled by Jaygopal Goswami, the more my earlier impressions come back to life.

Yours obediently,
Sri Lakshminarayan Bhattacharya."

We have received another letter from Rai Bahadur Sri Sarat Chandra Chattopadhyay, M.A., B.L., retired Government Pleader of Rangpur, brother of the High Commissioner, the civilian Sir Atul Chandra Chattopadhyay. He wrote me the letter on 24 March 1925; it is quoted in a footnote,[^6] and the substance of part of it is given below.

<!-- p. 20 -->

"Dear Sir, I have received your kind letter of the 19th. I knew the late Pandit Jaygopal Goswami of Shantipur well. At one time I had the honour of his friendship. I saw him copying an old manuscript of Govinda Dasa's Karcha. He was copying it in order to publish it. That would be more than 40 years ago. The manuscript was then in a very decayed state, and that, I suppose, is why he was making a copy of it."

The letter of Sri Harilal Goswami of Shantipur shows that some of those who saw the manuscript of the Karcha are still alive in Shantipur. Besides, Banwari Lal Goswami, who is now 70, and his brother Sri Mohan Lal Goswami, who is now 60, saw this manuscript themselves and know all the circumstances connected with it.

However, feuds among kinsmen have gone on in our society unabated from the time of Kurukshetra to the present day. A kinsman of Goswami Mahashaya, Sri Radhavinod Goswami, has said at a public meeting in Dacca that he himself knows that Jaygopal Goswami forged Govinda Dasa's Karcha and was "outcasted" in Shantipur for it. There is no need to relate here the quarrels that arose on this occasion. But Pandit Banwari Lal Goswami has written to me — and several residents of Shantipur, Sri Nalini Mohan Sanyal among them, have supported him — that when the Karcha was obtained Radhavinod Goswami had not even been born; or if he had, he was crawling on all fours in the courtyard of his own house.

<!-- p. 21 -->

Another gentleman of Shantipur says: "Goswami Mahashaya, having lost a few pages of the manuscript, sat idle for a long time; it was I who advised him to forge those few leaves." And, just as a boy given sweets from the confectioner's shop gulps them down at once, Goswami Mahashaya is supposed to have taken this good advice on the spot and forged those few pages.

If Goswami Mahashaya had composed the book himself, why should he sit idle for so many years after losing a few pages? He could have made them good himself without delay.

The wrangling of Shantipur, thorny with factions and poisoned by feuds among kinsmen, will not help in the least to establish the real facts. I therefore drop the discussion of this subject.

## 2. The History of the Opponents' Agitation

The opponents' agitation did not first begin in Shantipur. It began in the Amrita Bazar office. Having read the copy of the Karcha,[^7] the late Sisir Kumar Ghosh became a warm partisan of the book. He asked Goswami Mahashaya to leave two formes of the copy with him for a few days. Dr. Shambhu Mukhopadhyay took them from him and lost them. Relying on a vague memory of those two formes, Sisir Babu began writing about the Karcha in the "Amiya-Nimai-charita," and in that book, through a slip of memory, he called Govinda Dasa a Kayastha. Then, some years later, when the Karcha was published, he saw that in the printed book Govinda Dasa was called a Karmakar. Meanwhile that volume of his Amiya-Nimai-charita had already been printed. So, to keep his own statements consistent, he declared that the first part of the Karcha was not authentic. In the first edition the Karcha runs to 227 printed pages in all. Of these, the genuineness of the part up to page 51, to the line "Clasping [the Lord's] knees, Rama Raya weeps" (that is, the part that had been lost), was declared doubtful. Of what was done in this connection against the simple-hearted old Jaygopal Goswami I shall say nothing for the present. At that time Goswami Mahashaya came to my lodgings at No. 12, Shyampukur Lane, and told me the whole story

<!-- p. 22 -->

with great feeling. He told me how, some years after the copy was lost, he had with great labour restored the text of the lost leaves from another fragmentary manuscript obtained from Harinath Goswami of Shantipur and from his own notes. But when a man like Sisir Babu said that the manuscript had read "Kayastha" and not "Karmakar," a party of people rose up to prove with great vehemence that these 51 pages of the Karcha were forged. So this controversy arose over a question of caste.

This protest perhaps arose for another reason too. Banwari Lal Goswami Mahashaya has hinted at that as well. When Pandit Jaygopal came to Sisir Babu with the copy of the Karcha, Sisir Babu expressed the intention of bringing the book out himself from the Amrita Bazar Patrika office and asked the Pandit Mahashaya for it, and the Pandit Mahashaya did not give it. Had he given it, and had the book come out from the Amrita Bazar Patrika office, there would probably have been no agitation against it.

So we have a history of how the first attack on this book began. When anything starts in this country its waves go on for a long time, for the Bengali has plenty of time for idle pursuits. So there is nothing surprising in the fact that the agitation that started then is still going on.

But the drum of truth soon sounded. In the meantime the Sahitya Parishat published a very old book, Jayananda's "Chaitanya-mangala." Sri Prachyavidyamaharnava Nagendranath Basu and the late Kalidas Nath edited it from several old manuscripts. The Vairagya-khanda of this book states plainly that Mahaprabhu's companion at his sannyasa was "Govinda the Karmakar." This discovery silenced the opponents. Even so, they once tried to dismiss the whole manuscript of Jayananda's Chaitanya-mangala as a forgery. But their attempt came to nothing after an unanswerable reply by Nagendra Babu published in volume 5 of the Sahitya Parishat Patrika; and when Nagendra Babu then went himself to Deoghar and showed Sisir Babu the text of a 200- or 300-year-old manuscript of Jayananda's Chaitanya-mangala, Sisir Babu had no further reason for disbelief. Everyone then understood that Jaygopal Goswami had not struck out the reading "Kayastha" and put in "Karmakar" out of his own imagination. After this the opponents kept complete silence for some 27 or 28 years. The zeal for unjust accusations against the old Brahmin was quenched for a long time.

### The Revival of the Agitation: "The Whole Karcha Is a Forgery"

In various books of mine, English and Bengali, I have assigned Govinda Dasa's Karcha a very high place in the field of Vaishnava history, and have even judged it superior in historical reliability to the Chaitanya-bhagavata and the Chaitanya-charitamrita. Orthodox Vaishnavas revere these two books — the Chaitanya-charitamrita above all — as equal to the Vedas. What the Bible is to the orthodox Christian, the Chaitanya-charitamrita is to the orthodox Vaishnava. So when I declared that the little Karcha, composed by an unlettered smith, was in some respects superior even to the Chaitanya-charitamrita and other famous books, some Vaishnavas were bitterly annoyed with me. Among them Sri Rasik Mohan Vidyabhushan may be named in particular. The earlier agitators had tried to prove only a few pages of the Karcha forged; but now, 27 or 28 years after that agitation, they set to work with a will to prove that the whole manuscript was a fabrication of Goswami Mahashaya's own brain. Twenty-seven or twenty-eight years ago many people had seen the old manuscript, so such a protest could not have stood then; now many of those people have gone to heaven, and so the way is clear. But the letters of those who are still alive and saw the book have already been quoted above.

In fact there is another special reason why they do not like the Karcha. Although the events related in the Chaitanya-charitamrita, the Chaitanya-chandrodaya, the Chaitanya-bhagavata, the Chaitanya-mangala and other books do not agree everywhere, at bottom they are all written in one strain. Everywhere in these books, in the effort to prove Chaitanya to be Sri Krishna, his divine play (deva-lila) is brought in at every turn. The Vaishnavas of those days wanted to hear only of Chaitanya's divine play; they took no pleasure in seeing him simply, as a man.

This was not peculiar to Hindu belief; throughout the world in those days supernatural events were the mainstay of popular religious faith. Christians nowadays consider the Gospel written by Mark the most precious in the New Testament, precisely because there is little of the supernatural in it. But at one time the book was neglected for this very reason.[^8]

<!-- p. 24 -->

The Chaitanya-bhagavata, the Chaitanya-charitamrita and other books relate that Chaitanya-deva now takes the form of the Boar ("At that moment the Lord took the form of the Boar; Murari was struck dumb at the wondrous sight" — Chaitanya-bhagavata, Madhya, ch. 3), now the form of Nrisimha, now appears four-armed ("Vishvambhara sits in the hero's posture (virasana), four-armed, holding conch, discus, mace and lotus" — Chaitanya-bhagavata, Madhya, ch. 2), now shows himself eight-armed to a Brahmin guest (Chaitanya-bhagavata, Madhya, ch. 3); he showed a six-armed form to Nityananda in Navadvipa and to Sarvabhauma in Puri (Chaitanya-bhagavata, Madhya, ch. 3). Krishnadasa, the author of the Chaitanya-charitamrita, also wrote down these stories. Murari Gupta's Karcha records that Chaitanya-deva, straight from the lying-in room, holds learned disputations with Advaitacharya on various abstruse philosophical truths. Now at his command tigers and elephants dance, chanting "Krishna, Krishna" (Chaitanya-charitamrita, Madhya, ch. 17, vv. 12–13); now wild parrots, hen and cock (shari and shuka), fly down onto his hand and recite to him various verses from the Govinda-lilamrita (Chaitanya-charitamrita, Madhya, ch. 17, v. 76).[^9] In Lochana Dasa's Chaitanya-mangala we find Vibhishana coming from Lanka to meet the Lord. To prove that he is Vishnu reclining on Ananta, one writer says that as an infant he lay on the back of a terrible black serpent ("The serpent lay coiled around, and the Thakura lay upon the serpent" — Chaitanya-bhagavata, ch. 3). Some, to prove him one who "holds the universe in his belly" (brahmanda-bhandodara), have written that in the form of Jagannatha he alone eats 54 offerings (bhoga) every day, consuming hundreds of loads of rice each time. Advaitacharya says to Chaitanya-deva, "The food of three goes down in each mouthful of yours" (Chaitanya-charitamrita, Madhya, ch. 3, v. 49); at the request of Shathi's mother Chaitanya-deva alone ate food enough for ten or twelve, and so her son-in-law Amogha said, "Ten people would be satisfied with this food — and a lone sannyasi eats all this!" (Chaitanya-charitamrita, ch. 15, v. 90). All this is only to prove that he "holds the universe in his belly."

<!-- p. 25 -->

To prove that he is God in the form of Rudra, some writers describe how he smashes and wrecks houses into rubble, and grows so fearsome that he strikes even his own mother till she faints (Chaitanya-charitamrita, Adi, ch. 3; and Adi, ch. 14, v. 6). It is also described how, like a conjurer, he now makes kadamba flowers blossom on a citron (jamir) tree (Antya, ch. 5), and now: "The Lord planted a mango stone in the courtyard. At once a tree sprang up and began to grow. Before their eyes the tree bore fruit; many fruits ripened, and all were amazed" (Chaitanya-charitamrita, Adi, ch. 17, v. 65). How many such examples shall I give? If the reader searches these famous books he will find many more instances of the kind.

Some of the orthodox Vaishnavas, though educated, have not been able to escape the hold of these ingrained notions. They regard all these miraculous powers (vibhuti) and all this divine majesty (aishvarya) of Chaitanya as authentic. But in Govinda Dasa's Karcha there is none of this. "Of all Sri Krishna's sports, the best is his human play (nara-lila)": in the figure of Chaitanya-deva drawn by the Karcha it is the glory of the nara-lila that is found in abundance. So to those who have grown used to picturing Chaitanya-deva in their hearts from all these supernatural tales, the unadorned man-god of the Karcha will seem somewhat out of place. That is why, if there are discrepancies among the books they consider authoritative, they pass them by with eyes shut; but the Chaitanya of the Karcha is in many ways a new ideal. There is very little in it about his supernatural nature. If Sri Krishna, king of kings of Mathura, suddenly appeared among his subjects as a cowherd boy, with a herding stick in his hand and a rope for tethering cows, they might well not recognize him at all. Some orthodox Vaishnavas, accustomed to the age-old notions of the supernatural, have found the Chaitanya of the Karcha unfamiliar and rejected him.

But here one point must be clearly understood. In reverence for the Chaitanya-charitamrita and similar books I will yield to no one. For those who wish to understand what the Vaishnava religion is, the Charitamrita is the only resort. Anyone who would grasp the inner meaning of the Vaishnavism of this country while leaving this great book aside will fail, like one who tries to cross the ocean on a raft. The extraordinary learning and bhakti of Kaviraja Goswami shine on every page of it. I do not know whether any other community anywhere in the world has a religious scripture so philosophical. But it does not follow that its claim to pre-eminence as history can be admitted. Everyone sees that among the divine images — Bhagavati, Ganesha, Vasudeva and others — one has ten arms, another an elephant's head, another four arms. But when sculptors or painters make the figures of their attendants, these

<!-- p. 26 -->

are conceived entirely naturally. In the same way, although in these religious books Chaitanya-deva is often described supernaturally, when the authors recorded the surrounding circumstances they described them accurately and naturally. So I do not mean to say that these books have no great value even as contemporary history. But many of the pictures of Mahaprabhu we find in them are supernatural and exaggerated; so it is impossible to accept them all as historical. We do not wish to attack the faith of those who believe in the supernatural; on the contrary, we can respect them as devotees. But faith in these miracles belongs to the realm of feeling. If anyone wants to prove them historical, we can never agree with him. It is remarkable that some of this orthodox party accept these religious books as historical truth down to the last anusvara and visarga, yet spread a dense net of historical argument over the minutest points of the Karcha. Among them are educated men too: they are not content to set up everything in these books as irrefutable truth; they also hesitate to admit the historicity of any book in which they do not find the model of those miraculous pictures. What could be more astonishing? People who can freely believe that Murari Gupta proved himself to be Angada by putting out a tail before a Brahmin family ("I praise Murari Gupta, mighty as Angada, whose tail a Brahmin and all his family beheld"), and that when Chaitanya-deva summoned the Sudarshana discus to punish Jagai and Madhai, the discus whirled humming in the sky at his command ("'Discus, discus, discus!' the Lord calls again and again. In haste the discus came and stood before him" — Chaitanya-bhagavata, Madhya, [ch.] 16) — these same people refuse to believe the Karcha's statement that Chaitanya-deva took his food by mouth (and not through the nostrils), and think it impossible that he passed his holy hand over the body of his attendant when the latter had an upset stomach. Neither their belief nor their disbelief knows any bounds. The sharp ray of inquiry they are turning on Govinda Dasa's Karcha could shake the foundations of any famous history in the world. How many arguments are raised over every line! And yet the Chaitanya-bhagavata and the Chaitanya-charitamrita, apart from miracles, contain things that are quite incredible. See what indignity Chaitanya-deva, barely past boyhood, inflicts in his Rudra form on the ninety-year-old Advaitacharya: "Dragging Advaita from his seat, the Lord threw him down in the courtyard and pummelled him with his own fists" (Chaitanya-bhagavata, Madhya, [ch.] 13). To those who, seeing this terrible form of Chaitanya Prabhu, would count him an incarnation of Rudra,

<!-- p. 27 -->

we bow from afar. Is this the image of Chaitanya-deva, the embodiment of love? If this is not a distortion of him, what shall we call distortion? On the night before taking sannyasa Chaitanya-deva spent the night with two of his companions; but Lochana Dasa writes that he spent that night with Vishnupriya. Not only that: what is told there of their conjugal union is, in regard to Chaitanya-deva — the very image of renunciant love — on the eve of his sannyasa, utterly unseemly, incredible and incongruous. And yet the very people who accept all these wholly imaginary tales and miraculous sports as historical events raise subtle arguments, like Naiyayikas [logicians], about the unreliability of Govinda Dasa's tiniest details. Bravo to their belief, and bravo to their disbelief!

In old Vaishnava literature many Sanskrit and old Bengali manuscripts are found under the names "Gaura-ganoddesha" and "Dig-darshani." In them it is clearly laid down which of Mahaprabhu's companions is an incarnation of whom. A sample of this array of incarnations[^10] is given in a footnote. Many of the educated Vaishnavas who oppose Govinda Dasa believe in this theory of incarnations;

<!-- p. 28 -->

so it can by no means be admitted that the historical sense of these Vaishnava pandits is very keen. And yet, when judging Govinda Dasa's Karcha, they are busy sharpening all kinds of weapons to display the depth of their historical learning.

<!-- p. 29 -->

If they want to find faults in the Karcha, let them first examine properly the historical truth of their own "authoritative" books. There, hundreds of holes gape open like pits. As already said, the old manuscript library of the University has many ancient Sanskrit and Bengali works propagating this theory of incarnations. The Chaitanya Prabhu of whom Vrindavana Dasa wrote, "The Lord ever dwelt in the mood of a servant (dasya-bhava); he would let himself be called nothing but 'Krishna's servant.' No one had the boldness to call him God to his face — only a servant" (Chaitanya-bhagavata, Antya, [ch.] 10) — he could never have favoured this theory of incarnations.

Rai Bahadur Rasamay Mitra has written[^11] that for a long time, seeing the discrepancies between the Karcha and the Chaitanya-charitamrita in thought and language, he had been trying to prove it forged. By chance Jaygopal Goswami came to him trying to get one of his books prescribed as a school text. Taking this opportunity, he held out to Goswami Mahashaya the bait of having his book prescribed, and urged him to confess that at least the first part of the Karcha was forged; and from the hints and gestures he caught from Goswami Mahashaya in reply, he formed the clear impression that he had forged part of the Karcha. When authors are tempted with the prospect of having their books prescribed, it is not very hard to get such hints and gestures from them. On this I quote a little from Banwari Babu's letter: "Rasamay is younger than I, so when the Karcha came out he was a green horn. There was never any friendship between his family and ours. I knew nearly all my father's friends. I did not know that Rasamay was his bosom friend. Had the Pandit Mahashaya forged the Karcha and brought it out, he would certainly not have poured out his heart to Rasamay, a chance acquaintance of the road, a fellow passenger in a carriage. It is human nature to hide one's sins; no one will be inclined to believe that the aged Goswami went about the streets of Calcutta with the Rasamay-drum tied round his neck to proclaim his own sin."

I have no special reason for disrespect towards those who, though educated, have set out in this way to prove the Karcha false; for though they are blinded by prejudice,

<!-- p. 30 -->

they are not doing it out of any self-interest. They cannot escape the hold of the inherited beliefs that have taken possession of their minds.

But I will show by one example how grotesquely their prejudice sometimes shows itself. On p. 31 of the Karcha there is mention of a dark, emaciated sannyasi. At the sight of Chaitanya-deva his heart blossomed with love, and Govinda writes of him on this occasion: "As if a charred log swelled up with love." But an orthodox Vaishnava pandit, without reading carefully, thought that Chaitanya-deva himself had been called a "charred log." Then his poetic wrath boiled over. Chaitanya-deva, whose colour is the gold of the champaka, before whom the lightning admits defeat and the atasi flower is put to shame — the wretched writer had called him "burnt wood"! And so, seething with rage, he wrote and printed a fiery speech two formes long. Meeting me by chance, he learned from me — I brought the Karcha and showed him — that he had vented his wrath on an utter misunderstanding. He fled from me and burnt those two formes; the speech never saw the light.

But besides two or three people blinded by prejudice, another class of people has appeared who deserve no indulgence whatever, for they are deliberately perverting the truth.

They go about proclaiming in newspaper columns, in the bazaar and at all kinds of meetings and gatherings that Govinda Dasa's Karcha says Mahaprabhu was attached to harlots. They say further that Govinda Dasa has portrayed Chaitanya-deva as a Sahajiya who sought the company of women. On hearing such things simple devotees are naturally bound to be extremely angry. There are many uneducated and half-educated Vaishnava men of wealth who have been greatly incensed on hearing this. Many have had no chance to read Govinda Dasa's Karcha. But, deceived by the words of these people, they have, I hear, raised money and set up a fund to purge the Vaishnava religion of filth. Some of these people are openly trying to make out not only Jaygopal Goswami but me as well to be a forger. The lack of honesty in some of them is as plain as daylight. As already said, in Jayananda's Chaitanya-mangala Govinda the Karmakar is mentioned as Chaitanya's companion at his renunciation, and that Chaitanya-mangala has been published by the Sahitya Parishat, edited by Nagendranath Basu and Kalidas Nath. In an article by "a certain distinguished Vaishnava" printed in the Ananda Bazar Patrika of 26 Magh 1331, it was asserted that there was only one old manuscript of Jayananda and that

<!-- p. 31 -->

it was kept in Nagendra Babu's library of old manuscripts. The manuscript, it was said, read "Govindananda ar" ["and Govindananda"]. The writer of the article says, "Dinesh Babu had free access to that library," and that he struck out the reading "Govindananda ar" and made it "Govinda Karmakar," which is why in the Chaitanya-mangala edited by Nagendra Babu and others the line has been printed as "Govinda Karmakar." Following the writing of this "distinguished Vaishnava," several more articles have been published by various people, in which too the attempt has been made to prove me a forger.

The University of Calcutta has collected a great many old Bengali manuscripts, and in two of them, manuscripts of the Chaitanya-mangala, the name of Govinda the Karmakar occurs. The letter on this subject from Dr. Sri Gauranganath Bandyopadhyay, M.A., Ph.D., Secretary of the Post-Graduate Department of Arts of the University, is given below. Facsimiles of the two leaves of those old manuscripts are also given with it. Dr. Gauranga's letter runs thus: "I have examined the two manuscripts of Jayananda's Chaitanya-mangala (Nos. 544 and 545) in the Bengali old manuscripts section of the Calcutta University Library. Both manuscripts are about two hundred and fifty years old. On page 62 of manuscript No. 544 and on page 42 of manuscript No. 545 I found the name of Govinda the Karmakar mentioned. Sri Gauranganath Bandyopadhyay, 23rd February, 1925."

The "distinguished Vaishnava" had written that "Nagendra Babu edited the book after looking at a single manuscript (which, according to him, read 'Govindananda ar'), and that I deceived Nagendra Babu by forging 'Govinda Karmakar.'" Prachyavidyamaharnava Nagendranath Basu has sent me the following letter in reply to this charge:

"Dear friend, I see that a number of articles have come out against you concerning Govinda Dasa's Karcha. In the Ananda Bazar Patrika of the 8th of Phalgun, in an article 'Historical Research or Conjuring?', the writer says that in the old manuscript of Jayananda the reading was 'Nityananda Prabhu Govindananda ar.' 'Afterwards, when this manuscript was printed by the Bangiya Sahitya Parishat, a marvellous mystery of creation came to light within this reading. The reading turned into "Nityananda Prabhu Govinda Karmakar".[^12] Who will unravel the secret of this mystery of creation?'

<!-- p. 32 -->

The Chaitanya-mangala of Jayananda published by the Sahitya Parishat was edited by the two of us, myself and the late Kalidas Nath. The writer says this edition was made with the help of a single manuscript; in fact it was not so. I have seen many manuscripts of the Chaitanya-mangala, and in them the reading is precisely 'Govinda Karmakar'; nowhere has the reading 'Govindananda' been struck out and 'Govinda Karmakar' put in. It is needless to say that the writer has written a falsehood. Yours, Sri Nagendranath Basu. 22.2.25."

The charge that the Karcha says Chaitanya-deva was attached to harlots and was a Sahajiya is another gross perversion of the truth. Below I quote in full the passage of Govinda's Karcha that speaks of a harlot named Satyabai (pp. 24–26). Let the reader judge whether Mahaprabhu's character has been brightened or tarnished by this writing. It is said that once Airavata [the elephant] was swept away in the current of the holy Ganges. There is no counting the sinners and sufferers who were swept away in the same manner by the impetuous Ganges-stream of Mahaprabhu's bhakti and touched by its holy power. Reading the following lines, the reader will catch a glimpse of the unclouded beauty of that Ganges of bhakti. Those who can draw such a monstrous perverse meaning from it — one cannot bring oneself to call them sincere with their own hearts. It seems rather that they are deliberately perverting the truth to inflame people. This is what the Karcha says:

At that time he had wholly lost consciousness in ecstasy. He even trampled the body of the wealthy Tirtharam: "[The Lord] trampled him underfoot, unaware of the outer world."

Quoting the line "Clasping Satya in his arms he says, 'Say Hari,'" one opponent of the Karcha writes: "In this place there are further words tainted with obscenity. We do not quote them" (Ananda Bazar, 12 Magh 1331). Not content with putting the worst possible construction on the words, the writer goes on to frighten his readers with false bogeys. I can say with certainty that there is no one who, after reading the passage quoted, could put any other meaning on Mahaprabhu's utterly pure devotion to God. What shines out in the passage quoted is precisely that he came to save souls scorched by suffering with the Ganges-stream of bhakti. Here is a pure nectar which one earns the right to drink only by becoming as pure as an infant at its mother's breast; there is no room here for the leech's habit.

One party is blinded by prejudice; the other resorts to all manner of disgraceful means. Through the efforts of these two parties the agitation against the Karcha is spreading in all directions.

### The Karcha's Distinctive Place in Present-Day Vaishnava Literature

But however widely the agitation may spread, I am firmly convinced of one thing: it is no longer in anyone's power to destroy the Karcha. A handful of people have whipped up this agitation among idle folk. But the broad-minded Vaishnava community has accepted this book as one of the principal authoritative works on the life of Chaitanya-deva. Although Sisir Kumar Ghosh of the Amrita Bazar

<!-- p. 34 -->

and Motilal Ghosh had doubts about the genuineness of the text of the Karcha up to page 51 (page 21 in the present edition) — and the reason why has already been written above — still, they were men who could appreciate merit. Both brothers deeply felt the incomparable excellence of the Karcha. The articles on the Karcha that the revered Sisir Kumar Ghosh published in the Sri Vishnupriya Patrika in the Gauranga year 407 show his high opinion of this book. In the Gauranga year 410 the respected Motilal Ghosh published an article on the Karcha in the Sri Vishnupriya Patrika. Part of it is quoted below:

"A book about the lila of Sri Gauranga so valuable and so full of events is extremely rare. ** Throughout the Lord's travels Govinda was always with him and saw all his doings with his own eyes. ** So it is needless to say that his accounts are especially vivid and minutely detailed. Indeed, on reading Govinda's descriptions one must admit that he wrote down the Lord's deeds having seen them with his own eyes. Without seeing with one's own eyes it would be very hard to give such a description. ** Every Bengali ought to read this book; and whoever reads it will have to admit that such a description could not be produced by imagination. ** But in the Sri Chaitanya-charitamrita we see that only Krishnadasa went South with the Lord; there is not a whiff of Govinda's name in it. How, then, can Govinda's Karcha be believed? The answer is this: although Govinda's name is not found in the Chaitanya-charitamrita, that does not prove that Govinda did not go South with the Lord. ** Krishnadasa Kaviraja wrote down Krishnadasa's story in his book much later, from hearsay. ** After the southern tour such great events occurred in the Lord's life that Dasa Goswami and others (who furnished Krishnadasa Kaviraja with his material) would naturally not have taken much pains to give an exact account of such trifling matters as who went South with him. ** He too learned this account by report; probably because Krishnadasa brought to Nadia the news of the Lord's return from the South, people came to believe that he alone had been the Lord's companion in the South. ** However that may be, Govinda's Karcha cannot be disbelieved. ** It is impossible even to imagine that such a book was written without seeing with one's own eyes."

### The Agitation against Travellers

Attempts to discredit travellers' books by seizing them by the topknot and giving them a shake are nothing new in the world. The Portuguese traveller Mendez Pinto came to South India at almost the same time as the Karcha (1537–1558 CE). At one time a number of people made a great outcry that his travel narrative was utterly incredible; Congreve, in his play "Love for Love," even wrote, in rebuke of a certain liar: "Thou crown of liars! Thou art Ferdinand Mendez Pinto come again!" (Act 2, Scene 5) [Sen's Bengali rendering; Congreve's actual line is "Ferdinand Mendez Pinto was but a type of thee, thou liar of the first magnitude"]. This remark shows how widespread Pinto's ill repute was. But now opinion about him has changed completely. Reading the introduction by M. A. Vambery, editor of the latest edition of his book (1855), one will be convinced of the truth of his descriptions.

Marco Polo is the emperor among travellers, but Vambery says: "This crest-jewel of travellers was somewhat indifferent to the prevailing religious beliefs, and for that reason he was for a long time an object of contempt and ridicule to the Italians." The accounts he wrote are valuable, genuine pictures of their time; yet for a long time he was disregarded as a liar.[^16]

### The Distinctive Features of the Karcha

In our day supernatural events will not be accepted as historical evidence. Such facts as "the slaying of hundred-shouldered Ravana" or "Hanuman's leap across the ocean" will no longer count as historical truth. The time is past when Chaitanya could be kept hidden under the imprints of tilaka and the leaves of tulasi. The civilized world will not now accept his Boar form. The educated public no longer wishes to hear that he terrified a sinner with the Sudarshana discus; rather, they want to know with what unerring aim of love the wayfarer who had lost his way, fallen into a well, and reached the last extremity of vice, came back by his grace to the straight path of righteousness. The Karcha has abundant examples of this kind.

The chief merit of the Karcha is that its pictures come alive through the assembling of small details. All the descriptions in the Karcha are fresh and living because they consist of things heard with one's own ears and seen with one's own eyes. Describing Chaitanya Prabhu's house: "The house above the Ganges, most charming; five large rooms, lovely to see" (p. 4); of Shachi-devi: "Shachi-devi, gentle of form, very short of stature, ever calls out 'Nimai, Nimai!'" (p. 4); of Vishnupriya: "Modest, gentle, a soft smile on her lips; I became the servant of her feet"; of Advaitacharya: "Grey hair and grey beard, most winning; his beard falls, covering all his chest" (p. 4); of Khanja Acharya: "The lame Acharya comes in deep love; lame he is, yet he comes before all the rest" (p. 84); of Balaramadasa: "Rama is a great master at blowing the horn; Balarama Dasa comes thrilled with joy" (p. 84); of Ramadasa: "Ramadasa is very skilled at beating the bheri drum; that is why he comes every day to the kirtan" (p. 86); at Narayangarh: "He ate five gandas [twenty] of laddus and filled his belly"; at Kashi Mishra's house: "I eat eight fried bitter gourds (karela) with relish" (p. 14 [so the scan]); and the receiving of "two coconuts as alms" (p. 48) near Chandapur — these and other small details carry with them the breath of reality. That is why Moti Babu wrote, "Without seeing with one's own eyes it would be very hard to give such a description." And the Munsif Sri Manmatha Kumar Ray, B.L., wrote in the paper Forward: "Such a book full of so many and so varied geographical and historical details could not be written by any man unless he personally visited and witnessed the happening of the events recorded in the Karcha." Where and when Chaitanya Prabhu went and how many days he stayed, his companion noted down as he went along the road. Chaitanya-deva set out from Puri "on the seventh day of Vaishakha"

<!-- p. 39 -->

(p. 21). "On the last day of Ashvina, having come back to the city of Baroda, the Lord sings the name of Hari" (p. 76). "On the full-moon day of Magha" he bathed in the Tamraparni (p. 42). "On the first of Ashvina we go to Dvaraka" (p. 73). And: "On the third day of Magha my Gora-raya, with his companions, arrives at Puri" (p. 84). Such descriptions are not one or two but many. Seeing the way an eyewitness speaks, no one is likely to be mistaken.

Those who have read the Chaitanya-charitamrita, the Chaitanya-bhagavata and other books are acquainted with many wondrous sports of Chaitanya-deva: roaring in the form of the Boar and holding on the tip of his tusk a water-pot representing the Earth; in the form of the Lion striking his claws into the Kazi's breast; in the form of Damodara eating alone the food of many; in the form of Krishna reclining on Ananta, lying as an infant on the back of a terrible black serpent — I have spoken of this already. Vaishnavas must accept all of this. If they do not, there is the curse of Kaviraja Goswami: "He who does not believe in the supernatural sports ruins both this world and the next." "Listen with faith; do not argue in your mind" (Chaitanya-charitamrita, Madhya, ch. 7, v. 78, and ch. 8, v. 192). The Chaitanya-bhagavata does not stop at threats of the next world, but takes some of the punishment in this world into its own hands as well. For anyone who disbelieves the supernatural sports it describes, it prescribes as follows: "Then I kick him on the head."

So the historian, at the risk of all these kicks and cuffs and the ruin of this world and the next, must tread a little carefully, must he not? But set the picture drawn in the Karcha beside Mahaprabhu's fierce, terrible (bhairava) sports. Govinda Dasa has not painted him as wondrous with this glory and might of the supernatural. In one place: "A single torn outer cloth — the guise of a madman" (p. 61); in another: "Smeared with dust, his hair twisted into a knot, speaking of nothing else; Nimai walks the road saying 'Krishna, Krishna'" (p. 33); and elsewhere: "Everyone calls the Lord 'mad Haribol'; to tease him, how many people shout 'Haribol!'" (p. 39). At Rasalkunda an old Brahmin abuses him as "a hypocrite and a scoundrel," yet he, though given ample cause for anger, does not summon the Sudarshana discus to crush the sinner. In one place: "Three nights went by under a tree; fasting, without food, he eats nothing; tears stream down his breast; a hundred calls and not a word, like a madman" (p. 29). "His body wasted with hunger, he cannot walk — yet the Lord gives the name of Hari from house to house. The people of that country jabber 'kai-mai' [in a tongue not understood] — yet Chaitanya Gosai hands out the Name. Whoever sees the Lord once has no strength to go away" (p. 29). And when he gives the name of Krishna: "A fragrance poured forth, enchanting all; lost to himself, Gaurahari sings the Name. All eyes are on the Lord's face;

<!-- p. 40 -->

tears fall ceaselessly. Great Marathas come in throngs, and all sit listening to the Name. Looking behind me I see hundreds of gentlewomen standing. Countless Vaishnavas, Shaivas and sannyasis come and listen to the name of Hari with eyes closed. All listen to the name of Hari with devotion; the women wipe their tears with the ends of their saris" (p. 51).

Is not this fasting, emaciated Chaitanya-deva dearer to us for his sweetness than the incarnation of the gluttonous Damodara or the world-destroying Nrisimha? The Karcha may be but a handful of alms from the life of Chaitanya, but it is a handful of gold.

The names of those, other than Vaishnava devotees, who met Mahaprabhu from time to time have been left out of the famous Vaishnava books, for later Vaishnava society had no need to remember the names of such people. Govinda Dasa, being present on those occasions, jotted down their names then and there. At Chaitanya Prabhu's taking of sannyasa we find, alongside the Vaishnava devotees, the names of several unknown yet great pandits: "Chanda Chandeshvara, crest-jewel of pandits," Shambhuchandra, Kashishvara Nyayaratna, Siddheshvara, Ramaratna, Panchanana Vaidantika and others (p. 12). How could anyone else know the names of the places he mentions during the Deccan tour without seeing them? The name of Purnanagara (Poona), of course, is on the map. But the "Accha-sarovara" [Clear Lake] that is there is not on the map. On inquiry I have learned that the "Accha-sarovara" described by Govinda is still there. And who could have known that the god Khandoba is there? Who could have written, without seeing, of the wretched Muralis, the temple servant-women (sevadasi) there? Inquiry has shown that the sevadasi Muralis still live at the temple of Khandoba. Bhilpantha, Naroji, Bhargadeva, Bhavabhuti Sheth, Adi Narayana, Dhundiram, Balaji, Tanna Maharaj, Madhavendra-bhuja [reading uncertain], Dakorji, Ranchhodji and other outlandish names — is it natural to make them up out of one's head? In the very places in connection with which these names are mentioned, names of that kind are current to this day.

There is no counting the small details in the Karcha that no one could write without having seen with his own eyes: "Seeing that Jharikhanda, the body trembles. Going a long way, we found a small canal; there the beloved [Lord] bathes" (p. 72). Of a wild fruit: "The tree that stands like the four-ridged cactus (chaushira sij); the fruit is shaped like the carambola (kamranga)" (p. 71). At Kinjukanchi [reading uncertain]: "Every day payasa (rice cooked in milk) is made from two maunds of milk" (p. 32). Of Trikala-Ishvara: "His gauripatta [the base of the linga] measures four cubits" (p. 32). On the bank of the river Bhadra: "We eat champi fruits — whatever we get as alms" (p. 32). In the Shiva temple of Tripatrapura: "If one shouts 'Bom!' in his temple, the echo goes round for a whole danda [about 24 minutes]" (p. 37).

<!-- p. 41 -->

Leaving Chandrapur: "Two days and two nights we go, cutting through the mountains; in all that while, search as we may, no village or hamlet. The road is very hard, we cannot go on; we see only rows of kadamba trees" (p. 48). Such descriptions will never seem to spring from imagination.

On the very first page of the Karcha it is written that Govinda Dasa's wife quarrelled with him and abused him as worthless and a fool. Thus humiliated by his wife, Govinda Dasa left home. When Chaitanya, after taking sannyasa, was passing along the road through Kanchananagar, Shashimukhi, getting the news, came running, fell at Govinda's feet and began to lament piteously. Chaitanya-deva tried to calm her with philosophical words, but "hearing these words of the Lord, she suddenly covered her eyes with the end of her sari and began to weep." At the good wife's pitiful weeping, "compassion arose in the Lord, and at once, turning round, he began to speak to me. The Lord says: 'Govinda, you stay at home; I will go to Puri with another servant'" (p. 13).

But how can one who has tasted Chaitanya's company be bound again by worldly life? Govinda Dasa slipped out of Shashimukhi's hands, brushed aside all the arguments of his friends and kin, and followed Chaitanya-deva again. Then, when two years later Chaitanya-deva returned to Puri after his Deccan tour, Shashimukhi no doubt made inquiries about him through others. In those days the road to Puri was not easy. From the accounts of the outrages of the ghatiyals (toll-collectors at the river crossings) on the Puri road in Premadasa's Chaitanya-chandrodaya-kaumudi, it seems that anyone who had to go from Bengal to Puri in those days would be filled with dread. A few Bengali devotees used to go from time to time to see Mahaprabhu, relying on the protection of powerful men like Shivananda Sena. The outrages of the ghatiyals were so terrible that once even Shivananda Sena was imprisoned by them.

At that time (the first half of the sixteenth century) the road to Puri was extremely hard for women. So Shashimukhi probably got news of her husband through others. Just as Chaitanya had once ordered Govinda to go back home, so now, seeing Shashimukhi's distress, he might perhaps do the same; probably from this fear Govinda Dasa was compelled to hide himself. In the first part of the Karcha he gives a detailed account of himself, and his name is found on many leaves; so, for fear of being found out, he hid the Karcha away altogether: "I keep the record (karcha) in great secrecy," that is, he kept the Karcha hidden as far as he could. Probably that is also why we find in the Chaitanya-chandrodaya-kaumudi that when anyone asked him where his home was, he would say, "My home is in North Radha." Kanchananagar of course lies within North Radha, but his not mentioning the name of his village or giving any account of himself

<!-- p. 42 -->

seems to indicate a certain caution. Judging from what the Karcha reveals of Shashimukhi's character, if she had learned that her husband was in Puri, she would certainly have come to Puri across forest and jungle, and would not have gone back without melting Mahaprabhu's heart and "arresting" Govinda Dasa once more.

In this way Govinda Dasa kept the Karcha "in great secrecy," and he says so himself. For a long time this book remained hidden. That is why the author of the Chaitanya-bhagavata, the author of the Chaitanya-charitamrita and others could learn nothing about it. But that he was Mahaprabhu's companion at the time of his renunciation and somewhat before it, and that he went with him to the Deccan, is attested in several old books. That the name of Chaitanya's companion Govinda the Karmakar is mentioned in the Vairagya-khanda of Jayananda's Chaitanya-mangala I have already written. This Jayananda was a contemporary of Mahaprabhu. About 375 years ago the famous poet Balarama Dasa wrote very plainly in one of his padas that Chaitanya went to the Deccan taking Govinda with him (Gaurapada-tarangini, p. 404). In the Prabasi for Shravan 1332 (p. 421) the writer of a "scientific" article says that since Balarama Dasa mentions Govinda as Mahaprabhu's companion on the Deccan tour, one must of course believe that someone named Govinda went there with Chaitanya-deva. But what is the proof that this Govinda wrote the history of the Deccan tour, that is, the Karcha? (Yet on every leaf of the Karcha it is written that Govinda was Mahaprabhu's companion on the Deccan tour.) The science of this question has remained quite beyond my comprehension. Either reading the question muddled my head, or the questioner's head was not quite right when he asked it.

So it has now been proved by the testimony of old books that a man named Govinda the Karmakar was Chaitanya-deva's companion and travelled with the Lord in the Deccan, and the whole story of that journey is found in the Karcha! Persons of standing have testified that they saw the worm-eaten, decayed manuscript of that Karcha. Now the Prabasi writer asks: what is the proof that a manuscript is old just because it is worm-eaten and decayed? The writer is evidently trying very hard to be scientific; his caution makes us laugh. But Rai Bahadur Sarat Chandra Chattopadhyay, who saw this manuscript with Jaygopal Goswami more than 40 years ago, writes 'old', that is, ancient, and 'worn out condition', that is, in a very decayed state. One naturally shudders to think what further research-based, subtle scientific question that writer will ask after this.

### References to Govinda Dasa in Other Old Books

These few books are not all. In the Chaitanya-chandrodaya-kaumudi an account of Govinda Dasa is given. We take this Govinda to be the author of the Karcha. The manuscript of that book which we have obtained belongs to the University manuscript library and its number is 2145. The manuscript was composed in Shaka 1604.[^17] In the Karcha we see that at Chaitanya Prabhu's command Govinda Dasa was sent with his letter to Advaitacharya at Shantipur. In the Chaitanya-chandrodaya-kaumudi the author Premadasa very probably describes some later events in Govinda Dasa's life. It tells how Govinda Dasa first went to Srikhanda and, after meeting Narahari Sarkar, went to Advaita's house, and later, coming to Kanchrapara, returned with Shivananda Sena to Mahaprabhu at Puri. The caution with which he tried to hide himself on coming to Bengal is visible in this story. He described himself as "a stranger" and "a native of North Radha" — Kanchananagar of course lies within North Radha.

In Vrindavana Dasa's famous Chaitanya-bhagavata we see that Govinda was Chaitanya's companion at the time of his sannyasa; Achyuta Babu too has proved this well in the Sri Sri Vishnupriya-Gauranga. Here the words of the Karcha agree with those of the Chaitanya-bhagavata: "With Nityananda, Gadadhara and Mukunda; Govinda behind, Keshava Bharati in front" (Antya, ch. 1). The "distinguished Vaishnava" wrote in the Ananda Bazar Patrika of 26 Magh 1331 that the Govinda named in the Chaitanya-bhagavata as Mahaprabhu's companion at that time was "Govindananda." But that this Govinda is not "Govindananda," the brother of Vasu Ghosha, is shown in the Chaitanya-bhagavata itself: "Pramai [?], Govindananda, Sri Chandrashekhara, Vasudeva, Srigarbha, Sri Mukunda, Sridhara, Govinda, Jagadananda, Nandana Acharya." Here it is clearly shown that Govinda and Govindananda are two different persons. The Chaitanya-bhagavata mentions Govinda in one or two other places as well.[^18] Since nearly four hundred years ago Jayananda plainly stated that Govinda the Karmakar was Mahaprabhu's companion at and before his renunciation, and the famous poet Balarama Dasa also made it known a little less than four hundred years ago, and the Karcha sets that evidence in a bright light, we can certainly accept the mentions in the Chaitanya-bhagavata and the Chaitanya-chandrodaya-kaumudi as a further proof.

### Reply to the Charges

As already said, in order to blow away Govinda Dasa's Karcha it has been put in front of such cannon that any historical fortress would have been shattered and razed. Every line is subjected to hair-splitting scrutiny and dispute. Any narrative attacked in this way can be brought down; but the remarkable thing is that if the muzzle of this cannon were turned a little and aimed at the authoritative books of the orthodox Vaishnavas, they would be blown away in a day. These are events of four and a quarter centuries ago, so many of the charges rest merely on imagination and conjecture. Some of these charges are simply ridiculous. A certain well-known person among those who thought the first edition of the Karcha forged up to page 51 made the following charges in the Sri Vishnupriya Patrika in the month of Kartik of the Gauranga year 410.

1. The Karcha says that when Govinda's belly swelled, Chaitanya Prabhu passed his holy hand over his body. According to the accuser this is wholly incredible, because Chaitanya-deva is God himself. That he should go stroking the body of a lowly servant is impossible.

2. The Karcha says (according to the accuser) that at Kashi Mishra's house "the Lord ate his fill." But there is nothing in the Karcha about eating "his fill." It does say that he ate; but the accuser says, "After taking sannyasa he (Mahaprabhu) all but gave up eating rice, and sustained life by taking a tiny amount through the nose."

Such an astonishing thing we hear here for the first time. It is needless to waste words on the various fantastic notions of this orthodox Vaishnava party. The passage from the Karcha is quoted here: "The Lord says, 'This rice is very fine. By what name do people call it?' Mishra says, 'Its name is Jagannatha-bhoga; when it is offered as bhoga every wish is fulfilled.' Hearing 'Jagannatha-bhoga' the Lord started, and at once streams of love began to flow" (p. 14). This description agrees with the Chaitanya-charitamrita's account of Mahaprabhu's eating prasada at Puri: "As soon as he took a little and put it on his tongue … tasting the savour of millions of nectars, the Lord was amazed; his whole body thrilled, streams of tears flowed from his eyes" (Chaitanya-charitamrita, Antya).

3. The objector has written that Govinda Dasa's statement that he ate Mahaprabhu's leavings is also not credible, since hundreds of Brahmins were present to eat his leavings. There is no need to answer this argument.

<!-- p. 45 -->

4. A curious view of the Prabasi writer (Shravan 1332) is that the Karcha was not forged in recent times; it was forged in 1512 or 1513 CE. He says that very probably "after the Lord's return some devotee wrote down from his companion Krishnadasa (or whoever his companion was) the names of the holy places of the South; or, when the Lord's devotees heard these accounts on the first night after his arrival at Puri, someone may have made a record (karcha) of them then." With conjecture and imagination one can compose a novel, but one cannot write history.

4 [so numbered in the original]. The Prabasi writer informs us that Govinda Dasa was an old man when he travelled with Mahaprabhu. A line is quoted as proof. Govinda writes: "I keep the record (karcha) as best I can." He was not lettered, and so could not properly write down Chaitanya Prabhu's disputations with the pandits of the South. He wrote what he could, according to his own slender ability. That is surely the plain meaning of this line; how it became proof of Govinda's old age I have failed to understand.

5. The Prabasi says that in some parts of the Deccan rice is the staple food, but the author of the Karcha nowhere writes that Mahaprabhu received rice as alms. The writer of the article does not seem to have read the Karcha carefully. In the forest of Chorananda: "Some bring firewood and sugar, some rice; some milk, some ghee, some fruits and roots" (p. 56) — alms of rice are mentioned there and in other places.

We have no time to busy ourselves with these trifling charges. But we shall say something about the other mistaken notions over which the opponents are making a great agitation.
