## References to Govinda Dasa in Old Vaishnava Literature

"Having delivered Nilachala, taking Govinda with me, I shall go to the southern country."
— A pada of the poet Balarama Dasa, 375 years old. Gaurapada-tarangini, Sahitya Parishat edition, p. 404.

"Mukunda Datta the Vaidya, Govinda the Karmakar! Come with me to Katwa, on the bank of the Ganges."
— Jayananda's Chaitanya-mangala, 400 years old. Sahitya Parishat edition, Vairagya-khanda, p. 83.

"With Nityananda, Gadadhara and Mukunda; Govinda behind, Keshava Bharati in front."
— Vrindavana Dasa's Chaitanya-bhagavata, 400 years old, Antya, ch. 2.

"Then, with Nityananda and Gadadhara, taking Bharati along, [the Lord] set out in many a joyful mood. Behind them I follow, chalk in hand [for writing]."

— Chaitanya-chandrodaya-kaumudi, composed by Premadasa in Shaka 1634. Manuscript library of the University of Calcutta, MS No. 2145, folio 148.

## Dedication

He whose love of God —
released by the matted-lock austerities
of that Shiva-like best of men —
turned the abode of Navadvipa into a second Hardwar
and took on visible form;
the herald of the good tidings of bhakti, beloved disciple of Madhavendra Puri —
Sri Advaita Prabhu, purifier of the world:
to his descendant —
tried by endless persecution and ingratitude, established in truth,
Prabhupada the late Jaygopal Goswami, a man of great fortune,
who, following in the steps of his holy forefathers,
discovered Govinda Dasa's Karcha — a small branch of the Ganges of bhakti
lying hidden beneath the sand-banks of oblivion —
and so unveiled to view the painted image of the human play (nara-lila) of Gauranga Thakura —
to his sacred name
I dedicate this new edition of the Karcha.

Sri Dinesh Chandra Sen

## The History of the Recovery of Govinda Dasa's Karcha

<!-- p. 9 -->

About forty-five years ago Kalidas Nath of Shantipur brought several Vaishnava books (manuscripts, puthi) to my father, the late Jaygopal Goswami.[^1] Among them were "Govinda-dasa's Karcha" and a book called "Advaita-vikasha." Taking these two to be old, unpublished works, my father kept them to read. At first Kalidas hesitated to give them up, but at my father's earnest request he left the two old manuscripts with him for a few days. My father could write very fast, and within a few days he had copied out both manuscripts.

Some years later my father took two or three formes of this manuscript — in his own handwriting — to Sisir Babu [Sisir Kumar Ghosh]. My father had not known Sisir Babu before; but learning that he was a great devotee of Gauranga, he became eager to show it to him. The owner of an orchard is anxious to feed mangoes to one who loves mangoes and knows their worth. It was because Goswami Mahashaya thought Sisir Kumar a true connoisseur of the rasa of love for Gauranga that he took this song of Chaitanya's virtues to him. Sisir Babu knew merit when he saw it: after reading only a few pages of the Karcha he was charmed, and he asked my father to hand over the whole manuscript to him. But my father said, "I am a poor Brahmin, and I have resolved to publish this book myself. Some of my friends, learned devotees of Gauranga, tell me that publishing it may bring in a little money." Sisir Babu replied, "Then publish it yourself. Leave the few pages you have brought. I will read them and within seven

<!-- p. 10 -->

days send them back to you by registered post." Seven days slowly went by. The pages did not come back in time. My father grew anxious, because he had already — this was forty-five years ago — returned the original manuscript to Kalidas Nath. He wrote Sisir Babu several letters. Sisir Babu was a busy man. After waiting long for a reply and finally despairing, my father came to Calcutta himself and called on Sisir Babu. The moment he saw my father, Sisir Babu said, "I could not answer your letters; I am very much ashamed before you. I gave those few pages of Govinda Dasa's Karcha to Shambhu Babu to read, and he has lost them." My father came back disappointed and asked Kalidas Nath to get the old manuscript for him again, but Kalidas Nath said, "I have returned the book to the man I got it from; there is no chance of getting it back."

Some time after this my father learned that in Shantipur, in the house of the Pagla Goswamis, Harinath Goswami had another manuscript of Govinda Dasa's Karcha. This manuscript was badly marred by textual corruption and was incomplete. By comparing its text with some notes my father had, the lost leaves were recovered with great difficulty; the book was then given to the managers of the Sanskrit Press Depository to publish. In this way Govinda Dasa's Karcha was published in Shaka 1817 (1895 CE).

Sisir Babu tried to make out that Govinda Dasa was a Kayastha. But after Jayananda's Chaitanya-mangala was published, the public understood that Govinda was no Kayastha but exactly what the printed book says, that is, a true Karmakar (blacksmith). Whether Sisir Babu was somewhat put out by this, I cannot say. Perhaps he was also somewhat put out because my father could not grant his request and put the book into his hands. Be that as it may, he expressed doubt about the genuineness of the first few pages of the printed Karcha of Govinda Dasa. Nevertheless, he wrote the account of Mahaprabhu's southern tour in his Amiya-Nimai-charita on the basis of the Karcha, and his brother Moti Babu [Motilal Ghosh] in the Sri Vishnupriya Patrika again and again extolled the merits and historical value of the Karcha.

So, for various tangled reasons, a slight doubt arose about the first few pages; and now, after many years, that very doubt has become the ground for rejecting the whole manuscript. This agitation started from the Amrita Bazar [Patrika].

<!-- p. 11 -->

The publication of the Karcha was warmly encouraged by the late Madana Gopal Goswami of Shantipur, foremost among pandits and a great bhagavata. Many people saw the old manuscript at that time, and some of them are still alive. Elsewhere [in this book] the letters of Rai Bahadur Sarat Chandra Chattopadhyay and of the eminent pandit Lakshmikanta Tarkachudamani are printed; they saw that old book with their own eyes. I am the eldest son of the late Jaygopal Goswami; I am now 70 years old. The old manuscript was in our house for some time. I saw it too.

Whether Govinda Dasa was a Kayastha or a Karmakar is no concern of ours. The minute description of the Southern Road (Dakshinapatha) in this book is something no one could imagine who had not travelled all over the Deccan. Some of the villages mentioned in it are not marked even on the maps of the Survey Office. Alas, for those who are attacking my late father so very unjustly and want to make him out a cheat, there is no atonement for this grievous offence of reviling a Vaishnava. What pain these unjust and false charges are causing our hearts — what more can I write?

In some places in the book my father, while editing, replaced old difficult words. Perhaps now and then, where part of a line had been eaten by insects, he filled it in. Everyone who edits an old manuscript does this.

What I have written is the plain truth. In this dark Kali age there is never a lack of witnesses to prove night is day. So now "evidence" is turning up that we were "outcasted" (ek-ghare) for the offence of forging the manuscript. If our society were so wide awake today, the evil days of the country would be over! How many things happen in this country out of feuds between kinsmen! I pray to God to forgive those who are doing us all these wrongs.

Where now is that Vaishnava humility, and where that outpouring of bhakti? My ancestor Advaita Acharya won Chaitanya by severe austerity. Chaitanya is nearer to our family than the nearest kin; he is the object of our family's meditation and its stay. You may try to dim Chaitanya's glory, but a descendant of Advaita will never rush to do such a thing. There is no passage in the Karcha that detracts from Gaura's greatness; had there been, my father would not have published this Karcha.

Sri Banwari Lal Goswami.

[^1]: In a letter Banwari Lal has written me a few more things, namely: "I remember Kalidas said, 'The language of the Karcha is quite flawless; nowhere is it exaggerated; the book is full of clarity (prasada-guna).' An old unpublished book, and on top of that the simplicity and fluency of its language — this attracted my father intensely. The reading of Govinda Dasa's Karcha began at once. After only a few pages had been read, the late Madana Goswami Mahashaya arrived, and my father said eagerly, 'Madana, a wonderful book! Let me read it again from the start; listen.'" — Sri Dinesh Chandra Sen.

# Introduction (Bhumika)

## 1. The Old Manuscripts of the Karcha

<!-- p. 15 -->

Sri Banwari Lal Goswami, one of the editors of this book, has given a detailed account of how the Karcha was obtained. He is well known in the field of Bengali letters; his books "Khichuri," "Polao" and others have won a notable place in Bengali poetry. Goswami Mahashaya is now 70. Those who know him know his straightforward nature and his fire. In his zeal to speak hard truths he sometimes cannot keep the caution that is due.

Sri Banwari Lal Goswami's father, the late Jaygopal Goswami of Shantipur, published Govinda Dasa's Karcha. Banwari Lal was then about 40 and was his father's right hand in everything; so all he writes are events he witnessed himself. Jaygopal Goswami obtained two old manuscripts of the Karcha. The first was very decayed and worm-eaten. He got it from the late Kalidas Nath and made a copy of it. Several leaves of that copy were lost by Dr. Shambhu Mukhopadhyay, editor of the journal "Reis and Rayyet." Long before that, Goswami Mahashaya had returned the old manuscript. When he asked Kalidas Nath for it again, he did not get it: Nath Mahashaya had returned the manuscript to its owner and there was no chance of getting it back. So the incomplete copy lay with Goswami Mahashaya for some years. Then by good fortune another manuscript of the Karcha turned up with the late Harinath Goswami of Shantipur; it was fragmentary and full of errors. From the first part of this fragmentary, faulty manuscript and from the notes he had of the old manuscript, Jaygopal Goswami with great difficulty restored the lost portion. In 1895 (Shaka 1817) Govinda Dasa's Karcha was first published by the Sanskrit Press Depository, Calcutta.

### "Bring out the old manuscript!"

Some of those who have raised doubts about the authenticity of this Karcha say: "Bring out the old manuscript of the Karcha, and then we will believe." Both manuscripts

<!-- p. 16 -->

from which Goswami Mahashaya published the Karcha were returned to their owners, and it is now impossible to get them.

It is certain that in many places in this country treasures lie buried underground; but can anyone dig them out the moment he is told to? Such manuscripts are not to be had by writing to Newman's or Thacker's. The very people in whose houses old manuscripts lie often do not know of them. Recovering old handwritten manuscripts is no easy business. The collecting of Bengali manuscripts has barely begun. The Karcha itself hints that Govinda Dasa for some reason kept his book hidden; so its old copies certainly cannot be plentiful. On top of that, a vicious conspiracy is now being carried on against this manuscript.

Old Bengali manuscripts are mostly kept in the houses of people of the lower classes. In the rainy season streams of water pour through the holes in thatched roofs, and year after year hundreds of manuscripts perish. Then there are fires, floods and the mischief of children. Many people, moreover, now and then throw old manuscripts into the Ganges. Even of books by authors famous throughout the country, old manuscripts often cannot be found. There was an old manuscript of the Ramayana in the library of the Sahitya Parishat containing Krittibasa's long autobiographical account; I saw it, and so did Sri Hirendranath Datta and many others. That manuscript cannot be found now. Another Ramayana with the same autobiography, written in 1501 [so printed], was in the house of the late Haradhan Datta Bhaktinidhi of Badanganj, Hooghly; Rai Bahadur Jogesh Chandra Ray saw it. But it too is no longer to be found. In the libraries of the Sahitya Parishat and the University there are more than a hundred old manuscripts of Krittibasa's Ramayana, but none of them contains that autobiography, and it is not to be found anywhere else. Can anyone search it out of Bengal on demand? And must the autobiography be thrown out for the offence of not being found?

Nityananda Dasa, author of the Prema-vilasa, mentions again and again that he wrote a large life of Nityananda, but no old manuscript of that book can be found. Krishnadasa Kaviraja mentions in his Chaitanya-charitamrita, Madhya, chapter 25, verse 8, that he composed a large book called "Pancha-tattva-akhyana." No trace of it has been found to this day. Lala Jayanarayan Sen's "Harilila" was very famous in the Dacca region in the eighteenth century. After much effort the University of Calcutta bought an old manuscript of it for Rs. 200 from Sri Anandanath Ray, the historian of Faridpur,

<!-- p. 17 -->

and it was stolen. I could not get another copy of it by any means. I had a 200-year-old manuscript of a Bengali translation of a Smriti text; it was ruined by damp. For all my efforts I have not been able to find its match. All the authors mentioned have an established place in Bengali literature, and their books were widely circulated; yet when even their manuscripts have become rare, let no one cherish the vain hope that a manuscript of Govinda Dasa's little-circulated Karcha will turn up the moment it is asked for. At most it may turn up suddenly by chance. Old handwritten manuscripts apart, how many books printed a hundred years ago are now quite unobtainable — and a thousand or two thousand copies of them were printed!

This reminds me of something. There is still a group of people who say that there was no poet called Shakespeare and that the plays attributed to him were really written by Bacon. Their chief argument is this: "No manuscript in Shakespeare's hand is to be found. Tradition says he dedicated two books, Venus and Adonis and Lucrece, to the Earl of Southampton — but where is the actual letter of dedication?"

In answer to this question Mr. G. L. Pugh wrote in jest: "No letters preserved, therefore none were written."

[By the same logic:] "No manuscripts preserved, therefore no manuscript was ever written."[^2]

[^2]: "No letters preserved, therefore none were written." No Mss preserved, therefore none were written." — Statesman, February 10, 1926.

### Village Intrigue

When the wave of agitation against the Karcha reached Shantipur, there happened what usually happens in a village: some of Jaygopal Goswami's enemies seized on the matter and spread the story that they had seen with their own eyes Goswami Mahashaya sitting and composing this book out of his imagination, and that for the offence of composing it he had been "outcasted" (ek-ghare) in Shantipur.

In reply to this charge against his father Banwari Lal Goswami has written: "Now 'evidence' is turning up that we were 'outcasted' for forging the manuscript. If our society were so wide awake today, the evil days of the country would be over!"

<!-- p. 18 -->

We made inquiries about this of Sri Nalini Mohan Sanyal of Shantipur, retired school inspector of the Burdwan Division. Sanyal Mahashaya is now 64. The substance of his letter, written in English, is given below[^3]: "Dear Dr. Sen, you have asked whether Pandit Jaygopal Goswami of Shantipur was 'outcasted' by the Vaishnava community there on the charge of forging Govinda Dasa's Karcha. I belong to the Vaishnava community of Shantipur. Our family and Goswami Mahashaya's have been intimate for generations. I am now 63. I never heard that he was 'outcasted' in Shantipur. I believe Govinda Dasa's Karcha to be an excellent authentic work.

Yours,
Sri Nalini Mohan Sanyal.
26th March, 1925."

Besides Sanyal Mahashaya's letter I have received several more. I quote some of them in part in a footnote.[^4] On a later page I shall quote the letter of Rai Bahadur Sarat Chandra Chattopadhyay. Anyone who reads all these letters will see that this agitation, having entered Shantipur, has created village factionalism, and that there is therefore no reason for us to break into a sweat

<!-- p. 19 -->

over it. Even if it were true that Jaygopal Goswami had been "outcasted," in the field of letters that would not count as proof of any offence on his part, since village "outcastings" are almost always a matter of enmity.

[^3]: The English original: "My Dear Dr. Sen, You have asked me to let you know if the late Pandit Joy Gopal Goswami was boycotted by the Vaisnava community of Santipur for having published the Karcha of Govinda Das alleged to have been forged by the Pandit. I belong to the Vaisnava community of Santipur and am now 63 years old. My family and the venerable Pandit's family were in close touch with each other for genrations [sic]. I never heard of his being boycotted at Santipur. I believe the Karcha published by him to be a genuine historical work of great merit. Yours sincerely, (Sd) Nalini Mohan Sanyal, 26th. March, 1925."

[^4]: On this point Pandit Harilal Goswami of Shantipur, a man of about eighty, writes: "For all my inquiries I could not learn that the revered Jaygopal Goswami Mahashaya was ever 'outcasted' over Govinda Dasa's Karcha; on the contrary, everyone said he was never outcasted." Sri Kirtishchandra Goswami of Shantipur writes: "Respected Sir, I have received your letter of 5.3.25. That the venerable Jaygopal Goswami Mahashaya, after bringing out Govinda Dasa's Karcha, suffered social punishment for forgery or was 'outcasted' is a baseless report. I myself never heard any such report, nor could I learn of it from anyone. I asked Sri Ramachandra Goswami, Vice-Chairman of the local municipality, about this; he said he knew of no such thing."

### Those Who Saw the Old Manuscript

The manuscript came into Goswami Mahashaya's hands some 14 or 15 years before the Karcha was published. That was 45 or 46 years ago. At that time many people saw the manuscript Kalidas Nath had given. Many of the people of that day are no longer alive, but happily there are still two or three educated and accomplished men who saw it. The name of the famous pandit of Bakla, Lakshminarayan Tarkachudamani,[^5] is known to many. He is now 80. He has written me the following letter:

"Forty-five or forty-six years ago, while I was living at Keota near Hooghly, I saw a manuscript of Govinda Dasa's Karcha with a Brahmin devoted to Hari, the late Gorachand Chakravarti. The manuscript was worm-eaten and decayed. He was copying it and often called me to make out obscure passages; that is why I remember much of it. Now I see Govinda Dasa's Karcha, compiled by the late Jaygopal Goswami, in print. I believe the manuscript I saw with Chakravarti Mahashaya and this printed book to be one and the same. The more I read the book compiled by Jaygopal Goswami, the more my earlier impressions come back to life.

Yours obediently,
Sri Lakshminarayan Bhattacharya."

We have received another letter from Rai Bahadur Sri Sarat Chandra Chattopadhyay, M.A., B.L., retired Government Pleader of Rangpur, brother of the High Commissioner, the civilian Sir Atul Chandra Chattopadhyay. He wrote me the letter on 24 March 1925; it is quoted in a footnote,[^6] and the substance of part of it is given below.

<!-- p. 20 -->

"Dear Sir, I have received your kind letter of the 19th. I knew the late Pandit Jaygopal Goswami of Shantipur well. At one time I had the honour of his friendship. I saw him copying an old manuscript of Govinda Dasa's Karcha. He was copying it in order to publish it. That would be more than 40 years ago. The manuscript was then in a very decayed state, and that, I suppose, is why he was making a copy of it."

The letter of Sri Harilal Goswami of Shantipur shows that some of those who saw the manuscript of the Karcha are still alive in Shantipur. Besides, Banwari Lal Goswami, who is now 70, and his brother Sri Mohan Lal Goswami, who is now 60, saw this manuscript themselves and know all the circumstances connected with it.

However, feuds among kinsmen have gone on in our society unabated from the time of Kurukshetra to the present day. A kinsman of Goswami Mahashaya, Sri Radhavinod Goswami, has said at a public meeting in Dacca that he himself knows that Jaygopal Goswami forged Govinda Dasa's Karcha and was "outcasted" in Shantipur for it. There is no need to relate here the quarrels that arose on this occasion. But Pandit Banwari Lal Goswami has written to me — and several residents of Shantipur, Sri Nalini Mohan Sanyal among them, have supported him — that when the Karcha was obtained Radhavinod Goswami had not even been born; or if he had, he was crawling on all fours in the courtyard of his own house.

<!-- p. 21 -->

Another gentleman of Shantipur says: "Goswami Mahashaya, having lost a few pages of the manuscript, sat idle for a long time; it was I who advised him to forge those few leaves." And, just as a boy given sweets from the confectioner's shop gulps them down at once, Goswami Mahashaya is supposed to have taken this good advice on the spot and forged those few pages.

If Goswami Mahashaya had composed the book himself, why should he sit idle for so many years after losing a few pages? He could have made them good himself without delay.

The wrangling of Shantipur, thorny with factions and poisoned by feuds among kinsmen, will not help in the least to establish the real facts. I therefore drop the discussion of this subject.

## 2. The History of the Opponents' Agitation

The opponents' agitation did not first begin in Shantipur. It began in the Amrita Bazar office. Having read the copy of the Karcha,[^7] the late Sisir Kumar Ghosh became a warm partisan of the book. He asked Goswami Mahashaya to leave two formes of the copy with him for a few days. Dr. Shambhu Mukhopadhyay took them from him and lost them. Relying on a vague memory of those two formes, Sisir Babu began writing about the Karcha in the "Amiya-Nimai-charita," and in that book, through a slip of memory, he called Govinda Dasa a Kayastha. Then, some years later, when the Karcha was published, he saw that in the printed book Govinda Dasa was called a Karmakar. Meanwhile that volume of his Amiya-Nimai-charita had already been printed. So, to keep his own statements consistent, he declared that the first part of the Karcha was not authentic. In the first edition the Karcha runs to 227 printed pages in all. Of these, the genuineness of the part up to page 51, to the line "Clasping [the Lord's] knees, Rama Raya weeps" (that is, the part that had been lost), was declared doubtful. Of what was done in this connection against the simple-hearted old Jaygopal Goswami I shall say nothing for the present. At that time Goswami Mahashaya came to my lodgings at No. 12, Shyampukur Lane, and told me the whole story

<!-- p. 22 -->

with great feeling. He told me how, some years after the copy was lost, he had with great labour restored the text of the lost leaves from another fragmentary manuscript obtained from Harinath Goswami of Shantipur and from his own notes. But when a man like Sisir Babu said that the manuscript had read "Kayastha" and not "Karmakar," a party of people rose up to prove with great vehemence that these 51 pages of the Karcha were forged. So this controversy arose over a question of caste.

This protest perhaps arose for another reason too. Banwari Lal Goswami Mahashaya has hinted at that as well. When Pandit Jaygopal came to Sisir Babu with the copy of the Karcha, Sisir Babu expressed the intention of bringing the book out himself from the Amrita Bazar Patrika office and asked the Pandit Mahashaya for it, and the Pandit Mahashaya did not give it. Had he given it, and had the book come out from the Amrita Bazar Patrika office, there would probably have been no agitation against it.

So we have a history of how the first attack on this book began. When anything starts in this country its waves go on for a long time, for the Bengali has plenty of time for idle pursuits. So there is nothing surprising in the fact that the agitation that started then is still going on.

But the drum of truth soon sounded. In the meantime the Sahitya Parishat published a very old book, Jayananda's "Chaitanya-mangala." Sri Prachyavidyamaharnava Nagendranath Basu and the late Kalidas Nath edited it from several old manuscripts. The Vairagya-khanda of this book states plainly that Mahaprabhu's companion at his sannyasa was "Govinda the Karmakar." This discovery silenced the opponents. Even so, they once tried to dismiss the whole manuscript of Jayananda's Chaitanya-mangala as a forgery. But their attempt came to nothing after an unanswerable reply by Nagendra Babu published in volume 5 of the Sahitya Parishat Patrika; and when Nagendra Babu then went himself to Deoghar and showed Sisir Babu the text of a 200- or 300-year-old manuscript of Jayananda's Chaitanya-mangala, Sisir Babu had no further reason for disbelief. Everyone then understood that Jaygopal Goswami had not struck out the reading "Kayastha" and put in "Karmakar" out of his own imagination. After this the opponents kept complete silence for some 27 or 28 years. The zeal for unjust accusations against the old Brahmin was quenched for a long time.
