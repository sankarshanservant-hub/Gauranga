# Nagendranath Vasu. The Poet Jayananda and the Chaitanya-mangala (1897)

*Sahitya-Parishat-Patrika, vol. 4, 1304 B.S. (1897), pp. 196–230.*[^1]

*[p. 196]*

At the time when, with the rising of the moon of Navadvipa,[^2] Bengal took on a new aspect under the wondrous waves of love of the hundreds of devotees he had favoured — in those happy days of the Vaishnavas the poet Jayananda was born. Many will now take the name of this fine poet for a new one; and indeed, since the name of this great soul lay hidden in worm-eaten old manuscripts, many of us had never before heard even the name of this Vaishnava poet. Yet there was a day when the sweet songs of Jayananda were heard in every village and hamlet of Bengal, and hundreds of people, beside themselves, listened to those sweet lays. That song-book of Jayananda is called the Chaitanya-mangala.

A copy of this Chaitanya-mangala has recently come into our hands. Unfortunately the manuscript is incomplete: folios 1, 17 to 36, and 38 to 40 are missing; all the other folios are present. The number of the last folio is 76. As can be seen, nearly a third of the book has been lost. In spite of much searching we have been unable to collect the remaining leaves.

At the end of the manuscript the year and date of copying, the scribe's name and so on are written thus:

> "Here ends the Sri Chaitanya-mangala in nine khandas. [Written] as seen, etc.[^3] The writing of Sri Dharmadasa Acharya. Shaka year 16[0]1,[^v1] month of Chaitra, on Thursday, in the dark fortnight, on the sixth lunar day, in the third watch of the day, the book of Sri […][^4] Dasa was completed. Thus, 2 san 9m,[^v2] date 13 Chaitra."

According to this concluding statement of the book, our manuscript is 218 years old.[^5] Judging by the condition of the manuscript and the style of the writing, this seems to be correct.

The number of shlokas in the complete manuscript would come, at a rough estimate, to about four thousand; but we shall discuss only what we have obtained. Someone may say: if a third of the manuscript cannot be found, what is the use of discussing a fragmentary manuscript? The proper course is to discuss it when the complete manuscript is found. Now, whatever in this *[p. 197]* fragmentary manuscript is found unclear or lacking may perhaps become much clearer when the complete manuscript is found.

But what we have obtained has so increased our interest and enthusiasm that we could not refrain from making these highly important matters known to the public. Perhaps it may not be to the liking of many; but thinking that it will be of some interest to friends who love old Bengali literature, who inquire into Vaishnava doctrine and who are fond of antiquities, we have today ventured to put forward this essay.

From as much of the manuscript as has been found we shall be able to learn the following:

1. Particulars of the author.

2. A list of a number of principal works composed in Bengali before the poet.

3. The condition of Srihatta and Navadvipa at that time.

4. A clearer description of some matters which the biographers of Chaitanya have not clearly mentioned.

5. The state of the Bengali language at that time.

6. Specimens of the poet's poetic gift.

Besides these principal matters we find in this fragmentary book many other smaller things worth knowing. It is for this reason that we have chosen this manuscript for discussion.

## Particulars of the Poet

In various places in the book[^v3] the poet Jayananda gives an account of himself thus:

> 1. "In the month of Vaishakha, on the bright twelfth lunar day,
> Jayananda was born, in the house of his maternal grandfather.
>
> His name was Guiya, because his mother's children died;[^6]
> he received the name Jayananda by Chaitanya's grace.
>
> Jayananda's father is Subuddhi Mishra Gosani,
> a supreme bhagavata, beyond compare.
>
> Formerly [he was] the Gosani's disciple; in the writing of books
> he himself […] taught all his disciples.[^7]
>
> By the fruit of the austerities of his father, Subuddhi Mishra,
> Jayananda was born for the Chaitanya-mangala."
> (fol. 2, p. 2, lines 3–4)[^8]
>
> 2. "In the month of Vaishakha, on the bright twelfth lunar day —
> on that day Jayananda was born.
>
> His name was Guiya, because his mother's children died;
> he received the name Jayananda by Chaitanya's grace.
>
> His mother, Rodani, is a handmaid of the sage Nityananda;[^9]
> born from her womb, I float in the bliss of Chaitanya.
>
> *[p. 198]*
>
> His uncles, younger and elder, are pashandas,[^10] with little devotion to Chaitanya.[^v4]
>
> Vaninatha Mishra kept six-night fasts;
> [like] Durvasa, Bharati, Vyasa, [he is] renowned in the world.[^11]
>
> His son is Mahananda Vidyabhushana,
> versed in all the shastras, endowed with every auspicious mark.
>
> His brother is Indriyananda, king of poets in Bharata;[^12]
> at an early age he left his body on earth.
>
> The elder uncle is Vaishnava Mishra, [who kept] the vow of [visiting] all tirthas;
> the younger brother [of the father] is Ramananda Mishra, a bhagavata.
>
> In the Bandyaghati line they worship Raghunatha;
> among them Jayananda is devoted to Chaitanya.
>
> Thus far the Vairagya-khanda is completed;
> the mind has resolved to sing the Sannyasa-khanda.
>
> Meditating on the two feet of Chaitanya and Gadadhara,
> 'The Vairagya-khanda is completed,' sings Jayananda." (43.2.2–8)
>
> 3. "Jayananda's father is Subuddhi Mishra Gosani,
> who has nothing but meditation on Chaitanya's feet.
>
> Meditating on the two feet of Chaitanya and Gadadhara,
> Jayananda joyfully sings the Tirtha-khanda." (54.1.6–7)[^13]
>
> 4. "Chaitanya set out for the land of Gauda
> by special command of Sri Jagannatha.
>
> Passing Tungana, Bhadrak-para, leaving Asuragarh,
> he lodged in the town of Soro.[^14]
>
> By way of Remuna and Bansda[^15] he went to Dantan and stayed there;
> he spent the night at Jaleshvara.
>
> Leaving Devasharana, he entered Mandarana
> and appeared at Bardhamana.[^16]
>
> In the heat of the month of Jyaishtha, on a road of burning sand,
> he lay down under a tree.
>
> Near Bardhamana there is a small village,
> its name is Amaipura.
>
> There was Subuddhi Mishra, the Gosani's former disciple;
> in his house he rested.
>
> His son Guiya he named Jayananda;
> Rodani cooked, holding him [in her arms].[^17]
>
> Having eaten [Rodani's food], he set out for the town of Nadia
> and arrived at Bayara.[^18]
>
> Wondrous is the Vijaya-khanda, a pure well of nectar;
> the people of the world drink it through their ears.
>
> The lotus feet of Chaitanya — honey full of nectar:
> in that hope Jayananda lives." (70.2.15; 71.1.1–3)[^19]
>
> *[p. 199]*
>
> 5. "Receiving the prasada garland of Sri Virabhadra Gosani,
> receiving the blessing of Sri Abhirama Gosani alone,
>
> taking on my head the command of Gadadhara Pandita Gosani,
> I spread in some measure the song Sri Chaitanya-mangala." (2.2.2)[^20]
>
> 6. "By the grace of the water that washed the feet of Abhirama Gosani,
> by the command of Pandita Gosani, by Chaitanya's blessing,
>
> by the fruit of the austerities of his father, Subuddhi Mishra,
> Jayananda's mind turned to the Chaitanya-mangala." (43.2.1–2)

From the verses quoted above we obtain the following account of the poet.

The poet Jayananda was born in the famous brahmana family of the Bandyaghati line in which the celebrated smarta pandita of that time, Raghunandana, and others appeared. It was in the month of Vaishakha, on the bright twelfth lunar day. The poet was born in the house of his maternal grandfather. The poet's mother was named Rodani, his father Sri Subuddhi Mishra.[^v5]

The poet's elder paternal uncle was named Vaishnava Mishra, and his younger paternal uncle Ramananda Mishra. He had a relative named Vaninatha Mishra; whether he was a younger or an elder uncle of the poet could not be clearly made out. This Vaninatha's son was Mahananda Vidyabhushana, and his younger full brother Indriyananda Kavindra. From the account above it is broadly clear that in the brahmana family in which Jayananda was born there was no lack of learned and worthy panditas. Jayananda's forefathers were initiated into the mantra of Rama. Although his father was a disciple of Chaitanya and his mother a devotee of Nityananda, the poet's uncles, younger and elder, did not revere Chaitanya. Thus at that time there were differences of view even within a single family. In the Vaishnavachara-darpana, too, the name of Subuddhi Mishra, a resident of Bardhamana, appears among the branches of Chaitanya. In Krishnadasa's Chaitanya-charitamrita, in the description of the principal branches, the following mention is found: "Subuddhi Mishra, Hridayananda, Kamala-nayana."[^21]

Jayananda's mother's children did not survive. The poet was born after many prayers and vows by Subuddhi Mishra. At first the father and mother did not think that this child would live and bring glory to their family. So what usually happens in such cases happened: for the same reason for which Chaitanya had been named "Nimai," the poet was first named "Guiya."[^22] How did Guiya become Jayananda? The poet has told this himself.

*[p. 200]*

Chaitanya, now a sannyasi, is returning from Nilachala to Nadia. Passing through Bardhamana, he arrived at the village of Amaipura, at the dwelling of his disciple Subuddhi Mishra.[^v6] The poet's mother Rodani cooked and fed Chaitanya to his full satisfaction. During this stay Gauranga changed the poet's name "Guiya" and gave him the name "Jayananda." Jayananda was initiated into the mantra of Chaitanya. Who the guru was who gave him the mantra cannot be clearly known. But according to the signature line "by the grace of the water that washed the feet of Abhirama Gosani," Abhirama Gosvami seems to have been his mantra-guru. The poet composed the Chaitanya-mangala by the grace of Nityananda's son Virabhadra and at the command of Gadadhara Pandita.

In what Shaka year Jayananda was born, and in what Shaka year the Chaitanya-mangala was completed, nothing is written in the present manuscript. But with the help of the events described in the book and the Vaishnava literature of the time we shall be able to fix the date roughly.

It is known from the poet's own verses that Jayananda was born before Chaitanya, as a sannyasi, returned from Nilachala to Gauda. Krishnadasa Kaviraja writes in the Chaitanya-charitamrita:

> "Sri Krishna Chaitanya, descending at Navadvipa,
> was manifest in his play for forty-eight years.
>
> Fourteen hundred and seven Shaka is the record of his birth;
> in fourteen fifty-five he disappeared.
>
> Twenty-four years the Lord lived as a householder,
> delighting without cease in kirtana.
>
> At the end of twenty-four years, taking sannyasa,
> for another twenty-four years he lived in Nilachala.
>
> Of these, six years were goings and comings:
> now to the south, now to Gauda, now to Vrindavana.
>
> Eighteen years he remained in Nilachala
> and flooded everyone with the nectar of the play of love for Krishna."[^23]
> (Chaitanya-charitamrita, part 1, ch. 13)

According to these words of Krishnadasa Kaviraja, Mahaprabhu left home in 1431 Shaka, and after that for six years, that is, until 1437 Shaka, he travelled in various places — once to Nilachala, once to Gauda, once to Vrindavana, once to the south. That being so, he came to the village of Amaipura, the home of Jayananda's father, before 1437 Shaka, and Jayananda was born before that. When Mahaprabhu first saw Jayananda, he had not yet received the name Jayananda; his parents called him "Guiya." At that time *[p. 201]* the poet had probably not yet passed out of infancy; had he been older, his name would have become fixed, and without some special reason there would have been no need to change it. Names are generally given in infancy. Lord Chaitanya did not like the name "Guiya," so he gave the name "Jayananda." That being so, we may suppose that the poet was born between 1433 and 1435 Shaka.[^24] The poet saw the deeds of Chaitanya with his own eyes; he himself hints at this:

> "You are the eyes of all the people of Nadia;
> these words are true, and Jayananda is witness to it." (9.1.3)

What must now be considered is when the poet composed the Chaitanya-mangala.

At the very beginning of the book the poet mentions many Bengali Vaishnava authors and works that preceded him; I quote the list below:

> "Chaitanya has infinite forms, infinite avataras;
> infinite kings of poets sing his greatness.
>
> The great poet Valmiki made the Ramayana;
> Krittivasa, having felt it deeply, made the panchali.[^25]
>
> The revered Vyasa made the Sri Bhagavata;
> Gunaraja Khan made the Sri Krishna-vijaya.
>
> Jayadeva, Vidyapati and Chandidasa —
> they made known the deeds of Sri Krishna.
>
> Sarvabhauma Bhattacharya, an avatara of Vyasa,
> first proclaimed the Chaitanya-charitra.
>
> The Thousand Names of Chaitanya, in a composition of shlokas,
> Sarvabhauma composed in sheer bliss of love.
>
> Sri Paramananda Puri Gosani, the revered,
> set it out briefly in the Govinda-vijaya.
>
> Dividing it into Adi-khanda, Madhya-khanda, Shesha-khanda,
> Sri Vrindavana Dasa composed [his book] — above all.
>
> The poetry of Gauridasa Pandita is of the highest order:
> in his song-compositions every word resounds.
>
> Paramananda Gupta set it out briefly —
> the song Gauranga-vijaya, wondrous to hear.
>
> Gopala Basu made in song-compositions
> his Chaitanya-mangala in the chamara metre.[^26]
>
> Now, in words, [in] chamara, in song and the delight of music,
> Jayananda sings the Chaitanya-mangala last of all.
>
> And hundreds more poets will be born without end
> and will spread the Chaitanya-mangala.
>
> Meditating on the two feet of Chaitanya and Gadadhara,
> Jayananda has composed the Adi-khanda." (2.2.9–12; 3.1.1–3)[^27]

The few verses quoted above are of great value to the lover of old Bengali literature. They prove that before the poet Jayananda there appeared the poets Krittivasa, Gunaraja Khan, Vidyapati, Chandidasa, Sarvabhauma Bhattacharya, Paramananda Puri, Vrindavana Dasa, Gauridasa Pandita, Paramananda Gupta and Gopala Basu. Their fame and standing held the hearts of the devoted Vaishnavas. Krittivasa, Gunaraja Khan, Vidyapati, Chandidasa and Vrindavana Dasa, the author of the Chaitanya-bhagavata, *[p. 202]* need no introduction here: their beloved works have been printed and adorn the treasury of Bengali literature, and many already know about these poets. But how many have seen or heard of such old works as Sarvabhauma Bhattacharya's Chaitanya-charitra, Paramananda Puri's Govinda-vijaya, Gauridasa Pandita's Chaitanya-sangita, Paramananda Gupta's Gauranga-vijaya and Gopala Basu's Chaitanya-mangala? My dear friend Sri Dinesh Chandra Sen, churning the ocean of Bengali literature, has published a priceless book called Banga Bhasha o Sahitya;[^28] but among the hundreds of Vaishnava authors described in it we did not find even a mention of these few works. Many of those who have now become especially renowned for the study of old Vaishnava literature have not heard even the names of these books. So the search for these books is very difficult. If they were brought to light, many problems of Chaitanya's life might be solved. It is a matter of great joy that, just as we have now come forward to offer a handful of flowers at the lotus feet of Krittivasa, calling him the Valmiki of Bengal, so the poet Jayananda too, as though thinking of him in the same way, mentions his name first of all.

Sri Achyutacharana Chaudhuri writes that Vrindavana Dasa composed the Chaitanya-mangala in 1457 Shaka, and that in 1492 Shaka, in order to settle a dispute with Lochana Dasa, this Chaitanya-mangala of Vrindavana Dasa was somewhat altered and circulated under the name Chaitanya-bhagavata. That Jayananda's Chaitanya-mangala was composed after 1457 Shaka, that is, after Vrindavana's Chaitanya-mangala had been composed, there is not the slightest doubt. But the question now is whether by that time Vrindavana Dasa's book had received the title Chaitanya-bhagavata. Probably it was not yet called the Chaitanya-bhagavata. Otherwise the poet Jayananda would have made particular mention of Vrindavana's title "Bhagavata" or of the affair of the "Bhagavata": the Vaishnava society of the time had by no means forgotten the dispute between Vrindavana Dasa and Lochana Dasa over the titles of their books. On the other hand we find (according to Achyuta Babu) that Lochana Dasa composed the Chaitanya-mangala in 1459 Shaka at the command of Narahari Sarakara. Yet the poet Jayananda does not mention even Lochana Dasa's name. In such a case many may suspect that Jayananda composed his book before Lochana Dasa. This suspicion will be dispelled by Jayananda's own words. At the close of his book he describes how Advaita Prabhu left his body. Advaita Acharya disappeared in 1480 Shaka. So it must certainly be admitted that Jayananda put forth the Chaitanya-mangala after 1480 Shaka. But now the question arises: if Lochana Dasa's Chaitanya-mangala was composed long before Advaita Prabhu's disappearance, why does Jayananda not mention it? There may be two reasons for this. First, Jayananda was older than Lochana Dasa. Moreover, *[p. 203]* Lochana's book is (according to Achyuta Babu) the composition of a boy of fourteen; since, apart from the young poet's poetic skill and superhuman descriptions, it does not describe many things worth knowing about Chaitanya's life, Jayananda did not think it necessary to give his name or an account of his book. Secondly, the poet Jayananda was a zealous adherent of Nityananda's son Virabhadra; this is evident from the poet's own writing. Thakura Narahari did not regard Nityananda with the same eyes with which he regarded Chaitanya. For this reason Nityananda's disciple Vrindavana does not mention even the name of Thakura Narahari in his book. Yet Narahari was one of Chaitanya's principal companions; it was he who fanned Mahaprabhu with the chamara; with him in view Vrindavana Dasa wrote:

> "Some fortunate ones are granted [only] to see;
> some fortunate one waves the chamara."

For the same reason for which Vrindavana Dasa did not name Thakura Narahari, Jayananda, the son of a woman disciple of Nityananda,[^30] passed over the name of Lochana Dasa, Narahari's beloved disciple. Yet he too, after the manner of Vrindavana Dasa,[^31] in the words

> "And hundreds more poets will be born without end;
> they will spread the Chaitanya-mangala" (3.1.2) —

in this mention of the "Chaitanya-mangala," probably alludes to Lochana Dasa.[^v7]

However that may be, we can now fix roughly the time of composition of the Chaitanya-mangala under discussion. The poet Jayananda put forth the Chaitanya-mangala after Advaita Acharya's disappearance, that is, after 1480 Shaka, and before 1492 Shaka.[^32] The devotee poet himself, chamara in hand, used to go from land to land singing the Chaitanya-mangala.

To divide Chaitanya's life and the song into particular palas,[^33] the poet arranged his book in nine khandas. He describes these nine khandas thus:

> "First, the Adi-khanda: the dharma and the deeds of the yugas;
> second, the Nadia-khanda: the birth of Gauranga.
>
> Third, the Vairagya-khanda: leaving the household life;
> fourth, the Sannyasa-khanda: the Lord's sannyasa.
>
> Fifth, the Utkala-khanda: he went to Nilachala;
> sixth, the Prakasha-khanda: the radiant manifestation.[^34]
>
> Seventh, the Tirtha-khanda: visiting many tirthas;
> eighth, the Vijaya-khanda: he went to the city of Vaikuntha.
>
> Ninth, the Uttara-khanda: the song of him with all his companions,[^35]
> of all the yuga-avataras that Gauranga revealed.
>
> This song in nine khandas is the Chaitanya-mangala:
> whoever hears it, all his sins go down to Rasatala." (3.1.3–6)

## The State of the Country

In this Chaitanya-mangala in nine khandas the poet Jayananda has described, in most graceful language and without concealment, the reason why the father of Chaitanya Mahaprabhu came to Navadvipa and the condition of Navadvipa at that time. I give his words below:

> "In the land of Srihatta lawlessness and famine arose;
> dacoity, theft, drought and pestilence set in.
>
> The land fell into ruin; seeing the calamity,
> all the people fled away to various lands.
>
> Nilambara Chakravarti and Mishra Purandara
> with all their kin quickly left Jayapura.
>
> 'In what land shall we live?' all of them reasoned.
> 'In this land we shall find no protection; let us go elsewhere.
>
> A dwelling fit for us all is on the bank of the Ganga,
> just as Nanda left Gokula because of its calamities.
>
> Long ago a king of ascetics told me:
> "Leave this land and go into the very midst of Nadia."
>
> A lawless land is not fit to live in;
> in Srihatta not half a sesame seed's worth of good people remains.
>
> One should live among people whose love and mind are for Krishna,
> where there are ascetics purified by the tirthas, full of knowledge, who have conquered the senses,
>
> where they have faith in the Ganga, the Vaishnavas and mahaprasada —
> in people of little fortune these things are not manifest.
>
> We shall bathe in the Ganga and live in Navadvipa:
> that is living in Vaikuntha — what need then of japa and austerity?'
>
> Riding in fine palanquins, Mishra came with his kin,
> and he saw the Ganga and Navadvipa and floated in the bliss of prema.
>
> Navadvipa is heaven on earth in all the circle of the world;
> people of many classes live on the bank of the Jahnavi.
>
> Meditating on the lotus feet of Chaitanya-chandra,
> Jayananda sings the Lord's mangala in bliss.
>
> *Raga Suhi.*
>
> A city wondrous with many paintings and colours,
> where people of many castes dwell;
> temples and shrines plastered with lime,
> trees and creepers of every hue.
>
> Glory, glory to the blessed city of Nadia
> on the bank of the Alakananda!
> Kamala, the lovely, plays there,
> adorned with bakula garlands.
>
> On every house a wondrous finial pot,
> fluttering banners fly;
> as once was the city of Ayodhya —
> they flash like strokes of lightning.
>
> Dance-halls, schools, long ponds, lakes,
> wells, tanks with flights of steps;
> monasteries, pavilions, well-built courtyards,
> gardens of kunda jasmine and tulasi.
>
> At every door there shine
> wondrous painted panels;
> in every lane joyous dancing and song,
> in every house the recitation of the Veda.
>
> Taking the form of the twice-born, gods and gandharvas
> were born in Navadvipa;
> becoming brahmanas' wives, Indra's vidyadharis
> sing beside the Ganga.
>
> Leaving heaven, all the hosts of gandharvas
> were born as vaidyas and their wives;
> gods, rishis and munis, taking the form of brahmanas,
> study the shruti and the Gita.
>
> *[p. 205]*
>
> At the hour of cow-dust,[^36] in every house,
> the sound of mridanga, kartal and conch;
> white yak-tail whisks, peacock fans,
> and canopies gleaming above them.
>
> Courtyards walled with brick,
> well-made doors of houses;
> vermilion, orpiment, glass, shields,
> four-walled pavilions, halls with carved door-frames.
>
> In the halls handsome, massive pillars
> adorned with marks of moon and sun;
> peacocks, parrots, cranes, doves,
> lions, swans, chakravakas.
>
> Bedsteads, benches, thrones, seats,
> pavilions, peacock fans;
> wondrous yak-tail whisks, canopies,
> in every house fine conch-shell bangles.
>
> Basins, betel boxes, areca caskets,
> mirrors, little cups for unguents;
> copper pots, brass pitchers for juices,
> tripods from Varanasi.
>
> Boxes inlaid with conch-shell, bowls, platters,
> little dishes full of sweets;
> spouted water-pots from Tirhut, copper […],
> cool brass ewers.[^37]
>
> Stone vessels of fine workmanship,
> cloth dyed with chalk colour;[^38]
> Oriya and Gaudiya [locks] and combs,
> wondrous little caskets.[^39]
>
> Tar bracelets, strings of cowries, golden amulets,
> armlets, wrist-bangles, jewelled anklets;
> gold ornaments shaped like ketaki leaves, coral, pearls,
> "khuras" from the land of Kashmir.
>
> Gold leaf, betel boxes,
> wondrous beni braids from the land of Kanchi… [^40]
> silks, Tibetan cloth, saklat broadcloth, blankets,
> shriram-khani and jamakha fabrics;
> sapphires from Tibet,
> lakshmi-vilasa and taraka cloths.
>
> I cannot write how many servants and maids
> toil in the temple of love;[^41]
> whatever is rare in all the worlds
> is sold in the market of Nadia.
>
> Meditating on the honey of the lotus feet
> of Chaitanya and Gadadhara, the lord of his life,
> the brahmana Jayananda sings the Chaitanya-mangala,
> the deep secret of the nigamas.
>
> *Raga Kanada.*
>
> To Shachi eight daughters were born in due time;
> by the bond of fate some time went by.
>
> Jagannatha Mishra became Mishra Purandara —
> a good poet, a pandita, a great logician, handsome.
>
> Seeing his severe austerity, all people marvelled:
> bathing, sandhya, the daily shraddha — the observances of a brahmana;
>
> offerings, homa, japa, sacrifice, puja with incense and lamps,
> reading the Sri Bhagavata before Govinda.
>
> Another son was born, named Vishvarupa;
> a great famine arose in the village of Navadvipa.
>
> Seeing the calamity — incessant dacoity and theft —
> all the people fled away to various lands.
>
> Then Jagannatha Mishra joyfully
> performed Vishvarupa's ten rites one after another.
>
> Suddenly in Navadvipa there came fear of the king:
> seizing brahmanas, the king takes their caste and their life.
>
> *[p. 206]*
>
> In whatever house in Navadvipa he hears the sound of a conch,
> he takes its owner's wealth and life and destroys his caste.
>
> If he sees tilaka on a forehead, a sacred thread on a shoulder,
> he loots house and home and binds the man in fetters.
>
> He breaks temples and shrines, uproots the tulasi;
> in fear for their lives the people of Navadvipa have no peace.
>
> He forbade bathing in the Ganga at all the markets and ghats;
> ashvattha and jackfruit trees are cut down by hundreds.
>
> All the yavanas who lived in the village of Piralya
> brought ruin on the brahmanas of Navadvipa.
>
> Strife between brahmanas and yavanas has gone on from age to age;
> the dreadful village of Piralya lies near Navadvipa.
>
> Before the lord of Gauda they made a false accusation:
> 'The brahmanas of Navadvipa will bring you calamity.
>
> There is a prophecy that a brahmana will become king in Gauda;
> do not be careless, lest calamity follow.
>
> A brahmana of Navadvipa will surely become king;
> it is written by the gandharvas that his subjects will bear bows.'[^42]
>
> This lie lodged in the king's mind,
> and the king gave the order: 'Lay Nadia waste!'
>
> Visharada's son, Sarvabhauma Bhattacharya,
> went with his whole family to Utkala, leaving the kingdom of Gauda.
>
> In Utkala the warlike king Prataparudra
> honoured Sarvabhauma on a jewelled throne.
>
> His brother Vidya-vachaspati stayed on in Gauda;
> Visharada took up residence in Varanasi.
>
> Vidya-virinchi and Vidyaranya in Navadvipa,
> Bhattacharya-shiromani in the sight of all.
>
> The lord of Gauda heard that Nadia had been laid waste,
> and at night he saw a most terrible dream.
>
> Kali, bearing sword and skull-cup, naked,
> a garland of heads on her neck, crying 'Cut! Cut!' —
>
> seizing the king by the hair, she drove a spear into his chest
> and poured boiling oil into his ears and nostrils:
>
> 'Today I will throw your capital of Gauda into the Ganga;
> I will cut down your whole line, your elephants, horses and troops!'
>
> The lord of Gauda says: 'Mother, spare my body!
> I will resettle Navadvipa, if you save my life!'
>
> The king rubbed his nose on the ground,[^43] and then Kali let him go;
> the lord of Gauda fainted and fell to the earth.
>
> In the morning he told the dream to his confidant;
> hearing of the wondrous dream, all people were seized with dread.
>
> 'The command of the lord of Gauda: let Navadvipa dwell in happiness;
> there is no royal tax — let all people plough their fields.
>
> Whoever from today obstructs the markets and ghats
> shall pay a fine to the treasury and be impaled on the stake.
>
> Whoever breaks temples and shrines, whoever cuts an ashvattha —
> impale him on a stake in the market of Nadia.
>
> Let all the vaidyas and brahmanas who live in Navadvipa
> hold all manner of festivals to their hearts' content.
>
> Let dance, song and music sound in every house;
> let banners fly on the finials above the temples.
>
> Let there be flower markets, a profusion of perfumes;
> let conch, bell and instruments sound with cries of victory.
>
> As Navadvipa was a capital in former days,
> let it be heard of as a hundred times greater.
>
> If you see a yavana within the bounds of Navadvipa,
> kill him at your own will — or spare him.[^44]
>
> Worship the gods in happiness, perform sacrifice, homa and charity;
> the markets and ghats are not forbidden — let them bathe in the Ganga.
>
> What authority have I over the subjects of Navadvipa?
> Truly, truly I say: they are the essence of the world.'
>
> By the king's command Navadvipa was created anew;
> in autumn, at the end of the night, there was a rain of flowers.
>
> *[p. 207]*
>
> The great men who had left the village
> all came back to Navadvipa, their wishes fulfilled.
>
> Meditating on the two feet of Chaitanya and Gadadhara,
> Jayananda composes the Nadia-khanda in bliss."

From these few quoted verses we can learn only this: at one time a great epidemic broke out in Srihatta. The inhabitants were leaving the country and fleeing. At the time of that great pestilence Nilambara Chakravarti and Purandara Mishra fled with their wives to Navadvipa. The former prosperity of that Navadvipa which had once been famous as the beloved capital of Lakshmanasena, lord of Gauda, had not yet wholly vanished at the time of Mishra's arrival: countless temples and rows of mansions, the dwellings of various castes, still enhanced the beauty of Navadvipa.

Before Chaitanya's birth the oppression of the yavanas in Navadvipa had grown terrible. Many of the people of the village of Piraliya near Navadvipa had become yavanas.[^45] The Piraliya villagers[^v8] bore a particular grudge against Navadvipa: they informed the Muslim king that a brahmana would become king in Navadvipa. Hearing that, could the yavana king remain calm? He ordered the brahmanas of Navadvipa to be seized and made yavanas. At the command of the lord of Gauda the Piraliya villagers came and began to make yavanas of whomever they could. At the time of this calamity many brahmana panditas fled from Navadvipa. The famous Vasudeva Sarvabhauma was one of them. In this evil time the birth rite and the other rites of Vishvarupa were performed. At the piteous cries of the brahmanas Mahamaya took pity. The devotee poet writes that Mahamaya, in the form of the naked and terrible Kali bearing sword and skull-cup, appeared before the eyes of the sleeping yavana king. In the dream the yavana king was greatly frightened. His mind changed: he committed no further oppression on Navadvipa and granted the people of Navadvipa security. Here one thing must be said. The poet Jayananda was a supreme Vaishnava, and his uncles and forefathers were worshippers of Rama. Why then did he not describe the pride of the mleccha king being crushed by Vishnu or by Hanuman? It seems to us that some actual event lies concealed in the poet's description. Before the rise of Chaitanya the Shaktas were especially predominant everywhere in Bengal. Some rite of the Shaktas had, by the turn of fate, changed the yavana king's mind; that is probably why the lord of the mlecchas granted security to the harassed people of Navadvipa. Indeed, as the sea grows still before a great storm, so Navadvipa had grown still before the rise of Mahaprabhu.

At that time, among the householder's utensils and articles of luxury, there were in use: basins, boxes, betel cases, mirrors, little cups for unguents, copper pots, brass pitchers, Varanasi tripods, boxes and bowls, dishes of sweets, little sweet-dishes, spouted water-pots from Tirhut, brass ewers, stone vessels, cloth dyed with chalk colour, Oriya *[p. 208]* and Gaudiya locks, wondrous combs, little caskets, tar bracelets, strings of cowries, gold amulets, Kashmiri "khuras" (?),[^46] beni from Kanchi, silk cloth, Tibetan blankets, shriram-khani and jamakha, sapphires from Tibet, lakshmi-vilasa and so on. Besides these, Jayananda also mentions such luxuries as krishna-keli cloth, vishnu-taila oil and lakshmi-vilasa bedsteads.

Jayananda gives the following list of the foods of that time:

> "Among the relishes, laphra, mung soup, chhana-bara,
> fried ol, jhol, bara, grated coconut, bari;
>
> bara-amla[?], kanji-bara, dahi-bara, kshira, chhana,
> amrita-gutika, milk, kora, sugar, pana;
>
> madhu-manda, ghrita-manda, chini-manda, pitha […],
> arsha, dalima and pu[…] kakra;
>
> sosbara, kandabana, satpuli, sharkara […];
>
> deuli-sakar, honey, shak-barya, puli,
> maricha, jhanjhari, madhusrava, jhilimili;
>
> duhkhahara, manohara, khaichur, nabat,
> krishna-keli, hamsa-keli, chhaoya, parijata;
>
> harivallabha, nayana-sukha and dudhari,
> chandrakanti, gangajal, anna-pana, shikhari;
>
> eranda, padma-phal, kshiri, kshirsa, kshirara,
> soari, soma-danda, hati-shunda, dohara," etc.[^49]

## Particular Information

Although the poet Jayananda's Chaitanya-mangala is much smaller than the Chaitanya-bhagavata or the Chaitanya-charitamrita, from this song-book called a "mangala" one can learn a number of very important particular details of which there is not the slightest mention in the Chaitanya-bhagavata and the rest.

1. The Bengali biographers of Chaitanya mention only that Chaitanya's forefathers were inhabitants of Srihatta; but in what place in the land of Srihatta they lived, and where they had been before they settled in Srihatta, no one has written anything. The poet Jayananda, however, writes clearly:

> "In the land of Srihatta there is a village, Jayapura,
> a place full of every delight, without equal on earth.
>
> To the east the Sarasvati, to the north the Gomati,
> to the west the Dhol-samudra, to the south the Karati.
>
> In Jayapura there are hundreds of brahmana houses —
> people of godlike form, of great learning, possessors of great wealth.
>
> Born of the great house of Ravi,[^50] of a great lineage,
> conquerors of the quarters, expounding their darshanas like four-faced Brahma.
>
> *[p. 209]*
>
> In such a lineage Jagannatha Mishra was born;
> Nilambara Chakravarti gave him Shachi in marriage.
>
> Vasudeva Chakravarti was born in Srihatta;
> nearby [were born] Shachi and Jagannatha."[^51] (5.2.5–6)

Now we have learned that Chaitanya's forefathers lived at a place called Jayapura in the province of Srihatta. This Jayapura has the Sarasvati on its eastern boundary, the Gomati on its northern, the Dhol-samudra on the west and the Karati on the south.

On the other hand, Pradyumna Mishra, a native of Srihatta, writes in the Sanskrit work Sri Krishna-chaitanyodayavali that Chaitanya's forefather Madhukara Mishra came to Srihatta and settled at a place called Baraganga. His middle son Upendra Mishra lived near Mount Kailasa, in Gupta-Vrindavana, on the western bank of the river Ikshu, near a hidden pool called Amrita. His son Jagannatha Mishra, having finished grammar and the like in his homeland, came to Navadvipa.[^v9] Here Jagannatha Mishra married Shachi, the daughter of Nilambara Chakravarti, and became a householder.[^v10]

As I have said before, the poet Jayananda writes that it was because of a terrible pestilence in Srihatta that Jagannatha Mishra left Srihatta with his wife Shachi Devi and Nilambara Chakravarti and came *[p. 210]* to live in Navadvipa. In that case we must admit that Jagannatha's marriage with Shachi took place while they were living in Srihatta. But Pradyumna Mishra of Srihatta writes that the marriage of Jagannatha with Shachi was performed in Navadvipa. Now two different views confront us; which of them is true ought to be decided. But we are not competent to decide. It would be well if some experienced person versed in Vaishnava literature were to decide it.

Our dear friend Sri Achyutacharana Chaudhuri of Srihatta has proclaimed Pradyumna Mishra to be Chaitanya's paternal cousin and his contemporary. On the other hand, the poet Jayananda too is Chaitanya's contemporary. In such a case whose word is to be believed? Jayananda met most of the devotees then living in Navadvipa. In particular, from the way he describes Navadvipa it readily appears that he had seen Navadvipa with his own eyes. All Vaishnavas who came to Navadvipa went to pay their respects at the feet of Shachi Thakurani. In such a case, if Jayananda saw Shachi Thakurani and learned the family's past from her, then Jayananda's word must be given the greater credit. Especially since, had Shachi Devi's marriage taken place in Navadvipa, the Vaishnava authors of Navadvipa would have mentioned it with delight. It is also evident that the poet Jayananda recorded much that he had heard from the lives of Chaitanya composed by Chaitanya's companions Sarvabhauma Bhattacharya, Paramananda Puri, Paramananda Gupta and others, and from the lips of the principal devotees of Chaitanya of that time. By all this evidence the poet Jayananda's account appears the more trustworthy. The dwelling of Chaitanya's forefathers in Srihatta was at first at the place called Jayapura; afterwards, when Chaitanya's kinsmen in Srihatta left that place and settled in Gupta-Vrindavana, that is, in the present village of Dhaka-Dakshin,[^v11] that place too must have come to be regarded as a tirtha by the Vaishnava devotees of Chaitanya.

*[p. 211]*

Where, then, were Chaitanya's forefathers before they settled in Srihatta? On this Jayananda writes:

> "The forefathers of Chaitanya Gosani lived in Jajpur;
> they fled to the land of Srihatta for fear of King Bhramara.
>
> In that line there was a supreme Vaishnava named Kamala-lochana;
> by [the merit of] austerities in a former birth Chaitanya Gosani rested in his house." (48.2.1)

Now it is known that Chaitanya's forefathers lived in Jajpur in Utkala. For fear of King Bhramara they fled from there to the land of Srihatta.

*[p. 212]*

In the history of Utkala there is no clear mention of a king named "Bhramara." In such a case someone may perhaps ask: where did Jayananda get this name? The name is not Jayananda's invention. An inscription of Kapilendradeva, lord of Utkala, has been found at Gopinathapur in the Cuttack district. In it Maharaja Kapilendradeva's title "Bhramara" is seen.[^v12] At a time when the mighty power of the Muslim kings was ready to swallow all of India, this excellent Bhramara, King Kapilendra, humbled the pride of the lords of Malava, Gauda and Delhi and was able to preserve independence and Hindu power.

2. Vrindavana Dasa, the author of the Chaitanya-bhagavata, writes that Haridasa Thakura was born in the village of Burana:

*[p. 213]*

> "In the village of Burana Haridasa descended;
> by that good fortune kirtana was revealed in all those lands." (Chaitanya-bhagavata, Adi-khanda)

Sri Achyutacharana Chaudhuri too writes in his Life of Haridasa Thakura that Haridasa was born in Burana, that his father's name was Sumati Sharma and his mother's Gauri Devi. But in the Chaitanya-mangala under discussion the poet Jayananda gives this account:

> "One day Gaurachandra was dancing in Navadvipa;
> Haridasa Thakura came and joined him in person.
>
> A man of great renunciation, his body pure gold;
> his mother's name was Ujjvala, his father's Manohara.
>
> On the bank of the Svarnadi, the village of Kalagachhi of the Bhatas;
> born in a low family, [he bore] the former name […]."[^52] (14.2.11–13)

According to these verses, Haridasa Thakura's mother was named Ujjvala and his father Manohara; he was born in a low family (of the Bhata caste?) in the village of Kalagachhi on the bank of the Ganga.

The question now is this: Vrindavana Dasa composed his book long before Jayananda, and Jayananda himself acknowledges it. Why then did Jayananda, having seen and heard Vrindavana's book, express so different a view about Haridasa Thakura? Jayananda gives the names of Haridasa's mother and father, while Vrindavana mentions nothing of them. From this it appears that Jayananda was able to gather more information about Haridasa Thakura than Vrindavana. It may be inferred that he corrected Vrindavana Dasa's mistake. The poet Jayananda also knew that Haridasa had a special connection with Burana. He wrote of that too:

> "From Burana came Haridasa." (73.2.9)

Many of Haridasa's pastimes took place in Burana; probably that is why Vrindavana took Burana to be Haridasa's birthplace. In fact his birthplace is the village of Kalagachhi.

3. Many say that Mahaprabhu's companion at his sannyasa was Govinda Ghosha, not Govinda the smith. But the poet Jayananda clearly names Govinda the smith among Mahaprabhu's companions:

> "At this time Nityananda came to Navadvipa
> and revealed to Gauranga all the secrets of sannyasa.
>
> Hearing this, Gaurachandra was filled with joy;
> crossing the Ganga, Nityananda went on ahead.
>
> 'Mukunda Datta the vaidya, Govinda the smith,
> come with me from Nadia across the Ganga.
>
> Acharyaratna Chandrashekhara, Acharya Hari,
> Vasudeva Datta, Shuklambara Brahmachari,
>
> *[p. 214]*
>
> Vakreshvara Pandita, Bhagai, Gangadasa —
> in your presence I shall take sannyasa.'" (42.2.4–5)[^53]

The life of Chaitanya current under the name of Govinda Dasa's Karcha was composed by this Govinda the smith.[^54]

4. Some biographers write that when Chaitanya, after his sannyasa, first set out for Nilachala, a war was going on between the lord of Gauda and the king of Utkala, and that for this reason Ramachandra Khan at first dissuaded him from going to Nilachala. But in Jayananda's book nothing at all is written about this. On the contrary, Jayananda writes that while Mahaprabhu was residing in Nilachala, Prataparudra, the lord of Utkala, once wished to conquer Bengal, but Chaitanya, describing to him the great might of the lord of Gauda, restrained him. Thinking that this passage may some day be of slight use to historians, I quote Jayananda's words:

> "Thus (Chaitanya) stayed for two or four years;
> a great commotion arose between Gauda and Utkala.
>
> Prataparudra hopes to conquer Gauda;
> hearing this, the lord of Gauda mocks him.
>
> The king asked Chaitanya-deva for leave;
> the Lord says: 'An evil design has seized Prataparudra.
>
> The king is a Kalayavana,[^62] lord of the five Gaudas;
> see how great is the difference between a lion and a tiger.[^63]
>
> The yavanas will lay waste the land of Odra;
> Jagannatha, after all these days, will leave Nilachala.
>
> You will be put to shame, Prataparudra; heed my word,
> lest afterwards you have to sleep and eat facing Gauda.[^64]
>
> Conquer the land of Kanchi and various kingdoms;
> but that you should conquer Gauda — I do not see such a thing.
>
> The lord of Gauda will surely come to Nilachala;
> you will flee, and destruction will come upon Utkala.'
>
> Hearing that the Lord had forbidden it, Prataparudra
> went to Vijayanagara to make war." (Vijaya-khanda, 70.2)

5. Just as now, under English rule, many educated Hindus do not fail to imitate the English, so in the period of Muslim supremacy imitation of the Muslims was current. With the society of that time in view, Jayananda put these words into Chaitanya's mouth:

> "Brahmanas will keep house with shudra women;
> one who knows all the shastras will sell his daughter.
>
> Brahmanas will grow beards and read Persian;
> with socks on their feet, a stick in their hand, they will hold bows.
>
> The best of the twice-born will seek mansabs,[^65]
> will ply dacoity, theft and toll-extortion without cease.
>
> A shudra will become the teacher of the world, a mleccha will become king;
> the king will take everything, the subjects will be in misery." (7?.1)[^66]

6. How Mahaprabhu disappeared — that heart-rending story no devoted Vaishnava writer has clearly told. I have asked many devoted Vaishnavas about it; they say that that terrible matter must not be told: when one remembers it, *[p. 215]* the heart breaks. But that particular matter which we need to know, and which no earlier Vaishnava writer wrote down clearly, the devotee poet Jayananda, binding his heart with stone, has revealed thus:

> "In Nilachala, at night, Chaitanya in the Tota ashrama
> announced in due order that he would go to Vaikuntha:
>
> 'Having agreed on the bright seventh lunar day of Ashadha,
> send a chariot — I will go to the city of Vaikuntha.'
>
> In Ashadha, as he was dancing at the chariot procession,[^67]
> suddenly a brick struck his left foot.
>
> Advaita set out at dawn for the land of Gauda;
> in private [the Lord] told him something particular.
>
> In the waters of Narendra, with all his companions,
> Chaitanya sported in the water in many ways.
>
> On the sixth day the pain in his foot was great;
> on that account he at last lay down in the Tota.
>
> To Pandita Gosani he told everything:
> 'Tomorrow, at the tenth danda of the night, I shall surely go.'[^68]
>
> From somewhere came divine garlands of many colours;
> how many vidyadharas dance on the royal road!
>
> 'Bring the chariot, bring the chariot!' the gods call out;
> the Lord mounted the chariot with the Garuda banner.
>
> The illusory body remained lying there;
> Chaitanya went to Vaikuntha, leaving Jambudvipa.
>
> Many servants died of snakebite;
> there were meteors, thunderbolts, earthquakes." (75.2.14–15; 76.1.1–3)

Now we have learned that at the time of the Ratha-yatra Gauranga's tender foot was injured by the blow of a brick. But at the time there was no great pain. With all his companions he sported in the water of the Narendra lake for the last time. On the sixth day the pain in his foot grew worse. Lying in the Tota ashrama, he said to Pandita Gosai: "Tomorrow, at the tenth danda of the night, I shall leave this illusory body." Thus on the bright seventh lunar day of Ashadha Chaitanya brought his lila to a close.

## Language

The poet Jayananda wrote his book in the language that was current in Navadvipa and the surrounding region four hundred years ago. At that time the Bengali language was not yet bound by strict rules of grammar; a considerable difference is noticeable between the language of that time and present-day Bengali. In Jayananda's book various forms are found: a verb in the third person with a subject in the first person, and conversely a subject in the third person with a verb in the first person; the ending "-te" in the instrumental; "-e" or "-ti" in place of the locative ending "-te"; the use of "-si" in the second person; and so on.[^v13] As examples I quote some instances below:[^69]

*[p. 216]*

1. *karila* = *karilam* ("did" for "I did"):

> "Indradyumna weeps, given over to grief:
> 'What have I done, going to Brahmapuri!'" (56.2.6)

2. *jay* = *jao* ("you go"); *khay* = *khao* ("you eat"):

> "'You have become a brahmachari, you eat havishya;[^70]
> we all study, and you do not go to study.'" (13.2.13)

3. *-e* (locative) = *-te*:

> "At such a time calamity befell Dvaraka." (59.1.5)

4. *-ta* (locative) = *-te*:

> "Seventh, in the Tirtha-khanda, visiting many tirthas." (3.1.5)

5. *-si* (in the second person):

> "Smiling slightly, the Lord asks him:
> 'Having deceived me, my boy, you run away to the temple?'"

6. *bolaha* = *bala* ("you are called"):

> "'You are called Krishna-Chaitanya, jewel of sannyasis.'" (66.1.15)

7. *balu* = *bala* or *balaha* ("say"):

> "'Having made a vow, say it in my presence.'" (52.1.9)

8. *chhariba* = *chharibe* ("will leave"):

> "'Lakshmi will never leave [you], by the power of your austerities.'" (57.2.13)

9. *sajaba* = *sajaibe* ("will prepare"):

> "'The brahmanas will constantly prepare offerings of food.'" (58.2.14)

10. *pelaiya* = *phelaiya* ("throwing away"):

> "Throwing far away the charming book from his hand." (14.1.7)

11. *ibe* = *ebe*, *ekhan* ("now"):

> "'Now I feel as though I have become wretched.'" (42.1.8)

12. *tamu* = *tabu*, *tathapi* ("yet"):

> "'Yet I shall never leave this place.'" (61.2.8)

13. *tathir* = *tahar* ("its, of that"):

> "Because of it the Yadu line perishes." (59.1.12)

14. *nai* = *nadi* ("river"):

> "Seeing Cuttack, I sing on the bank of the Mahanadi." (52.2.6)

15. *Oranta* = *Urisya* ("Orissa"):

> *[p. 217]*
>
> "People proclaim: [here is] Indradyumna, king of Orissa." (57.2.15)

16. *delu* = *deul*, *devalaya* ("temple"):

> "In Jagannatha's temple, remaining in the midst of the throne." (53.1.5)

Besides these, many uncommon words are also used, whose meaning is not easily grasped. Some are quoted by way of evidence:

1. *dhamali*:

> "With Nityananda, the merry dhamali of rapture."[^71] (76.2.25)

2. *magara*:

> "The holy land of twenty yojanas, the city of Mathura;
> formerly it was […], now it is magara."[^72] (68.2.8)

3. *nachh*:

> "Birth after birth [may I be] the dog of his yard."[^73] (2.1.3)

4. *ubhar*:

> "A rain of flowers in Nilachala, an abundance of fragrance." (49.2.4)

5. *chidipo*:[^v14]

> "A chidipo quarrelled with me." (52.1.13)

6. *pheta*, "to close":

> "Again, closing the doors, he entered the temple."

7. *sanveta*,[^v14] "meeting, audience":

> "At Jagannatha's command Sarvabhauma closed the doors:
> 'Arrange [for me] a meeting with Chaitanya Gosani.'" (52.1.15)

8. *elaka* = *eraka* (a reed):

> "All the eraka reeds that grew from the particles of the pestle —
> with those reeds the Yadu line perished in battle."[^74] (59.2.13)

9. *udgraha*:

> "To perform the udgraha Chaitanya went […]."[^75] (62.2.19)

10. *thasithusi* = *thukthak* (a knocking):

> "Hearing the knocking sound 'thasi-thusi' inside the temple." (59.2.13) Etc.

## Poetic Gift

Jayananda is not a first-rank poet of love like Chandidasa or Vidyapati; and although he is a devotee, in expounding the spiritual doctrine of bhakti he cannot equal even Krishnadasa Kaviraja; *[p. 218]* in depicting human character or in charming with the beauty of nature he is not able to take the seat of Kavikankana Mukundarama or Ghanarama, nor is he the equal of Kashirama Dasa in vigour of language. But nevertheless, to the devotee, to those who are charmed by a simple portrait of devotion, the poet Jayananda is no ordinary person. Like Kaviraja Gosvami, he too is a humble poet. In many places he too, in such simple, heartfelt words as

> "May the dust of the Vaishnavas' feet touch my body;
> with my whole family I sell myself at the Vaishnavas' feet" —

shows the height of humility. The poet Jayananda was not deprived of humility, the priceless treasure of the Vaishnava poets. Through this great virtue Sri Chaitanya is reflected by his brush as the very image of humility. In describing the sentiment of pathos[^76] the poet has achieved considerable success; indeed, as one reads his simple verses steeped in pathos, the heart often melts with emotion. The conversation which Mahaprabhu had with Vishnupriya on the night before the day on which he was to take sannyasa is truly heart-touching. From the baromasya quoted above one can see how simple and pure in feeling the poet's composition is. Besides this, the poet's description of Navadvipa which I quoted earlier also gives evidence of his poetic gift. But there is something more to be said about the baromasya quoted above. We find this baromasya of Vishnupriya also in the Padakalpataru, in pada no. 1783. Most surprisingly, apart from the description of the month of Magha, it agrees in all other parts with the baromasya we have quoted. What does not agree is this:

*[p. 220]*

> "In Magha the cold is doubled — how much shall I bear?
> Without seeing you I shall not be able to keep my life.
>
> Here is the cruel dart that now remains [in my heart]:
> no offspring of yours remains on earth.
>
> O Lord Gauranga, take me to your side;
> this Lochana Dasa drowns in the ocean of separation."

Seeing Lochana Dasa's signature in the pada quoted in the Padakalpataru, many Vaishnavas take the baromasya of Vishnupriya under discussion to be Lochana Dasa's composition. But on the evidence we now have, it appears that the baromasya of Vishnupriya praised in Vaishnava society is not Lochana's composition. In Lochana Dasa's books called the Chaitanya-mangala and the Chaitanya-prema-vilasa there are many of his padas, but nowhere among them, speaking of Chaitanya and Vishnupriya, does he give the slightest hint of this baromasya. Had it been his composition, we should have found it in its proper place in his Chaitanya-mangala or Chaitanya-prema-vilasa; but since it is not in its proper place, it appears to be someone else's composition. A respected friend of mine has written that in a hundred-and-fifty-year-old manuscript of the Padakalpataru he did not find this baromasya with Lochana's signature. Especially since the pada with Jayananda's signature exists in a manuscript two hundred and eight years old[^81] — and until this pada with Lochana Dasa's signature turns up in some older manuscript, we shall take this baromasya to be Jayananda's composition. Jayananda names the established Vaishnava writers who came before him; had he taken Vishnupriya's baromasya from Lochana's pada, he would not have hesitated to name Lochana Dasa candidly. Not only in the one manuscript collected by us, but also in the portion of Jayananda's Chaitanya-mangala called the Vairagya-khanda collected by the Asiatic Society, and in another old manuscript recently found, I have found only Jayananda's signature. For all these reasons I have described the quoted baromasya of Vishnupriya as Jayananda's composition.

*[p. 227]*[^128]

After the above essay had been written, three manuscripts of Lochana Dasa's Chaitanya-mangala came into our hands. In all three manuscripts Vrindavana Dasa is mentioned:

> "With all my heart I will praise Vrindavana Dasa,
> whose song, the Bhagavata, has charmed the world." (Adi-khanda)

From these lines of Lochana Dasa it is clearly seen that even before his Chaitanya-mangala was composed, Vrindavana Dasa's book had already been circulated in Vaishnava society under the name Chaitanya-bhagavata. What, then, is the root of the tradition, current in the Vaishnava community, of a dispute over the titles of the books of Vrindavana and Lochana? In the works of Krishnadasa Kaviraja and Narottama there is mention of Vrindavana Dasa's Chaitanya-mangala. From this it must certainly be admitted that Vrindavana Dasa composed a book called the Chaitanya-mangala. That this Chaitanya-mangala afterwards became famous under the name Chaitanya-bhagavata — this is heard only as a tradition, but in no authoritative work have we found anything about it. Some may say that when Vrindavana Dasa's Chaitanya-mangala was sent to Vrindavana, it had not yet been named the Chaitanya-bhagavata. But that a book named the Chaitanya-bhagavata also went to Vrindavana is known from the Sita-charitra of Lokanatha Gosvami, a resident of Vrindavana. Krishnadasa Kaviraja and Lokanatha both lived together in Vrindavana. Why, then, did one of them use the title Chaitanya-mangala and the other the title Chaitanya-bhagavata? The name "Bhagavata" is very dear to the Vaishnavas. Achyuta Babu writes that the title Chaitanya-bhagavata was given in 1492 Shaka. But according to him Krishnadasa Kaviraja composed the Chaitanya-charitamrita in 1503 Shaka. Sri Dinesh Chandra Sen writes: "Krishnadasa Kaviraja passed away in 1582 AD (1504 Shaka)."[^129] So someone may say that Krishnadasa completed his book, or left this world, before the Chaitanya-bhagavata came to Vrindavana. But, as we have already said, Krishnadasa's companion Lokanatha Gosvami mentions the Chaitanya-bhagavata. Two months before Chaitanya took sannyasa (1432 Shaka), Lokanatha came to Vrindavana at his command to recover its lost tirthas. At that time Lokanatha's age cannot have been less than seventeen or eighteen years, for Chaitanya would not have sent a mere boy to recover tirthas. *[p. 228]* That being so, Lokanatha's birth must be placed roughly at about 1414 or 1415 Shaka. Lokanatha mentions the Chaitanya-charitamrita in his Sita-charitra. Even if he wrote his book immediately after the Charitamrita was finished, he would then have been about ninety years old. Apart from Krishnadasa Kaviraja we have not seen anyone (in Vaishnava literature) composing a book at so advanced an age. It seems to us that Lokanatha must have composed the Sita-charitra at the very time when Krishnadasa was composing the Charitamrita. Considering his old age, it does not seem likely that he brought out his book after the Charitamrita was completed. He saw the Chaitanya-bhagavata in Vrindavana, and yet his younger companion Krishnadasa Kaviraja had not heard the name Chaitanya-bhagavata — a wonder indeed! It is for this reason that we say we have grave doubts whether the tradition about the naming of the Chaitanya-bhagavata is true.

Achyuta Babu has written in a letter that because Vrindavana Dasa did not mention the name of Narahari Thakura, Lochana Dasa too at first did not use Nityananda's name in his book. Later, when the dispute was settled, he recognised his fault and used Nityananda's name. Immediately after this settlement of the dispute [Vrindavana Dasa] must have adopted the title Chaitanya-bhagavata. But because before this the Chaitanya-bhagavata had been signed by the Gosvamis of Vrindavana, Vrindavana Dasa, even after (later) recognising his error, could not put Narahari Thakura's name into his book. The tradition on which he relied in writing these words does not appear true to our small understanding. In Lochana's book Nityananda's name is mentioned hundreds of times, and considering the devotion Lochana shows to Nityananda, it can in no way be admitted that the word "Nityananda" is a later insertion. Such an insertion of a name would amount to writing a separate book. But there is no evidence that Lochana Dasa wrote his book twice. Therefore it seems to us that Lochana Dasa brought out his Chaitanya-mangala long after Vrindavana Dasa's Chaitanya-bhagavata had become honoured in Vaishnava society. How long afterwards Lochana Dasa's book was brought out is what must now be considered. Achyuta Babu writes: "Lochana Dasa, in his fourteenth year, in 1459 Shaka, brought out the Chaitanya-mangala at the command of Narahari Thakura." But the respected Sri Haradhana Datta Bhaktinidhi Mahashaya does not accept this view; he believes that Lochana's Chaitanya-mangala was composed many years after Mahaprabhu's disappearance. In support of his view he quotes this pada of Lochana's guru, Narahari Thakura:

*[p. 229]*

> "Seeing Gaura's lila, a great desire arose in my mind
> to write something in the vernacular and keep it.
> I am most lowly; I do not know how to write in order —
> how shall I write it?
>
> The lila of Gaura and Gadadhara [even] Shiva honours;
> who has the power to describe it,
> even if Sharada were to write without cease, for ever,
> and five-faced Sadashiva too?
>
> He who will write the book is not yet born;
> it will be long before he is born, O Lord.
> If it is composed in the vernacular, all people will understand —
> when will the Lord fulfil [my] desire?
>
> I write a few padas — if someone sees them
> and reveals the Lord's lila,
> Narahari will be happy, the sorrow of his heart will go;
> at the singing of the book stones will melt." (Pada-samudra)

Bhaktinidhi Mahashaya says that when this pada was composed Lochana had not been born. Later, taking his guru's command upon his head, he brought out the Chaitanya-mangala many years afterwards.

It now seems to us too that Lochana's Chaitanya-mangala was brought out many years after Chaitanya's disappearance. Jayananda composed the Chaitanya-mangala shortly after 1480 Shaka. At that time Lochana's Chaitanya-mangala had not yet been composed. That is why Jayananda does not mention even the name of Lochana or of his book. According to some, Narahari Thakura passed away in 1463 Shaka. Before that, Lochana Dasa had been commanded by his guru to bring out a life of Chaitanya. But there is no evidence that his book was brought out during his guru's lifetime. From the mention of the name "Chaitanya-bhagavata" in his book, and from Jayananda's silence about him, Lochana Dasa's book now appears to be a composition of a later time. Although Jayananda's book is in many respects superior to Lochana Dasa's in historical reliability, its poetry has paled beside Lochana's rasa-kindling descriptions. To the poetry-loving Bengali, Jayananda's history did not seem so valuable, and so Jayananda's achievement lay buried somewhere beneath Lochana Dasa's melodious compositions full of flowing nectar. Just as, when Madhavacharya's Sri Krishna-mangala appeared, the esteem for Gunaraja Khan's Sri Krishna-vijaya declined, *[p. 230]* so with the rise of Lochana Dasa the lustre of Jayananda's fame was clouded over. In particular, Jayananda, by making known the account of Mahaprabhu's bodily death, became unwelcome to devout Vaishnava society, whereas Lochana Dasa, by writing what was after the Vaishnavas' hearts, easily won the hearts of the devotees. The devout Vaishnavas were so enchanted by the rasa of Lochana Dasa's poetry that they did not hesitate to publish some sweet padas composed by others under Lochana's name. Probably it is for this very reason that in the present printed edition of the Padakalpataru the "Baromasya of Vishnupriya" composed by Jayananda has passed under Lochana Dasa's name.

    "From Jitamishra, his sons here: Kamesha, Ramesha, Ramesha,[^58] Madhava; the first three had no son; Madhava obtained a son, Kandarpaka. To him was born a son named Aniruddha; from him a son renowned as Vishvapati. From him came offspring named Bharga; his renowned son was Karttika. From him was born Sri Darpahari, and he obtained a son, Shivarama. Of Shiva, in turn, Ramapati is known; from him the brahmana named Jagannatha. The sister of the householder named Vishnudasa, […], named Shachi, Sri herself in beauty, Jagannatha took […].[^59] Then from Shachi's womb two were born, treasuries of fame, greatly awakening devotion; the elder, of unprecedented and peerless beauty, bore the name Sri Vishvarupa. In childhood, leaving everything, devoted to Vishnu, deeming sannyasa the highest, he went to the forest like Dhruva. And Shachi, though troubled at heart, stayed at home in the hope of Chaitanya; having no other refuge, she, knowing what is right, […]. For the spreading of Hari-nama, for the work of destroying Kali, on the full-moon day in the Shaka year [reckoned as] 'ocean–sky–Vedas–moon,'[^60] in Phalguna, under the conjunction with Phalguni, during an eclipse, at nightfall, from the milk-ocean of Sri Shachi's womb Gaurachandra rose. This Gaurachandra, lord of the gopis, dissolving impurity, the very form of death to Kali, the essence in the essenceless world, the queller of Smara's arrows, the excellent disciple of a sannyasi. Thus, as if by cunning, the cunning one, crest-jewel of the cunning, his form tinged with the colours of detachment, in the Shaka year 'moon–fire–Vedas–moon'[^60] left home and went to the forest."

    Of the two lists given above, apart from the name of Jagannatha Mishra not one of the preceding names agrees. In such a case it is a hard problem to determine which is true and which false. It would be well if the revered Gosvamis gave a decision on this. But let me say this much: just as among the Rarhi and Varendra brahmanas the practice of writing down genealogies has been current for a long time, so among the Pashchatya Vaidikas too the samajdars[^61] have long been writing down genealogies and preserving them with great care. That being so, the genealogy preserved in the old kulapanjika of the Pashchatya Vaidikas is not a thing to be altogether disregarded.
